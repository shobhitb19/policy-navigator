# Place: Auckland / Tāmaki Makaurau

## Context

Auckland (Tāmaki Makaurau) is New Zealand's largest city and dominant economic hub, with a population of approximately 1.72 million in 2020 (expected to reach 2 million in the early 2030s per Statistics New Zealand, 2021). It sits on a narrow isthmus between two harbours, constraining spatial expansion and making transport network design difficult. The city accounts for 37.9% of New Zealand's GDP and 35.5% of its workforce — materially exceeding its ~33% population share — implying a per-worker productivity premium of approximately 11.1% relative to the rest of New Zealand (Infometrics, 2021, cited in Gluckman et al., 2022).

Auckland operates under a unified local government (Auckland Council) established in 2010 through amalgamation of eight separate councils. The "Supercity" model was intended to improve strategic coordination but has produced incomplete alignment between central government (Wellington-based) and local decision-makers. Key long-term infrastructure decisions — port location, second harbour crossing, light rail corridor — remained unresolved as of 2022 despite decades on the agenda.

## Key constraints

- Isthmus geography limits east-west connectivity and constrains the roading and transit network.
- Structural car-dependency: most Aucklanders feel they need a car because public transit coverage is thin, especially in South and West Auckland.
- Auckland's PT fares are among the highest globally (ranked third highest in 2018), making transit economically inaccessible for lower-income residents.
- Economic cost of road congestion estimated at ~NZ$1.3 billion per year (Leung et al., 2017).
- Central-local governance fragmentation: central government controls most fiscal and strategic levers but lacks integrated planning with Auckland Council and its Council-Controlled Organisations (CCOs: Auckland Transport, Eke Panuku, Watercare, Auckland Unlimited).
- Housing affordability has deteriorated significantly since the early 2000s; Auckland was ranked lowest of all New Zealand cities on quality of life in the 2018 Nielsen survey.
- Māori and Pasifika communities are disproportionately concentrated in South and West Auckland, where transport coverage and economic opportunity are weakest.

## Priority levers

- [Zoning and FAR](../levers/zoning-and-far.md) — Medium Density Residential Standards (2022) and National Policy Statement on Urban Development
- [Transport investment](../levers/transport-investment.md) — City Rail Link, rapid transit network expansion, PT affordability reform
- [Metropolitan governance](../levers/metropolitan-governance.md) — integrated planning unit, Commission for Future Generations, fiscal devolution
- [Integrated planning](../levers/land-use-planning.md) — unified strategy and data infrastructure shared across central and local agencies

## Open questions

- To what extent does the Auckland productivity premium reflect genuine agglomeration effects vs. compositional sorting? What does this imply for the returns to densification?
- How should infrastructure investment be sequenced relative to governance reform to maximise Auckland's economic potential?
- What fiscal tools (local sales tax, GST share, development levies) could give Auckland Council the revenue base for long-term infrastructure investment?
- How can deliberative and participatory democratic mechanisms (citizens' juries, rangatahi advisory groups) improve legitimacy of long-term planning decisions?
