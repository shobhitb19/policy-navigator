# claim-0102

At country level, the intensity of reading skills use at work correlates strongly with GDP per hour worked, and this relationship persists after adjusting for literacy proficiency — evidence that the deployment of skills in production, not merely their possession, drives national labour productivity.

**Source:** [OECD (2016) — Skills Matter](../sources/source-0022.md) — [Chapter 4: How skills are used in the workplace](../sources/source-0022.md#section-4-skills-use)

> "The use of reading skills at work correlates strongly with output per hour worked... One possible explanation for this is that using skills simply reflects workers' proficiency in those skills. In other words, they both represent the human capital available to the firm. If so, the link between the use of reading skills at work and productivity could actually reflect a relationship between literacy proficiency and productivity. [But] the link persists after adjustment." (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Chapter 4)

**Qualifiers:**
- This is a cross-country correlation, not a causal estimate; reverse causality (more productive economies create more skill-intensive jobs) cannot be ruled out.
- The correlation holds for reading and writing skills use; it is weaker for numeracy and problem-solving at work.
- Country-level correlations aggregate over within-country variation in skills use by industry, firm, and occupation.
- The finding is consistent with firm-level evidence that higher skills use is associated with higher productivity and lower staff turnover.
- This relationship is directly relevant to the NZ productivity puzzle: if skills are not being fully deployed at work (due to mismatch, job design, or technology gaps), the economy underperforms even if the workforce is well-educated.
