# claim-0106

New Zealand's housing supply elasticity declined by roughly one-quarter to one-third between the mid-century period (1938–1977) and recent decades (1979–2018), meaning a given increase in housing demand now produces one-quarter to one-third fewer new homes than it did in the mid-20th century.

**Source:** [Te Waihanga (2022)](../sources/source-0023.md) — [Benchmarking against our past](../sources/source-0023.md#benchmarking-against-our-past)

> "Our results suggest that the New Zealand-wide housing supply elasticity has declined by one-quarter to one-third between the mid-century period and recent decades. In other words, when demand for housing increases, we build one-quarter to one-third fewer new homes than our grandparents did." (Nunns & Dodge, 2022, p.13)

**Qualifiers:**
- Elasticity estimates are derived from a reduced-form econometric model; standard errors are large, making precise estimates uncertain (see Table 6, p.34).
- Estimate is robust across multiple assumptions about housing demand elasticities (Table 7, p.35) but all assumptions yield a decline in the same direction.
- The decline in unit count is partly offset by larger average dwelling sizes; floor-area supply declined less sharply than dwelling count supply.
- Data period ends in 2018; construction activity increased substantially post-2018 in response to planning reforms.
