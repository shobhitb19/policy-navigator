# claim-0104

More than 25% of New Zealand adults are foreign-born — one of the three highest shares in the OECD survey (alongside Australia and Canada) — creating structural pressure toward qualification mismatch because immigrant credentials are frequently not fully recognised in the domestic labour market, leading qualified migrants to accept roles below their attainment level.

**Source:** [OECD (2016) — Skills Matter](../sources/source-0022.md) — [Chapter 3: Socio-demographic distribution](../sources/source-0022.md#section-3-demographics)

> "More than 25% of adults in [Australia, Canada] and New Zealand" are foreign-born. (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Chapter 3)

**Qualifiers:**
- On average across the 28-country PIAAC sample, approximately 12% of adults are foreign-born; NZ's >25% is approximately double the average.
- Foreign-born adults scored on average 24 points lower in literacy than native-born adults across the OECD (approximately half a standard deviation), reflecting differences in language proficiency, educational system comparability, and credential transferability.
- Qualification non-recognition is a policy-specific failure, not an inherent consequence of immigration: NZ's immigration policy selects on qualifications, but credential recognition systems do not always translate those qualifications into equivalent job access.
- This mechanism (high immigration + credential non-recognition = structural overqualification) is the primary driver of NZ's above-average mismatch rates (see claim-0105).
- See also claim-0093 (Koi Tū 2026): underutilisation of Māori, Pacific, and migrant workers compounds NZ's structural labour shortage.
