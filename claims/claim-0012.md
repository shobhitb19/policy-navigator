# claim-0012

Estimated agglomeration elasticities in production rose from 1980 to approximately 2000 and have since declined; by approximately 2020 they were roughly 1.5% below their peak, suggesting the productive advantages of cities are not stable over time.

**Source:** [Donovan et al. (2021)](../sources/source-0004.md) — [section 4.2.3](../sources/source-0004.md#section-4-2-3), [section 5](../sources/source-0004.md#section-5)

> "Considering the year of data trend... there is a small positive effect around the end of the 1990s and the start of the 2000s, which subsequently turns negative from the mid-2000s onwards. By 2020, estimates are approximately 1.5% smaller than they were two decades prior." (Donovan et al., 2021, section 4.2.3, p. 24)

**Evidence base:** [evidence-0001](../evidence/evidence-0001.md)

**Qualifiers:**
- The authors emphasise this is a statistical association in the meta-data, not direct evidence of a causal mechanism; speculation about causes is explicitly flagged as such.
- The year-of-publication trend is less precisely estimated than the year-of-data trend, likely due to difficulty separating it from study-level effects.
- Proposed explanations — congestion costs from sustained urban growth, initial concentration then diffusion of ICT, declining freight costs, stricter environmental regulations — are speculative.
- The trend in non-manufacturing activities (services) rose from 1980–2000 then began to fall, while manufacturing fell for the entire six decades.

**Related:** Qualifies: [claim-0001](claim-0001.md)
