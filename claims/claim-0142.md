# claim-0142

Strong migration inflows may restrict wage growth in some sectors, making jobs less attractive to local workers, which in turn encourages or reinforces some firms and industries to rely on low-cost, low-skill migrant labour rather than raising wages or investing in productivity-enhancing technology — a potential low-skill, low-wage labour market trap.

**Source:** [NZ Productivity Commission (2022)](../sources/source-0027.md) — [Chapter 3, Box 3.5: A low-skill, low-wage labour market trap](../sources/source-0027.md#chapter-3-low-skill-low-wage-trap)

> "Strong migration inflows may potentially restrict wage growth, making some jobs less attractive to local workers, which in turn encourages or reinforces some firms and industries to rely on low-cost (and low-skill) labour... In New Zealand, strong employment growth has previously kept pace with strong immigration inflows, but many immigrants are low paid and working in unskilled occupations in lower-productivity industries." (NZPC, 2022, Part 3, Box 3.5, p.35-36)

**Qualifiers:**
- The Commission frames this as a contested, theoretical mechanism rather than a proven outcome for New Zealand specifically — it cites UK evidence (Wilson & Hogarth, 2003) and a Birmingham case study (Anderson et al., 2006) as comparator evidence of a low-skills equilibrium occurring elsewhere.
- The mechanism depends on both firm and worker expected-returns calculations; the Commission notes "what to do about this chronic underinvestment in training is less clear," and policy options range from limiting low-wage migrant labour access to incentivising employer-led training.
- The balance of cited evidence suggests policy should focus on encouraging employers (not employees) to increase training (Brunello & De Paola, 2004; Brunello & Wruuck, 2019).
