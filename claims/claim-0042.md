# claim-0042

Between 1990 and 2015 every income group's cities expanded built-up area faster than population grew. But the mode of expansion differed sharply: low- and lower-middle-income cities grew almost entirely through horizontal spread; high-income cities added substantial infill and vertical layering alongside horizontal expansion, producing denser, taller central districts.

**Source:** [Lall et al. (2021)](../sources/source-0009.md) — [Chapters 1–2: Horizontal vs infill/vertical expansion by income group](../sources/source-0009.md#chapter-1-2-urban-form)

> "While pyramidal expansion flows from economic transformation, only agglomeration economies, based on specialization and tradables production, can be counted on to set a city's productivity and incomes on an upward path. And only a city that is economically on the rise will generate increasing economic demand for floor space." (Lall et al., 2021, p. 73)

**Qualifiers:**
- The cross-city analysis covers 9,500 cities using GHS-UCDB and WSF3D satellite data; the "mode of expansion" finding is an empirical regularity, not a deterministic law.
- Some high-income cities (notably US Sun Belt cities: Dallas, Houston) remain predominantly horizontal even with high incomes.
- The comparison period (1990–2015) predates mass adoption of remote work; post-2020 patterns may diverge.
