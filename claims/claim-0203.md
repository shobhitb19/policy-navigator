# claim-0203

Knowledge spillovers are geographically bounded because the most economically valuable knowledge is tacit — embodied in people and practices rather than codified in documents — and tacit knowledge transfers most effectively through face-to-face interaction, which requires proximity.

**Sources:** [Audretsch & Feldman (2004)](../sources/source-0034.md) — [Section 2: Why knowledge spillovers are geographically bounded](../sources/source-0034.md#section-2-knowledge-spillovers-geography); [Section 4: Tacit knowledge and proximity](../sources/source-0034.md#section-4-tacit-knowledge)

> "Knowledge spillovers tend to be geographically bounded because tacit knowledge — unlike codified information — requires face-to-face interaction for effective transmission." (Audretsch & Feldman, 2004, p. 2718)

**Qualifiers:**
- This is a theoretical mechanism supported by empirical clustering evidence (see claim-0204) rather than a directly measured causal effect of tacit knowledge flows.
- The distinction between tacit and codified knowledge is conceptually important but empirically difficult to operationalise; the patent citation approach (Jaffe et al. 1993) measures only a downstream product of knowledge spillovers, not the spillovers themselves.
- Digital communication technologies may have partially weakened the link between proximity and tacit knowledge transfer, though the empirical evidence on whether localisation has declined (Li 2014, claim-0205) does not support a strong technology-has-dissolved-geography conclusion.
- The Thomas Allen (1977) finding — that communication frequency between engineers drops exponentially beyond 30 metres — is a direct empirical observation of proximity effects on knowledge flows at the organisational level.
