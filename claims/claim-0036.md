# claim-0036

Atlanta (density: 6 people/hectare) and Barcelona (density: 176 people/hectare) had similar populations (~2.5–2.8 million) in 1990 but vastly different transport outcomes: per capita CO₂ from transport was 400 metric tons in Atlanta and 38 tons in Barcelona; 30% of Barcelona trips were by mass transit versus 4.5% in Atlanta.

**Source:** [World Bank (2009)](../sources/source-0008.md) — [Box 7.6: Atlanta vs Barcelona](../sources/source-0008.md#box-7-6-atlanta-barcelona)

> "Atlanta had a density of six people per hectare in 1990, and Barcelona had 176... Per capita CO₂ emission was 400 metric tons in Atlanta, 38 tons in Barcelona... Only 4.5% of trips are by mass transit in Atlanta, a fraction of the 30% in Barcelona." (World Bank, 2009, Chapter 7, Box 7.6, p. 211; citing Bertaud, 2004; Kenworthy, 2005)

**Qualifiers:**
- The CO₂ figures are "per capita" transport emissions for 1990; the precise measurement boundary (city vs metro area) is not specified in the cited passage, and this affects comparability.
- The density figures are for the broader built-up area, not the administrative city.
- Both the density difference and the transit mode share are partly cause and partly consequence of each other and of historical policy choices; this is a cross-sectional comparison, not a controlled experiment.
- Atlanta's relatively low density reflects US automobile-era suburban development patterns, not solely a failure of planning policy.

**Related:** Supports: [claim-0028](claim-0028.md) (per capita transport CO₂ falls with urban density — OECD cross-city evidence). See also: [claim-0035](claim-0035.md) (leapfrog development destroys connectivity).
