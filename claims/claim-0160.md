# claim-0160

An expansionary immigration shock has no significant effect on headline consumer prices, but its sub-components diverge: non-tradable prices rise significantly around one year after the shock, while tradable prices are likely to fall below baseline.

**Source:** [Javed, Power & Richardson (2026)](../sources/source-0028.md) — [Section 4.1: Baseline IRFs — consumer price levels (headline, tradable, non-tradable)](../sources/source-0028.md#section-4-1-price-levels)

> "Headline consumer price levels show no significant deviation from baseline in response to an expansionary immigration shock. The sub-component analysis of consumer prices shows that non-tradable prices are significantly elevated (at the 68% level) approximately one year after the shock. Tradable prices, by contrast, are likely to be below the baseline, with most of the posterior probability mass lying in negative territory." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.1, p. 14-15)

**Qualifiers:**
- The tradable price result is described by where probability mass lies, not as a separately confirmed statistically significant result at the 68% credible interval.
- Non-tradable prices (e.g., services, housing-related costs) are more exposed to local demand pressure from population growth than tradable prices, which are more exposed to international competition.
