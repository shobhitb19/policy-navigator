# claim-0144

Requiring migrants to work for a single sponsoring employer ("tying") increases employer monopsony power, which can drive down wages and conditions for both migrants and local workers and prevents job-to-job mobility — a recognised route to higher productivity — because workers cannot move to firms where they would be most productive.

**Source:** [NZ Productivity Commission (2022)](../sources/source-0027.md) — [Chapter 4, Findings 16-17 and Recommendation 6: Tying migrants to a single employer enables employer monopsony power](../sources/source-0027.md#chapter-4-employer-tying-monopsony)

> "Finding 17: Enabling job-to-job mobility is one route to improving productivity. When monopsony power is enabled by regulation (such as requiring migrants to stay with a single employer), workers may not be in firms where they can be most productive. Tying migrants to a single employer can lead to negative labour-market outcomes for migrants (including through exploitation) and local workers by increasing the monopsony power of employers." (NZPC, 2022, Part 4, p.56-58)

**Qualifiers:**
- The mechanism is described as well established in the international literature (citing OECD, 2021b on job-to-job mobility and productivity; Norlander, 2021 on guest-worker monopsony).
- Tying was originally intended to reduce displacement risk to local workers by linking work rights to occupations that had passed a Labour Market Test or appeared on a Skill Shortage List — the Commission finds the policy carries this unintended cost rather than judging the original objective illegitimate.
- An alternative (allowing migrants to move between any accredited employer) carries its own risks, including reduced visibility for immigration authorities into where migrants work and potential moral hazard from employer-migrant collusion to facilitate migration without genuine employment intent.
