# claim-0099

Across OECD countries, approximately 22% of workers are overqualified for their jobs, approximately 13% are underqualified, and approximately 40% work in occupations unrelated to their field of study — three distinct but overlapping forms of mismatch that together represent a pervasive feature of developed-economy labour markets.

**Source:** [OECD (2016) — Skills Matter](../sources/source-0022.md) — [Chapter 5: Mismatches of qualifications and skills](../sources/source-0022.md#section-5-mismatch)

> "On average, about 22% of workers reported that they are overqualified (i.e. that they have higher qualifications than required for their jobs)... while about 13% are underqualified for their jobs... some 40% of workers are employed in an occupation that is unrelated to their field of study." (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Chapter 5)

**Qualifiers:**
- Qualification mismatch is measured by self-reported job requirements ("What would be the usual qualifications needed to get this job?") compared with actual attainment. This is a normative rather than statistical approach, and is job-specific rather than occupation-average.
- Skills mismatch (overskilling/underskilling) is measured using the PIAAC proficiency distribution within occupations: a worker is overskilled if their proficiency exceeds the maximum of self-reported well-matched workers in their occupation and country.
- Field-of-study mismatch is based on a mapping of ISCO-08 3-digit occupations to fields of study; some occupations are legitimately matched to multiple fields.
- These three measures partially overlap: overqualified workers are also more likely to be field-of-study mismatched.
- Average rates conceal wide cross-country variation.
