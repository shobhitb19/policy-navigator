# claim-0224

Comprehensively eliminating house price distortions across all New Zealand regions over 2000–16 would have raised total GDP by 4.5–7.7% and output per worker by 0.9–1.4%, and would have halved or eliminated net migration outflows of New Zealanders to Australia, according to spatial equilibrium modelling.

**Source:** [Nunns (2019)](../sources/source-0041.md) — [Section 5: Counterfactual scenarios](../sources/source-0041.md#section-5-counterfactual-scenarios)

> "In the perfect labour mobility case, reducing land price distortions to zero would increase New Zealand's GDP by around 7.7%. However, output per worker would only increase by 0.9%, as the shift of New Zealand workers back across the Tasman would 'arbitrage' away some of the growth in productivity and wages. In the imperfect labour mobility case, the third scenario would increase total output by 4.5% … but output per worker would rise by 1.4%." (Nunns, 2019, pp. 37–38, Table 4)

**Qualifiers:**
- This is the "aspirational" Scenario 3, explicitly framed as an upper bound; the more plausible Scenario 2 (preventing further distortion growth after 2000) delivers roughly two-thirds of these gains (GDP +3.0–5.3%).
- Ranges span the imperfect-mobility (lower GDP, higher per-worker) and perfect-mobility (higher GDP, lower per-worker) specifications — the two move in opposite directions because trans-Tasman return migration dilutes per-worker gains.
- The published journal version (Nunns, 2021, NZEP 55(1)) reports revised estimates — output per worker +1.1–1.6%, labour force +3.3–7.2%, GDP +5.0–8.4% — which are the figures relayed by MHuD (2024) in claim-0075. The working-paper and journal figures describe the same modelling exercise at different revision stages.
- Model assumes full employment; results are robustness-tested to parameter changes in the technical appendix.
