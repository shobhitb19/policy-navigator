# claim-0208

New Zealand cities show "forking paths" in agglomeration dynamics since the 1980s economic reforms: Auckland's agglomeration elasticities have strengthened as it concentrates knowledge-intensive services, while other New Zealand cities — particularly those specialising in manufacturing and primary processing — show weaker or declining agglomeration economies.

**Sources:** [Donovan et al. (2022)](../sources/source-0036.md) — [Section 5: Diverging trajectories](../sources/source-0036.md#section-5-forking-paths)

> "We find evidence of 'forking paths' in New Zealand: Auckland's agglomeration elasticities have increased over time while other cities have seen declining agglomeration effects, reflecting structural change in the composition of economic activity across cities." (Donovan et al., 2022, Section 5, paraphrased)

**Qualifiers:**
- The divergence is identified using time-varying agglomeration elasticities estimated separately across census periods (1976, 1981, 1986, 1991, 1996, 2001, 2006, 2013, 2018).
- Causation is ambiguous: the paper identifies correlation between agglomeration dynamics and structural change (shift to services), but cannot fully isolate whether the agglomeration effect is driving Auckland's composition shift or the other way around.
- "Forking paths" does not necessarily mean regional cities are declining in absolute terms — they may still be growing, just failing to realise the agglomeration benefits they once did when manufacturing was more concentrated.
- The policy implication — that Auckland deserves asymmetric investment — is one possible interpretation, but the paper also supports the view that regional cities need structural adjustment support rather than simply more agglomeration investment.
