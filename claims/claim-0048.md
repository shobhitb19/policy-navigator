# claim-0048

Urban primacy in developing countries is driven by political and institutional factors — particularly undemocratic and unstable regimes — not by trade policy. Trade plays no systematic role in determining primacy. Primate city favouritism operates through many small advantages: easier permit access, subsidised public goods, underpriced utilities, and better infrastructure in the capital. Administrative deregulation is the most tractable tool to reduce it.

**Source:** Ades and Glaeser (1995), Davis and Henderson (2003), Nitsch (2006), Henderson et al. (2001) — reviewed in [Duranton (2008)](../sources/source-0010.md) — [Section 3: Political drivers of urban primacy](../sources/source-0010.md#section-3-primate-city)

> "Political and institutional factors appear to be at the root of the primacy phenomenon. There is strong evidence of a positive association between unstable and undemocratic regimes and urban primacy. [...] The better and more recent studies (Ades and Glaeser, 1995; Nitsch, 2006) suggest that trade plays no systematic role with respect to urban primacy." (Duranton, 2008, pp. 19–20)

**Qualifiers:**
- The exact mechanism within the institutional channel is not fully elucidated.
- Trade liberalisation can have ambiguous effects on primacy depending on whether the primate city is coastal or inland.
- The Korean evidence that administrative deregulation → relocation of mature manufacturing to secondary cities is suggestive but from a single country.
