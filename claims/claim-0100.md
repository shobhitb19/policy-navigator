# claim-0100

Overqualified workers earn approximately 14% less than well-matched workers with the same qualifications and proficiency; however, when compared with colleagues in the same job, overqualified workers earn approximately 4% more — reflecting their higher productivity — so the aggregate wage loss falls on the worker, not the firm, and the economy as a whole underperforms its human capital potential.

**Source:** [OECD (2016) — Skills Matter](../sources/source-0022.md) — [Chapter 5: Mismatches of qualifications and skills](../sources/source-0022.md#section-5-mismatch)

> "On average across countries/economies, overqualified workers earn about 14% less than well-matched workers with the same qualifications and skills proficiency. When compared to workers in the same job, overqualified workers earn 4% higher wages... they are more productive, as indicated by their higher proficiency. But, on aggregate, these workers and the economy would be better off if they were working at their adequate qualifications level." (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Chapter 5)

**Qualifiers:**
- The 14% wage penalty is relative to a well-matched worker with the same qualifications and skills proficiency — i.e. it cannot be explained by lower skills.
- The +4% premium within the same job suggests firms partially capture the productivity benefit of overqualified workers.
- Field-of-study mismatch wage effects are "small and often not statistically significant" compared with qualification mismatch.
- The wage penalty is largest where overqualification is most severe (Turkey, United States: ≥20% penalty); smallest in some European countries with compressed wage structures.
- A wage penalty greater than 20% is found in Turkey and the United States.
