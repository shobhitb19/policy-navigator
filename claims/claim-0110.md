# claim-0110

AMM model counterfactual analysis shows Auckland house prices would have risen only ~80% over 1978–2018 (vs 251% observed) had both the 1970s downzoning and the post-1990s travel speed decline been avoided — a ~70% reduction in price inflation. Either intervention alone would have reduced price growth by ~40–42%.

**Source:** [Te Waihanga (2022)](../sources/source-0023.md) — [Explaining these patterns](../sources/source-0023.md#explaining-these-patterns)

> "If we had not downzoned central Auckland in the 1970s, or if we had chosen to adopt successful congestion-mitigation policies, then housing would now be more abundant and house prices would be lower." (Nunns & Dodge, 2022, p.5)
>
> "In this counterfactual world, Auckland housing prices would be roughly half as high as they were in 2018." (p.25, fn 19)

| Scenario | House price growth 1978–2018 | Reduction vs observed |
|---|---|---|
| Observed data | 262% (modelled) / 251% (observed) | — |
| Avoid downzoning only | 156% | −41% |
| Avoid speed decline only | 153% | −42% |
| Avoid both | 80% | −69% |

**Qualifiers:**
- Results are from a calibrated simulation model (AMM), not a natural experiment; they depend on modelling assumptions about Cobb-Douglas utility and housing production functions.
- The model omits employment decentralisation and natural amenities (beaches, parks), both of which affected Auckland's spatial development over this period.
- Counterfactual 2 (avoiding speed decline) assumes congestion pricing could have maintained peak travel speeds; this is consistent with experience in Singapore and Stockholm but has not been tested in NZ.
- The model under-predicts urban expansion somewhat in both periods, likely because employment decentralisation is not captured.
