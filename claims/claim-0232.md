# claim-0232

As early as 2000, the New Zealand Treasury assessed New Zealand's agglomerating forces as weak and posed the resulting strategic question directly: whether New Zealand can maintain a critical mass of economic activity or whether activity will agglomerate offshore, with movement of people and firms to Sydney the canonical case.

**Source:** [Box (2000)](../sources/source-0044.md) — [New Zealand in the world](../sources/source-0044.md#nz-in-the-world)

> "[The paper] notes the weakness of agglomerating forces in New Zealand. The balance that will emerge between agglomeration and dispersion forces is unclear but the discussion raises a big question for New Zealand – can New Zealand maintain a critical mass of activity in the future or will activity agglomerate offshore?" (Box, 2000, p. 4)

> "Is movement of people and firms to Sydney efficient? What does this mean for policy? In particular, whose welfare are we trying to maximise – those left behind in New Zealand or all New Zealanders regardless of their location?" (Box, 2000, pp. 4–5)

**Qualifiers:**
- A conceptual assessment, not an empirical estimate; its significance is historical — the trans-Tasman spatial-equilibrium framing predates the current debate by a quarter-century.
- Subsequent modelling substantiated the concern: Nunns (2019) finds most of the economic cost of New Zealand's housing supply constraints operates through net migration of workers to Australia (claim-0225).
- The welfare question Box poses (residents vs all New Zealanders regardless of location) remains unresolved in New Zealand policy.

**Related:**
- [claim-0225](claim-0225.md) — trans-Tasman migration is the dominant channel of housing-constraint costs (Nunns 2019)
- [claim-0216](claim-0216.md) — small market and distance create high fixed costs (NZPC 2021)
- [claim-0212](claim-0212.md) — no technology diffusion from the international frontier to NZ non-frontier firms
