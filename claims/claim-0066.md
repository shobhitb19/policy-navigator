# claim-0066

Restrictive land use in Auckland has contributed to rising house prices and is estimated to have reduced New Zealand's national GDP by 0.9–1.8% by preventing workers from living where their productivity would be highest.

**Sources:** [Nunns (2019)](../sources/source-0041.md) — [Section 5: Counterfactual scenarios](../sources/source-0041.md#section-5-counterfactual-scenarios) (primary); [Auckland Council Chief Economist (2025)](../sources/source-0015.md) — [Warning lights](../sources/source-0015.md#warning-lights) (relay)

> "The first scenario, which would reduce the growth in house prices in Auckland but not in other regions … Increase in total output relative to observed outcome: 1.8% [perfect labour mobility] / 0.9% [imperfect labour mobility]." (Nunns, 2019, pp. 37–38, Table 4, Scenario 1)

> "Research finds that restrictive land use in Auckland has contributed to rising house prices and reduced national GDP by 0.9–1.8% by limiting people's ability to live in Auckland where their productivity would be higher." (Blick & Stewart, 2025, p. 2, citing Nunns 2019)

**Qualifiers:**
- The 0.9–1.8% range is Nunns (2019) Scenario 1 (halving the growth of Auckland land price distortions over 2000–16); the bounds are the imperfect- and perfect-labour-mobility model specifications. The mechanism is spatial misallocation, operating substantially through trans-Tasman migration (see claim-0225).
- The estimate predates the Auckland Unitary Plan reforms, which have since enabled significant housing supply increases.
- If house-price-to-income ratios had stayed at 2000 levels, today's median price would be ~$680,000 rather than ~$1 million (Blick & Stewart, 2025).
