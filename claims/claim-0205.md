# claim-0205

The geographic localisation of knowledge spillovers — as measured by patent citation patterns across 39 countries and 319 US MSAs — has not declined over the period 1975–2002, rejecting the hypothesis that globalisation and improved communications have made knowledge flows less geographically bounded ("dying over time").

**Sources:** [Li (2014)](../sources/source-0035.md) — [Section 3: Methodology](../sources/source-0035.md#section-3-methodology); [Section 4: International results](../sources/source-0035.md#section-4-international-results); [Section 5: US MSA results](../sources/source-0035.md#section-5-us-results)

> "I find no evidence that knowledge spillovers are 'dying over time': the border effect on patent citations has not diminished over the 1975–2002 period. If anything, the tendency of citations to remain within national borders has strengthened." (Li, 2014, pp. 153–154, paraphrased from abstract and Section 4)

**Qualifiers:**
- The "dying over time" test is a time-series test (does localisation shrink across cohorts?) while "dying with age" is a cross-section test (does a given knowledge vintage become less localised as it ages?). Li finds neither effect robustly.
- The period covered (1975–2002) predates the full development of internet-mediated collaboration; effects post-2002, when digital collaboration tools became ubiquitous, may differ.
- The finding that localisation has not declined does not mean localisation is very strong in absolute terms — it means the degree of localisation is stable, whatever level it is at.
- Patent citations overrepresent technology-intensive industries; service-sector and tacit-knowledge spillovers are not captured.
