# claim-0111

New road capacity tends to induce additional driving, resulting in few sustained benefits for average urban travel speed; reversing the decline in travel speeds through road investment alone is not feasible, and congestion pricing combined with deployment of new transport options is identified as the more effective approach.

**Source:** [Te Waihanga (2022)](../sources/source-0023.md) — [Cut to the chase](../sources/source-0023.md#cut-to-the-chase)

> "It will be difficult to reverse the decline in travel speeds through investment, as new road capacity tends to 'induce' additional driving, resulting in few sustained benefits for travel speed." (Nunns & Dodge, 2022, p.5)
>
> "In Auckland, a large share of the initial gains in average travel speeds have been consumed by rising congestion even though we have increased road capacity." (p.27)

**Qualifiers:**
- The induced-demand mechanism is well-established in the literature (Duranton and Turner 2012 is cited); the Te Waihanga paper treats it as established rather than providing new empirical estimates.
- Auckland's 2021–2031 transport investment ($31 billion) is forecast to increase congestion delays by 10%, consistent with the induced-demand hypothesis.
- Congestion pricing has demonstrated sustained speed benefits in Singapore and Stockholm, but Auckland had not implemented it at the time of publication.
- Applies most strongly in already-congested urban contexts; less binding in peripheral corridors where spare capacity exists.
