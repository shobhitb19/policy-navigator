# claim-0041

Doubling a city's per-capita income (population held constant) raises floor space per person by approximately 29%; doubling population (income held constant) reduces floor space per person by approximately 40%. Income growth — not population growth — is the primary driver of vertical layering and floor space expansion; pyramid-form cities are an economic outcome, not a planning target.

**Source:** [Lall et al. (2021)](../sources/source-0009.md) — [Chapter 2: Floor space demand elasticities](../sources/source-0009.md#chapter-2-floor-space-demand)

> "Cities that are economically on the rise will generate increasing economic demand for floor space — the prerequisite for land developers to invest in multistory construction around business districts and elevate the urban skyline. [...] A city whose population doubles without income growth cannot accommodate newcomers other than by crowding them into existing built-up area." (Lall et al., 2021, Chapter 4, p. 73)

**Qualifiers:**
- The 29%/−40% estimates are derived from the full 3D floor space demand model incorporating building heights (WSF3D data); they are larger in magnitude than the horizontal-only built-up area estimates in Annex 1.
- The cross-city panel covers 9,500 cities worldwide (GHS-UCDB, 1990–2015); estimates reflect average patterns and will vary by institutional context.
- Vertical layering is particularly income-elastic; horizontal expansion responds to both income and population.
- Policy implication: forcing densification through FAR rules alone, without income growth, will not produce pyramidal development.
