# claim-0050

Exclusionary zoning (minimum lot-size requirements and similar regulations) in developing cities raises formal housing costs until squatter settlements become the cheaper alternative for low-income workers. Removing these unnecessary regulations lowers living costs, raises net wages, and eliminates the market incentive for squatter settlement formation. Land titling has independent benefits: formal title is associated with greater female labour supply and investment in housing improvement.

**Source:** Henderson (2007), Field (2007), Di Tella et al. (2007) — reviewed in [Duranton (2008)](../sources/source-0010.md) — [Section 3: Dual housing markets and squatter settlements](../sources/source-0010.md#section-3-dual-housing)

> "Henderson (2007) argues that binding minimum lot-size is responsible for the growth of squatter settlements in Brazilian cities. [...] Removing unnecessary constraints in the formal housing sector is socially desirable since it lowers the cost of living, hence raises the net wage curve and eliminates squatter settlements." (Duranton, 2008, pp. 29–32)

**Qualifiers:**
- Squatter settlements are not simply "low-cost housing" — residents often pay a steep price for substitute public services.
- Titling policy has its own complications: the expectation of future titles can induce preemptive invasions.
- De Soto's (2000) argument that lack of title blocks collateral use is widely cited but the empirical evidence on the collateral channel specifically is disputed.
