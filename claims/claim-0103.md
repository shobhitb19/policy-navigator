# claim-0103

Skills mismatch at the macro-economic level increases unemployment and reduces GDP growth by wasting human capital and suppressing productivity — because workers deployed below their capacity contribute less to firm performance, are less likely to upgrade their skills, and represent a net loss on the economy's investment in education and training.

**Source:** [OECD (2016) — Skills Matter](../sources/source-0022.md) — [Chapter 5: Mismatches of qualifications and skills](../sources/source-0022.md#section-5-mismatch)

> "A mismatch between workers' skills and the demands of their job has potentially significant economic implications. At the individual level, it affects job satisfaction. At the firm level, it increases the rate of turnover and may reduce productivity. At the macro-economic level, it increases unemployment and reduces GDP growth through the waste of human capital and the implied reduction in productivity." (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Chapter 5)

**Qualifiers:**
- The macro-level claim is a structural argument; the document does not provide a direct GDP-level quantification for the OECD as a whole.
- The Australian Productivity Commission (2023) estimated that a revival of technology diffusion could lift Australian GDP by 1.6–4.9% — a related order-of-magnitude for how underutilised human capital (in the form of skills non-deployment) translates to output losses (per Winter et al., 2025, cited in claim-0084).
- The turnover-productivity link at the firm level is well-evidenced; the macro extrapolation is theoretically straightforward but harder to identify empirically.
- Mismatch that leads to overskilling (skills in excess of job requirements) differs from qualification mismatch in its productivity effects: overskilled workers can be more productive than their matched counterparts within the same job, but are still under-utilised relative to their potential.
