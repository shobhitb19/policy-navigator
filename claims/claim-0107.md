# claim-0107

A 1% rise in New Zealand's population caused house prices to increase by ~0.5% in the mid-century period (1938–1977) but by ~2.0% in recent decades (1979–2018) — a fourfold increase in the price impact of population growth, reflecting reduced housing supply responsiveness rather than stronger demand.

**Source:** [Te Waihanga (2022)](../sources/source-0023.md) — [Benchmarking against our past](../sources/source-0023.md#benchmarking-against-our-past)

> "Between the late 1930s and late 1970s, a 1% rise in population caused house prices to increase by roughly 0.5%. Between the late 1970s and late 2010s, a 1% rise in population caused house prices to increase by roughly 2.0%." (Nunns & Dodge, 2022, p.3)

**Qualifiers:**
- Coefficients are estimated with substantial uncertainty (Table 6, p.34); the difference between periods is directionally robust but point estimates carry wide confidence intervals.
- Population growth was faster in the mid-century period, yet prices rose less — ruling out demand acceleration as an explanation and pointing to supply-side deterioration.
- Income growth also had a larger impact on prices in recent decades (~1.2% per 1% income rise vs ~0.9% mid-century).
- The analysis does not separately identify interest rate effects; liberal mortgage credit in both periods complicates the counterfactual.
