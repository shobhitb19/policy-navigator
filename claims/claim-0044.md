# claim-0044

The inverse relationship between urban density and per-capita greenhouse gas emissions holds in high- and upper-middle-income cities but does not hold in low- and lower-middle-income cities. The latter are already far denser than high-income cities, and high density in poor cities reflects overcrowding and poverty rather than low-carbon lifestyles.

**Source:** [Lall et al. (2021)](../sources/source-0009.md) — [Spotlight 3: Urban form and greenhouse gas emissions](../sources/source-0009.md#spotlight-3-ghg-density)

> "While GHG emissions per person decline with urban densities for high- and upper-middle-income countries, the association does not seem to hold for low and lower-middle-income countries. Another insight here is that cities in low- and lower-middle-income countries are already far denser than cities in high- and upper-middle-income countries." (Lall et al., 2021, Spotlight 3, p. 69)

**Qualifiers:**
- The GHG data is from the Global Gridded Model of Carbon Footprints (Moran et al. 2018), which includes consumption-based emissions.
- The non-relationship in low-income cities does not mean density is harmful there — the density-GHG channel operates through car ownership and transport mode share, which are income-dependent.
- Future GHG trajectories in low-income cities depend critically on whether transport infrastructure is car-centric or transit-centric as incomes rise — lock-in is the key risk.
