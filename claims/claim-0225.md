# claim-0225

Most of the estimated economic impact of removing New Zealand's housing supply constraints arises from reduced net migration of New Zealand workers to Australia, rather than from reallocation of labour from low-productivity to high-productivity regions within New Zealand.

**Source:** [Nunns (2019)](../sources/source-0041.md) — [Section 5: The trans-Tasman channel](../sources/source-0041.md#section-5-trans-tasman-channel)

> "A comparison with the above results suggests that most of the estimated economic impacts arise from a reduction in net migration of workers from New Zealand to Australia, rather than reallocation of labour from low-productivity regions to high-productivity regions within New Zealand." (Nunns, 2019, p. 38)

**Qualifiers:**
- A model variant excluding trans-Tasman migration finds the full-reform scenario lifts output by only 0.4–0.8%, versus 4.5–7.7% when the Australia margin is included — the internal-reallocation effect alone is small relative to the retention effect.
- This reframes the cost of housing constraints: the binding margin for New Zealand is not only where workers locate within the country, but whether they remain in the country at all — Australia's cities function as the outside option in New Zealand's spatial equilibrium.
- Model-based finding; depends on the calibrated sensitivity of trans-Tasman migration to real income differentials.
