# claim-0148

The Productivity Commission's central institutional recommendation is to amend the Immigration Act 2009 to require a regularly published Government Policy Statement (GPS) on immigration that specifies a planning range for resident intake and criteria for managing temporary work visas with explicit reference to pressures on New Zealand's absorptive capacity.

**Source:** [NZ Productivity Commission (2022)](../sources/source-0027.md) — [Chapter 5, Recommendation 13: A Government Policy Statement for immigration](../sources/source-0027.md#chapter-5-government-policy-statement)

> "Recommendation 13: The Immigration Act 2009 should be amended to require the Minister, in consultation with the public, to regularly develop and publish an immigration Government Policy Statement (GPS)... how the demand for temporary and residence visas will be managed taking account of significant pressures (if any) on New Zealand's absorptive capacity over the period of the GPS including: specification of a planning range for the intake of new residents over the period covered by the GPS; and the criteria for managing access to temporary work visas and projections of migrant flows based on these criteria, over the period covered by the GPS." (NZPC, 2022, Part 5, Recommendation 13, p.69)

**Qualifiers:**
- The GPS must also specify short- and long-term objectives, performance measures, and how immigration policy will reflect Te Tiriti o Waitangi in partnership with Māori — it is a multi-purpose accountability mechanism, not solely an absorptive-capacity sequencing tool.
- The Commission modelled the proposal partly on two existing NZ public-management tools — the Land Transport GPS and the fiscal responsibility provisions of the Public Finance Act 1989 — ultimately favouring the latter as the better-suited template because immigration policy, unlike land transport, is administered directly by Ministers and officials rather than through an arm's-length Crown agency.
- The Commission illustrates the mechanism's intended effect using a hypothetical planning range of 1-1.5% annual population growth, while cautioning that applying any simple fixed rule across time without understanding underlying causes of population change would be unwise.
