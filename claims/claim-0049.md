# claim-0049

China's hukou migration restrictions kept cities significantly below their productivity-maximising size, causing large income losses. The net wage curve is quasi-flat after its maximum — cities slightly too large incur small welfare losses — but the cost of being constrained below the optimum is substantially larger. This asymmetry implies that anti-mobility policies impose greater welfare harm than overcrowding would.

**Source:** Au and Henderson (2006a,b), reviewed in [Duranton (2008)](../sources/source-0010.md) — [Section 3: Chinese cities undersized](../sources/source-0010.md#section-3-chinese-cities)

> "Au and Henderson (2006a,b) find that Chinese cities tend to be significantly undersized. This results in large income losses. The other finding of Au and Henderson (2006b) is that the net wage curve is quasi-flat after its maximum. This suggests that cities may become grossly oversized under free mobility but that the costs of being oversized are small (unlike the costs of being under-populated)." (Duranton, 2008, p. 18)

**Qualifiers:**
- China's hukou constraints are extreme in international comparison; the costs of mobility restrictions will vary by institutional context.
- The "quasi-flat net wage curve after its maximum" means naive cost-of-living estimates likely understate the costs of anti-mobility policy.
- Au and Henderson exploit natural variation from hukou to identify the productivity-maximising city size.
