# claim-0105

New Zealand ranks among the OECD countries with the highest combined qualification and field-of-study mismatch rates, consistent with its high foreign-born share (>25% of adults) and the structural tendency for immigrants to accept jobs outside their qualifications or field of study when credential recognition is imperfect — making skills mismatch one of NZ's more distinctive labour market features relative to OECD peers.

**Source:** [OECD (2016) — Skills Matter](../sources/source-0022.md) — [Chapter 5: Mismatches of qualifications and skills](../sources/source-0022.md#section-5-mismatch)

Per chart data in the document (Figures 5.2–5.8, which rank countries in descending order of mismatch prevalence), New Zealand appears in the high end of the distribution on both qualification mismatch and field-of-study mismatch. The document's text notes the strong association between high immigration shares and mismatch prevalence, and NZ's position is consistent with this structural relationship.

**Qualifiers:**
- NZ's specific percentage-point mismatch rate is from the document's figures rather than extractable text; the claim of being "among the highest" reflects the chart rankings per the document.
- The mismatch is not solely an immigration effect: NZ's labour market structure, with a large share of employment in occupations accessible via multiple qualification routes, also contributes.
- High mismatch does not necessarily mean low individual welfare — some workers are voluntarily overqualified (accepting lower-skill jobs by choice) — but the macro-level consequences (reduced GDP growth, wasted human capital per claim-0103) apply regardless of the individual's preferences.
- The mismatch rate for NZ is not a permanent feature: credentialing reforms, credential recognition agreements, and better information flows between employers and immigrant workers could reduce it over time.
- Qualification mismatch and overskilling have different wage implications (claim-0100): overqualified workers suffer a wage penalty vs. well-matched workers; overskilled workers may earn slightly more than equally-qualified colleagues in the same job. These effects compound in NZ if high-skilled immigrants accept both lower-qualification and lower-skill jobs.
