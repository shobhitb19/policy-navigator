# claim-0264

Auckland's measured fundamentals are improving while external perceptions stay flat, and the report finds Auckland "tending to win where it has significant levers it controls, and losing where it does not" — with the strongest readings clustered around city-government and locally-led activity, and improvement priorities sitting where national infrastructure and capital dominate.

**Source:** [Committee for Auckland & TBoC (2026)](../sources/source-0048.md) — [Headlines and highlights](../sources/source-0048.md#performance-perception)

> "Fundamentals improving, perceptions flat. Auckland's fundamentals are improving, but perceptions among visitors, experts and investors have remained relatively flat. The result is a widening gap between the city's performance and how it is perceived." (p. 11)
>
> "The power to change. Auckland is tending to win where it has significant levers it controls, and losing where it does not. The strongest readings cluster around city-government and locally-led activity. Improvement priorities sit in territory where national infrastructure and capital dominate." (p. 11)

**Qualifiers:**
- This is the report's own reading across its indicator set, not a tested hypothesis, and "levers it controls" is not operationalised. The pattern is nonetheless legible in the underlying movements: the largest rises are in conventions (+61 places), space tech (+50), climate tech (+36) and construction-cost affordability (+24), while the largest falls are in urban planning effectiveness (−41), mobile internet speeds (−26), governance efficiency (−20) and overall global city standing (−10).
- The urban-planning-effectiveness fall (−41 places) sits awkwardly against the housing-delivery gains (claim-0261). Perception and performance indicators are drawn from different instruments — IMD and IESE survey panels versus completion counts — and the report treats perception as lagging performance.
- The governance implication is the one that carries into spatial policy: a city that performs where it holds authority and under-performs where central government holds it is describing a decision-rights problem, not only a capability problem. The report notes elsewhere that "progress in most focus areas remains highly dependent on central-government alignment" and that "greater fiscal autonomy is a feature observed in some comparable international cities."
- The 2026 report does not restate the ten-pillar decile scores, so this is not directly comparable with the 2025 Prosperity-decile finding (claim-0090).
