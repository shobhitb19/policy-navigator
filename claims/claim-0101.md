# claim-0101

A one standard deviation increase in literacy proficiency (approximately 48 score points on the PIAAC scale) is associated with a 6% increase in hourly wages on average across OECD countries, independently of educational attainment — confirming that skills have a direct economic return beyond the credential signal, and that proficiency and qualifications are separate components of human capital.

**Source:** [OECD (2016) — Skills Matter](../sources/source-0022.md) — [Chapter 5: Outcomes of investment in skills](../sources/source-0022.md#section-5-mismatch)

> "For salaried employees, an increase of one standard deviation in proficiency is associated with a 6% increase in hourly wages, on average across OECD countries/economies that participated in the survey." (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Chapter 5)

**Qualifiers:**
- The 6% figure is an OECD average; the relationship is stronger in England (UK), Singapore, and the United States, and weaker in some countries with compressed wage structures.
- The returns to proficiency are estimated conditioning on years of education, age, gender, work experience, occupation, and field of study — so they are independent of these factors.
- The premium is highest for prime-age workers (30–49) and for men vs. women due to occupation and hours-worked differences.
- Proficiency accounts for approximately 3–6% of total wage variation (vs. 13% for educational attainment), but education and proficiency are mutually reinforcing — education builds proficiency, and higher-proficiency adults are more likely to undertake further education.
- In countries where returns to skills are highest (England/UK, Slovakia), skills proficiency explains up to one-fifth to one-third of the variation in wages.
