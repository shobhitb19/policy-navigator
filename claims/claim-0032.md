# claim-0032

African cities do not generate the same productivity, wage, or employment benefits from city size as cities in Asia and Latin America; once infrastructure quality is controlled for, the city-size effect on African firm productivity becomes statistically insignificant.

**Source:** [Collier, Jones & Spijkerman (2018)](../sources/source-0007.md) — [Section 3: African cities](../sources/source-0007.md#section-3-africa)

> "Once we control for city characteristics, the output elasticity falls and becomes insignificant [for the African sample]... Some city characteristics, like infrastructure, have a large negative effect on firm performance. On average, African firms lose four times the percentage of annual sales as firms in other developing regions due to power outages." (Collier et al., 2018, Section 3, p. 20)

**Qualifiers:**
- "City characteristics" here includes infrastructure quality (power reliability), business environment (regulatory burden on management time), and road density — conflated into a single conclusion.
- The study covers formal-sector firms only; informal-sector dynamics in African cities are not captured.
- "Insignificant" means the city-size premium is not detectable in the data, not that cities provide no benefit — it could reflect measurement error or collinearity with infrastructure proxies.
- The result is an average for African cities; there is heterogeneity within Africa that the study does not fully explore.

**Related:** Qualifies: [claim-0031](claim-0031.md) (the 5–6% elasticity does not hold for Africa). See also: [claim-0035](claim-0035.md) (spatial fragmentation as a structural cause), [claim-0033](claim-0033.md) (low urban wage premium in African countries).
