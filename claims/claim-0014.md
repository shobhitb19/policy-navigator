# claim-0014

More rigorous empirical controls consistently and substantially reduce measured agglomeration elasticities: individual worker effects reduce estimates by approximately 1.1%, housing supply or price controls by approximately 3.8%, and capital intensity controls by approximately 2.4%.

**Source:** [Donovan et al. (2021)](../sources/source-0004.md) — [section 4.1](../sources/source-0004.md#section-4-1), [section 1](../sources/source-0004.md#section-1)

> "We find effects for dependent variables that measure labour productivity (−1.1%); monetary indicators (1.7%)... the use of instrumental variables (−0.3%). We also identify effects for controls, such as sectoral composition (−0.2%), own skills (−0.9%), capital intensity (−2.4%), and individual worker effects (−1.1%). Several controls linked to the urban context, such as levels of human (−0.5%) and social (−0.8%) capital; housing (−3.8%) and wage (−1.2%) effects..." (Donovan et al., 2021, section 4.1, pp. 20–21)

**Evidence base:** [evidence-0001](../evidence/evidence-0001.md)

**Qualifiers:**
- These are parameter estimates from the preferred Bayesian mixed effects model (Model 5); they describe the average reduction in elasticity estimates when a given control is included.
- The effects are cumulative — a model including all these controls simultaneously would produce lower estimates than applying each adjustment individually.
- The large negative effect for housing controls (−3.8%) is notable but estimated from a small share of observations (2.4% of sample); treat with some caution.
- The implication is that studies without these controls — which constitute the majority of the literature — tend to overstate agglomeration economies.
