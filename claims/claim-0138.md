# claim-0138

Because New Zealand's immigration system selects mostly on skill, immigration has more than offset the outward "brain drain" of skilled New Zealanders, producing a net "brain exchange": in 2015-16, 40% of New Zealand's tertiary-educated resident population were immigrants, while only 21% of New Zealand citizens living offshore had a tertiary education.

**Source:** [NZ Productivity Commission (2022)](../sources/source-0027.md) — [Chapter 2, Finding 1: Brain drain offset by skill-selective immigration](../sources/source-0027.md#chapter-2-brain-drain-skills)

> "New Zealand has gained skills and other human capital from migration. Immigrants are more likely to be tertiary educated than New Zealand-born residents and outnumber tertiary-educated emigrants... As a result, immigration 'has more than offset New Zealand's brain drain, resulting in a "brain exchange"' (Carey, 2019, p. 18). Of those living in New Zealand in 2015–16 with high (ie, tertiary) education, 40% were immigrants. By contrast, of all New Zealand citizens living offshore (ie, emigrants), only 21% had a tertiary education. Therefore, net migration accounted for 19% of the tertiary-educated people in New Zealand in 2015–16." (NZPC, 2022, Part 2, p.18-19, citing Carey, 2019, Table 2.1)

**Qualifiers:**
- Figures are drawn from Carey (2019) using 2015-16 data; the comparison is a stock measure (population composition), not a flow measure of annual brain drain/gain.
- This finding describes the historical pattern up to 2015-16 and does not by itself establish that skill-selectivity will continue, especially given the shift toward lower-skilled temporary visa approvals documented since 2012 (see claim-0139).
- The Koi Tū (2026) population strategy report frames the same underlying dynamic more cautiously as "brain exchange" rather than guaranteed net gain, noting that the skill levels of departing NZ citizens are poorly documented.
