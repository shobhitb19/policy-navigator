# claim-0152

Skilled migrants, whether on residence or temporary visas, contribute to New Zealand firm productivity in a similar way to high-skilled New Zealand-born workers, while migrants on "other" visas (student, Recognised Seasonal Employer, working holiday) contribute similarly to low-skilled local workers.

**Source:** [NZ Productivity Commission (2022)](../sources/source-0027.md) — [Overview: Immigration is neither the cause of, nor solution to, New Zealand's productivity woes](../sources/source-0027.md#overview-immigration-productivity)

> "Skilled migrants (both those on residence and temporary visas) have contributed positively to firm productivity, in a similar way to high-skilled New Zealand-born workers... Finding 9: The selection of skills has been effective and resulted in skilled migrants contributing (positively) to firm productivity. Skilled migrants (both those on residence and temporary visas) have contributed to firm productivity in the same way as high-skilled New Zealand-born workers." (NZPC, 2022, Overview p.4; Part 3, Finding 9, p.41)

**Qualifiers:**
- Based on Commission research (Fabling et al., 2022) using IDI and Longitudinal Business Database data on the relationship between migrant labour share and labour productivity across industries, averaged 2005-2019.
- In most industries, the gap between a worker's productivity contribution and their wage is not significant regardless of migrant or local status; where a gap exists, high-skilled New Zealanders tend to have a productivity premium higher than their wage premium, while skilled migrants tend to have a productivity premium lower than their wage premium.
- Migrants are not uniformly distributed across the economy: some low-productivity industries have high migrant labour shares, and some high-productivity industries also rely heavily on migrant labour — the relationship between productivity and migrant labour use is not simple or monotonic.
