# claim-0020

Auckland's governance is characterised by structural fragmentation between central government (which controls most fiscal and strategic levers) and local government (Auckland Council plus multiple CCOs), producing disjointed planning, unresolved long-term decisions, and a dominant electoral-cycle focus that crowds out intergenerational thinking.

**Source:** [Gluckman et al. (2022)](../sources/source-0005.md) — [chapter 3](../sources/source-0005.md#chapter-3), [chapter 4](../sources/source-0005.md#chapter-4)

> "The single most critical enabler for Auckland to progress in a more desirable direction is to ensure alignment of its governance, strategy and planning infrastructures. Without enhancing the coordination of planning and providing clear strategic visioning, Auckland is likely to fail to meet its full potential... There is no obvious structure for coordination across central government agencies or with Auckland, beyond the relatively small Auckland Policy Office." (Gluckman et al., 2022, Chapter 3)

**Qualifiers:**
- The diagnosis of fragmentation is the authors' qualitative assessment based on 120+ stakeholder interviews; it is not a quantitative governance effectiveness study.
- Auckland Council was formed in 2010 through amalgamation of eight councils. The Koi Tū authors argue this did not produce sufficient central-local alignment.
- Specific unresolved decisions cited as evidence of fragmentation include: port location, second harbour crossing, and light rail corridor.
- The 2018 Nielsen Quality of Life Survey found 70% of Aucklanders believe the public has little influence on Council processes.
- Comparable governance fragmentation has been documented in Vancouver and Brisbane; different models in Dublin and Amsterdam are noted as contrasts.
