# claim-0171

A negative employment shock to a regionally specialised manufacturing industry causes net job losses elsewhere in the local economy (a 100-job shock yields a 36-job net employment loss), whereas an identical shock to a widely distributed manufacturing industry is followed by net employment gains elsewhere (a 100-job shock yields a 60-job net employment increase).

**Source:** [Coleman, Maré & Zheng (2019)](../sources/source-0029.md) — [Section 3.1: Bartik shock results — regionally-specialised vs widely-distributed manufacturing](../sources/source-0029.md#section-3-1-bartik-manufacturing)

> "Total employment reduced by 36 jobs if the shock reduced a regionally specialised manufacturing industry, but total employment increased by 60 jobs if the shock hit a widely distributed manufacturing industry" — figures are per 100-job own-sector shock, with cross-sector offset coefficients of −1.48 for widely-distributed manufacturing shocks and −0.60 for regionally-specialised manufacturing shocks. (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 3.1)

**Qualifiers:**
- Uses the Bartik (1991) shift-share shock methodology applied to New Zealand city-industry employment data 1976-2013.
- The mechanism for the divergence is not directly tested in this result; it is consistent with regionally specialised manufacturing being more tied to immobile local resources (e.g. processing of local primary output) than widely distributed manufacturing, which can more easily relocate or be substituted by other local industries.
- Contrasts with the primary sector result in claim-0172, where no compensating expansion occurs at all.
