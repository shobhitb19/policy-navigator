# claim-0259

Auckland's new housing is concentrated close to the city centre and at the urban edge, with comparatively little in the suburbs that public transport already serves — reinforcing car dependence in a city already in the bottom decile globally for residential density, street connectivity and access to mixed-use locations.

**Source:** [Committee for Auckland & TBoC (2026)](../sources/source-0048.md) — [Focus area 5: gaps and headwinds](../sources/source-0048.md#homes-gaps)

> "Connecting new housing. New housing is concentrated close to the city centre and at the urban edge, with comparatively little in those suburbs that public transport already serves. This may reinforce car dependence in a city which is already bottom decile globally for residential density, street connectivity and access to mixed-use locations." (p. 46)
>
> "Gap in walkable options. Auckland sees one of the steepest walkability gaps between central and outer suburbs, in terms of the choice that can be accessed in a 15-minute walk. Benchmarked against other cities, it is also more effective for city centre trips than for suburb-to-suburb journeys. This also limits access to jobs and services for households without a car." (p. 46)

**Qualifiers:**
- TBoC research based on TEMPO, the JRC Global Human Settlement Layer, Foursquare and TravelTime. The bottom-decile placements are against a global city field, not the ten-city peer group.
- The barbell pattern — centre and edge, but not the middle — is the crux. Intensification has been delivered where land economics already favoured it and where greenfield land was available, not where existing transit capacity could absorb it.
- This is a distributional statement about where the units went, not about how many were built. Auckland's aggregate delivery record over the same period is strong (claim-0261). The two findings together say the volume problem has been substantially addressed and the location problem has not.
- Bottom-decile residential density is a metropolitan-scale measure and is consistent with a CBD peak job density near the peer average (claim-0248): Auckland's problem is the density of everywhere that is not the CBD.
