# claim-0047

Urbanisation per se does not affect economic growth. But urban primacy does: a one standard deviation increase in urban primacy (~15 percentage points, from a mean of 31% of the urban population living in the largest city) is associated with approximately 1.5 percentage points lower annual GDP growth. The relationship is non-linear — moderate primacy can be growth-enhancing, excessive primacy is harmful.

**Source:** Henderson (2003), reviewed in [Duranton (2008)](../sources/source-0010.md) — [Section 4: Primacy and economic growth](../sources/source-0010.md#section-4-primacy-growth)

> "Henderson (2003) finds that an increase in urban primacy by one standard deviation (or 15 percent) from the mean (of 31 percent of the urban population living in the largest city) reduces the rate of GDP growth by about 1.5 percent per year. These are large effects." (Duranton, 2008, pp. 38–39; citing Henderson, J. V. 2003. "The urbanization process and economic growth: The so-what question." *Journal of Economic Growth* 8(1):47–71.)

**Qualifiers:**
- Henderson (2003) uses GMM with lagged primacy levels (10–15 year lags) as instruments; identification relies on lagged primacy being otherwise uncorrelated with changes in growth.
- This is the only cross-country study meeting rigorous methods standards per Duranton's review; the conclusion rests on one study.
- Two interpretations are plausible: (1) primacy itself harms growth through congestion; (2) the political economy behind primacy independently suppresses growth everywhere.
- The "urbanisation per se has no growth effect" finding is "rather unsurprising and confirms a broad consensus" (Duranton, 2008, p. 38).
