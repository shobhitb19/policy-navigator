# Lever: Housing Supply Reform

## What this is

Housing supply reform encompasses the regulatory, financial, and institutional changes that actively enable new dwelling construction — particularly in high-demand, high-productivity locations. It includes national policy statements overriding local plan restrictions, medium-density residential standards, consenting process reform, development contribution rules, and infrastructure funding mechanisms.

This lever is distinct from zoning and FAR reform (which sets the density rules) and land use planning (which provides the strategic framework). Housing supply reform focuses on the active *enablement* of supply: removing the barriers that prevent permitted density from being built.

## What problem it addresses

- Housing undersupply in productive cities reduces labour mobility: workers who cannot afford to live near high-productivity firms sort into lower-productivity locations. (See [claim-0066](../claims/claim-0066.md))
- High house prices relative to incomes act as a tax on young workers and firms seeking to locate in productive urban areas.
- The NZ housing stock has reached approximately 4× GDP (up from ~2× in the 1990s), reflecting decades of capitalised scarcity rather than productive investment. (See [claim-0078](../claims/claim-0078.md))
- Restrictive land use is estimated to have reduced NZ national GDP by 0.9–1.8% through spatial misallocation of labour. (See [claim-0066](../claims/claim-0066.md))

## How it works (mechanisms)

- Removing development restrictions increases the feasible supply of dwellings near jobs and services, reducing the price premium of productive locations and enabling better worker–job matching. (See [claim-0075](../claims/claim-0075.md))
- Streamlined consenting reduces the cost and time of building, lowering the threshold at which new supply is profitable.
- Infrastructure funding reform (e.g., development levies, special purpose vehicles, tax increment financing) resolves the coordination failure where developers cannot build because infrastructure doesn't exist, and councils cannot fund infrastructure without certainty of development.
- National policy instruments (NPS-UD, MDRS) that override local restrictive zoning correct the political economy failure where incumbent homeowners capture local planning processes. (See [claim-0066](../claims/claim-0066.md))

## Preconditions

- Infrastructure capacity (water, wastewater, transport) to actually support new development — supply reform without infrastructure investment can produce consented-but-unbuilt sites.
- Functioning construction sector with adequate capacity; when labour and materials are constrained, permissions do not translate to completions.
- Political durability: planning reform frequently provokes reversal by successor governments; cross-party buy-in or statutory entrenchment matters.

## Risks and trade-offs

- Supply-side reform alone does not guarantee affordability in the short run: the time lag between policy change and additional dwellings can be 3–7 years.
- Concentrated supply response in some areas can create infrastructure stress hotspots if phasing is uncoordinated.
- Without complementary inclusionary requirements or social housing provision, reform may increase overall supply without increasing affordable supply for lowest-income households.
- Auckland's housing completion performance (now top-third globally after the 2016 Unitary Plan) demonstrates that reform can work — but the affordability improvement has been slow. (See [claim-0089](../claims/claim-0089.md))

- [claim-0066](../claims/claim-0066.md) Restrictive Auckland land use reduced NZ GDP 0.9–1.8% via spatial misallocation (Nunns 2019)

- [claim-0224](../claims/claim-0224.md) Working-paper version of the same counterfactual: GDP +4.5–7.7%, output per worker +0.9–1.4% (Nunns 2019)

- [claim-0078](../claims/claim-0078.md) NZ housing stock ~$1.6 trillion ≈ 4× GDP — land wealth doubled relative to productive output over three decades
- [claim-0089](../claims/claim-0089.md) Auckland now top-third globally for new homes per capita; exceeds peer average by 60% — post-Unitary Plan reform working
