# Lever: Transport Investment

## What this is

Public investment in transport infrastructure and services, including urban rail, rapid bus transit, road networks, cycling and walking infrastructure, and the integration of these modes into a coherent network.

## What problem it addresses

- Limited job access for workers in peripheral or poorly connected neighbourhoods.
- Spatial poverty traps in underserved communities. (See [claim-0018](../claims/claim-0018.md), [claim-0229](../claims/claim-0229.md))
- High per-capita transport emissions from car-dependent urban forms.
- Fragmented modal networks that reduce the effective catchment area of any single investment.

## How it works (mechanisms)

- Transit investment reduces commute times and expands the number of jobs accessible within a given time budget. (See [claim-0230](../claims/claim-0230.md), [claim-0255](../claims/claim-0255.md))
- Complementary density zoning near transit nodes captures the value of accessibility and reduces car dependence. (See [claim-0028](../claims/claim-0028.md), [claim-0257](../claims/claim-0257.md))
- Investment in underserved areas can begin to break spatial poverty traps by connecting residents to labour markets. (See [claim-0046](../claims/claim-0046.md), [claim-0230](../claims/claim-0230.md))
- Pricing road use directly (time-of-use charging, fleetwide road user charges) can substitute for new capital investment: modelling for Auckland suggests time-of-use charging could cut excess congestion delay by ~35% and deliver equivalent network performance with ~20% less new capital investment. (See [claim-0125](../claims/claim-0125.md))

## Preconditions

- Governance authority to plan and fund at the metropolitan scale — fragmented funding can result in modal and spatial gaps.
- Land use changes (upzoning near stations) to ensure demand is generated and the investment is viable.
- Sequencing: transport investment without complementary land use changes can undershoot demand projections.

## Risks and trade-offs

- Transit investment can increase land values near stations, displacing low-income residents unless accompanied by affordable housing provisions.
- Road capacity investment can induce demand (more vehicle kilometres travelled), offsetting congestion relief over time.
- Large fixed-cost infrastructure has long payback periods and locks in assumptions about spatial patterns that may change.
- Funding allocation can work against stated priorities: the Green Party's response to the National Infrastructure Plan argues NZ's Government Policy Statement on Land Transport 2024–2027 sets aside significantly more funding for new state highway capital expenditure than for renewals and maintenance, which it argues is at odds with the Plan's own recommendations on capital allocation and asset management. (See [claim-0135](../claims/claim-0135.md))
