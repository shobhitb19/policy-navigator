# Lever: Infrastructure Provision

## What this is

Infrastructure provision refers to the deliberate planning, funding, and sequencing of enabling infrastructure — water, wastewater, roading, public transport, energy, telecommunications, and social infrastructure (schools, health facilities) — in advance of or alongside urban growth and development. The key insight is that infrastructure timing relative to settlement patterns determines whether development generates agglomeration benefits or sprawl costs.

This lever is distinct from transport investment (which focuses on modal choice and accessibility outcomes) and covers the broader enabling infrastructure that conditions where and how cities can grow.

## What problem it addresses

- Infrastructure built reactively — after informal settlement or low-density development — is consistently more expensive: utilities must be installed under existing structures, and political resistance from settled communities blocks roads or densification. (See [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md))
- Leapfrog development (disconnected growth beyond the urban fringe) destroys agglomeration potential by separating residents from employment and services without the network density to create spillovers. (See [claim-0035](../claims/claim-0035.md))
- NZ faces a structural infrastructure deficit: Te Waihanga's Forward Guidance projects spending rising from just over $20bn/year now to more than $40bn/year by the 2050s, with ~60% of that needing to go towards renewing and replacing existing assets rather than new builds. (See [claim-0119](../claims/claim-0119.md))
- NZ spends heavily relative to peers but gets poor value: ~5.8% of GDP/year on infrastructure over the past 20 years (top of the OECD by spend) while ranking towards the bottom of the OECD on investment efficiency/"bang for buck". (See [claim-0118](../claims/claim-0118.md))
- More than $330bn of NZ's existing infrastructure assets, most built after 1950, are now reaching the end of their usable life simultaneously — a maintenance and renewals bow wave compounding the new-build demand above. (See [claim-0122](../claims/claim-0122.md))
- Consenting costs alone consume around $1.3bn/year across NZ infrastructure projects, rising to ~16% of total budget for projects under $200,000, compounded by a fragmented zoning landscape (1,175 land-use zones across 67 territorial authorities, versus 13 in Japan). (See [claim-0120](../claims/claim-0120.md), [claim-0121](../claims/claim-0121.md))
- Early-stage infrastructure investment in cities with rapid growth (especially in the developing world) determines land values and urban form for generations; the returns to getting sequencing right are very high. (See [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md))

## How it works (mechanisms)

- Infrastructure provision ahead of settlement concentrates growth in planned areas, avoiding the path-dependence costs of retrofitting. Structured lots with roads, water, and sewage attract formal development; unserviced land generates informal housing. (See [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md))
- Enabling infrastructure in targeted growth corridors makes housing supply reform effective: consented density cannot be built without water and wastewater capacity.
- Enabling housing near existing or planned infrastructure capacity (e.g. transit stations) increases the number of people who benefit from and pay for that infrastructure, raising the return on the original investment; Australian modelling (Infrastructure Victoria) finds servicing a home costs ~NZ$68,000 more in a dispersed city than a compact one. (See [claim-0126](../claims/claim-0126.md), [claim-0127](../claims/claim-0127.md))
- Regional infrastructure investment can mitigate hyper-ageing in provincial areas by maintaining minimum service standards and reducing the cost of living in smaller communities — potentially retaining some working-age population.
- The sequencing logic — institutions → infrastructure → targeted interventions — means infrastructure provision amplifies the returns to other policy levers; the reverse order consistently produces worse outcomes. (See [claim-0038](../claims/claim-0038.md))

## Preconditions

- Long-term funding commitments: infrastructure investment cycles are 10–30 years; annual appropriations and electoral cycles are structurally too short. Requires multi-year capital programmes, special-purpose vehicles, or development bonds. NZ's existing infrastructure ownership is already split across commercial/private (44%, $10bn), local government (31%, $7.1bn), and central government (25%, $5.8bn) — any funding reform has to work across all three. (See [claim-0123](../claims/claim-0123.md))
- Accurate demand forecasting: building infrastructure for the wrong location, scale, or timing produces stranded assets. Requires integration with spatial plans and demographic projections.
- Land acquisition powers and developer contribution frameworks to share costs and coordinate timing.

## Risks and trade-offs

- Infrastructure provision in advance of demand requires public risk-taking that may not be repaid if growth does not materialise where planned.
- Infrastructure investment in high-cost locations (e.g., building for dispersed rural populations) can be fiscally unsustainable as those populations shrink. (See [claim-0091](../claims/claim-0091.md))
- Building ahead of demand in fast-growing developing cities can be transformative; in slow-growth contexts, the same approach can produce white elephants.
- Centralised provision reduces flexibility; public–private partnerships can improve project economics but introduce complexity and contract risk.
- High spend does not guarantee good outcomes: NZ's own experience (top-decile OECD infrastructure spend, bottom-decile OECD investment efficiency) shows that funding volume without asset management, project assurance, and prioritisation discipline can simply mean paying more for worse results. (See [claim-0118](../claims/claim-0118.md))
