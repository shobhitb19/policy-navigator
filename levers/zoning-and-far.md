# Lever: Zoning and Floor Area Ratio (FAR)

## What this is

Zoning rules specify what activities are permitted on each parcel of land and, through floor area ratio (FAR) or height limits, how much can be built. FAR is the ratio of total floor space to the area of the site; a higher FAR permits denser development.

## What problem it addresses

- Under-supply of housing and commercial space in high-demand locations, driving up prices and blocking labour mobility.
- Suboptimal land use that locks in low-density development in areas with high accessibility.
- Fragmented and inconsistent rules across a metropolitan area that make development uncertain and expensive: NZ has 1,175 land-use zones across 67 territorial authorities, compared to 13 in Japan, despite Japan having more than 20 times NZ's population. (See [claim-0121](../claims/claim-0121.md))

## How it works (mechanisms)

- Higher FAR → more floor space per site → increased housing and commercial supply → downward pressure on rents, all else equal. (See [claim-0027](../claims/claim-0027.md), [claim-0037](../claims/claim-0037.md))
- Mixed-use zoning enables walkable neighbourhoods with reduced car dependence, supporting lower emissions. (See [claim-0028](../claims/claim-0028.md), [claim-0036](../claims/claim-0036.md))
- Simplified permissive zoning near transit nodes concentrates density where accessibility is highest; upzoning around existing or planned infrastructure capacity raises the return on that infrastructure by increasing the number of people who use and pay for it. (See [claim-0126](../claims/claim-0126.md))
- Compact city form is materially cheaper to service than dispersed form: Australian modelling (Infrastructure Victoria) finds the infrastructure needed to service a home costs ~AU$59,000 (~NZ$68,000) more in a dispersed city than a compact one. (See [claim-0127](../claims/claim-0127.md))

## Preconditions

- Infrastructure capacity (water, transport, utilities) to support additional density.
- Governance authority that spans the relevant market area — piecemeal upzoning by individual suburbs can be offset by restrictive neighbouring suburbs.
- Transparent development contribution frameworks to share infrastructure costs between developers and the public.

## Risks and trade-offs

- Without affordable housing requirements, upzoning can raise land values and displace existing low-income residents in the short run.
- Rapid density changes in established neighbourhoods generate opposition that can stall reform.
- FAR changes alone do not guarantee supply response if construction costs, financing, or consenting processes are binding constraints.
