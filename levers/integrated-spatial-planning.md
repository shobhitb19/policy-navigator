# Lever: Integrated Spatial Planning

## What this is

Integrated spatial planning is the practice of developing and implementing a long-term, cross-sector spatial strategy that aligns housing, transport, infrastructure, economic development, and environmental objectives across a city or region — and connects those decisions to national population and investment plans. It is more than a land use plan: it requires institutional machinery that spans ministerial portfolios, tiers of government, and planning horizons extending decades.

This lever addresses the gap between having good individual sectoral policies (a transport plan, a housing plan, an infrastructure programme) and having them coherently organised around a shared spatial logic.

## What problem it addresses

- Housing, transport, and infrastructure decisions made in separate silos produce incoherence: roads built before housing, or housing consented without water capacity, or transit lines that stop short of employment centres.
- NZ's three-year election cycle structurally discourages long-term spatial commitments; decisions that require 10–30 years to deliver returns are systematically under-invested. (See [claim-0119](../claims/claim-0119.md))
- Early infrastructure decisions determine urban form for 150+ years, but without a spatial plan they are made incrementally and reactively. (See [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md))
- Central–local government fragmentation in NZ leaves spatial coordination gaps: immigration settings affect where people settle, but local authorities bear the infrastructure costs without influence over the flow. (See [claim-0020](../claims/claim-0020.md))
- Regulatory fragmentation compounds the coordination problem: NZ has 1,175 land-use zones across 67 territorial authorities, versus 13 in Japan — a country with more than 20 times NZ's population. (See [claim-0121](../claims/claim-0121.md))

## How it works (mechanisms)

- A long-term spatial plan commits infrastructure investment to growth corridors, enabling developers and local authorities to sequence delivery. Land values in planned areas rise to reflect future access, making value-capture financing feasible. (See [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md))
- Cross-sector alignment means transit investment is complemented by density permissions, and density permissions are only unlocked where infrastructure capacity exists — avoiding the common failure modes of each. (See [claim-0199](../claims/claim-0199.md))
- National spatial strategy provides local authorities with a mandate to approve development that would otherwise face NIMBY opposition, shifting the politics.
- Integrated demographic and spatial planning connects population projections (ageing, migration, ethnic diversity) to infrastructure sizing — avoiding both over- and under-provision. (See [claim-0091](../claims/claim-0091.md), [claim-0119](../claims/claim-0119.md))
- Enabling housing near existing or planned transport infrastructure capacity increases the number of people who benefit from and pay for that infrastructure — the spatial logic underpinning Te Waihanga's "upzone around key transport corridors" priority. (See [claim-0126](../claims/claim-0126.md))

## Preconditions

- Cross-ministerial institutional authority: no single agency currently has the mandate to coordinate land use, transport, housing, infrastructure, and population policy in NZ.
- Long-term commitment that survives electoral cycles — requires either statutory entrenchment or sustained cross-party agreement (the Koi Tū paper argues for a population commission as one vehicle). (See [claim-0094](../claims/claim-0094.md))
- Adequate data infrastructure: population projections, infrastructure condition data, land value data, and labour market geographics must be integrated to produce a credible spatial plan.

## Risks and trade-offs

- Integrated plans can become rigid: a 30-year spatial strategy may entrench assumptions that become obsolete (e.g., pre-COVID office demand projections, pre-EV transport investment).
- Central coordination can reduce local responsiveness; subsidiarity requires careful assignment — national strategy should set corridors and targets, local implementation should retain discretion on detail.
- Cross-party durability is rare in NZ's adversarial parliamentary system; Singapore and Denmark are often cited as models but operate under different political cultures.
- Commitments framed as "Supported" can still be contingent on unpassed legislation: the NZ Government's response to the National Infrastructure Plan commits to regional spatial planning aligning Land Transport Management Act and Local Government Act funding decisions, but this depends on the Natural Environment Bill and Planning Bill, with the first national instruments not expected until roughly nine months after Royal Assent. (See [claim-0132](../claims/claim-0132.md))

## Key claims

- [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md) Urban structures last 150+ years; early infrastructure determines city form for generations (Dar es Salaam 1970s roads visible today)
- [claim-0020](../claims/claim-0020.md) Central–local governance fragmentation is the primary structural barrier to Auckland's long-term planning capacity
- [claim-0025](../claims/claim-0025.md) Metro areas with governance bodies had declining sprawl 2000–2006; those without had increasing sprawl
- [claim-0091](../claims/claim-0091.md) NZ population growth slowing and diverging regionally — metro, provincial, and rural trajectories require differentiated spatial strategies
- [claim-0119](../claims/claim-0119.md) Te Waihanga Forward Guidance: infrastructure spending to rise from ~$20bn/year now to >$40bn/year by the 2050s — requires deliberate long-term planning (Te Waihanga 2026)
- [claim-0121](../claims/claim-0121.md) NZ has 1,175 land-use zones across 67 territorial authorities vs. Japan's 13 (Te Waihanga 2026)
- [claim-0126](../claims/claim-0126.md) Priority recommendation: upzone around key transport corridors so more people benefit from and pay for existing infrastructure capacity (Te Waihanga 2026)
- [claim-0132](../claims/claim-0132.md) NZ Government commits to centrally-led regional spatial planning aligning LTMA/LGA infrastructure funding decisions, contingent on the Natural Environment Bill and Planning Bill (NZ Government 2026)
