# Lever: Investment and FDI Attraction

## What this is

This lever covers policies to increase the rate of productive capital investment — both domestic business investment and inward foreign direct investment (FDI). It includes investment incentives (tax treatment of capital expenditure, depreciation rules, R&D co-investment), FDI regulatory settings (screening thresholds, sector restrictions, investor visa conditions), investment attraction agencies, and the broader enabling conditions (rule of law, talent availability, urban quality) that determine where internationally mobile capital locates.

FDI is treated as a distinct dimension because of its technology transfer and management practice spillover effects — beyond its direct capital contribution. (See [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md))

## What problem it addresses

- NZ's capital stock per worker is approximately 50% of OECD comparator levels — a structural deficit that directly suppresses labour productivity. (See [claim-0079](../claims/claim-0079.md))
- NZ's business investment rate is approximately 2.8% of GDP, versus approximately 3.9% for Australia — a persistent gap that compounds over time. (See [claim-0086](../claims/claim-0086.md))
- Auckland's annual share of new business attributable to FDI is more than 80% below the OECD peer average — limiting the flow of management practices and frontier technology into the economy. (See [claim-0090](../claims/claim-0090.md))
- Low FDI is one of four diffusion channels on which NZ sits below the OECD median; it reinforces the broader technology adoption deficit. (See [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md))
- NZ conducts only 0.02% of total OECD R&D; GERD is 1.5% of GDP versus an OECD average of approximately 2.7%. (See [claim-0080](../claims/claim-0080.md))

## How it works (mechanisms)

- **Capital investment:** Higher domestic investment rates raise the capital-to-labour ratio directly, increasing output per worker. Investment also embeds newer technology vintages — the complementarity identified in Treasury AN 25/12. (See [claim-0084](../claims/claim-0084.md))
- **FDI spillovers:** Foreign-owned firms bring management practices, supply chain relationships, and frontier technology that diffuse to domestic firms through labour mobility, demonstration effects, and buyer-supplier interactions. The spillover effect is typically larger when host-country absorptive capacity is high.
- **Scale effects:** FDI from globally connected firms increases Auckland's integration into global corporate networks. Auckland already ranks 63rd globally on corporate connectivity; FDI attraction can improve this standing and deepen the city's function as a regional hub. (See [claim-0090](../claims/claim-0090.md))
- **Venture capital and growth finance:** Improving access to patient capital for growth-stage firms accelerates the investment cycle that embeds new technology. NZ is below OECD median on VC availability.

## Preconditions

- **Urban quality and talent:** Internationally mobile capital locates where skilled workers want to live. Auckland's housing affordability, congestion, and productivity gaps make it less competitive for mobile investment than peer cities. (See [claim-0085](../claims/claim-0085.md))
- **Absorptive capacity:** FDI spillovers are larger when domestic firms can learn from foreign entrants; this requires workforce skills, proximity (agglomeration), and management capability.
- **Stable, predictable regulation:** FDI is sensitive to regulatory uncertainty. NZ's OIO screening framework and changing investment rules affect investor confidence.
- **Immigration settings aligned with investment:** talent-attracting immigration settings are a prerequisite for many FDI decisions; New Zealand's falling GTCI attract rank (23rd) is a concern. (See [claim-0096](../claims/claim-0096.md))

## Risks and trade-offs

- Investment incentives (accelerated depreciation, tax credits) can be costly and may subsidise investment that would have occurred anyway; design for additionality matters.
- FDI attraction can create dependence on footloose capital that exits when conditions change; domestic investment deepening is more durable but slower.
- Liberalising FDI rules in sensitive sectors (land, infrastructure, strategic assets) raises sovereignty and national security concerns that require separate treatment from generic business investment.
- The gap between NZ and OECD peers on capital intensity is so large (~50%) that closing it fully through policy alone is unlikely in a single political cycle; realistic goals involve narrowing the gap, not eliminating it.

## Key claims

- [claim-0079](../claims/claim-0079.md) NZ capital stock per worker ~50% of OECD comparators — structural deficit suppressing labour productivity (Winter et al. 2025)
- [claim-0084](../claims/claim-0084.md) Capital investment is simultaneously a diffusion vehicle — "why not both" complementarity (Treasury AN 25/12)
- [claim-0086](../claims/claim-0086.md) Auckland productivity 15–20% below international peers; NZ business investment 2.8% vs Australia 3.9% of GDP
- [claim-0090](../claims/claim-0090.md) Auckland FDI share of new business 80%+ below OECD peer average
- [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md) NZ below OECD median on FDI stock and all four main diffusion channels (Winter et al. 2025)
- [claim-0154](../claims/claim-0154.md) Immigration neither cause nor solution to NZ productivity challenge; must be paired with capital intensity improvements (NZPC 2022)
