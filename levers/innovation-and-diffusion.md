# Lever: Innovation and Technology Diffusion

## What this is

This lever covers policies that accelerate the rate at which firms adopt and adapt new technologies — the primary pathway through which the economy raises productivity. It includes direct R&D subsidies, technology extension services, innovation vouchers, digital infrastructure investment, and — critically — the urban and agglomeration conditions that enable knowledge spillovers and inter-firm learning.

The framing follows Treasury AN 25/12's "why not both" insight: capital investment and technology diffusion are complements, not alternatives. Policy that treats them as separate silos will underperform on both. The agglomeration-diffusion link makes this lever explicitly place-based: cities are the primary geography through which technology spreads.

## What problem it addresses

- Approximately 95% of NZ firms innovate by adopting/adapting existing technologies; only ~5% create new-to-world innovations (BOS 2023). Policy focused exclusively on frontier R&D misses the diffusion challenge facing the vast majority of the economy. (See [claim-0080](../claims/claim-0080.md))
- The share of NZ firms "fully up to date" on their core technology has fallen from 51% (2007) to 44% (2022) — the diffusion problem is worsening. (See [claim-0081](../claims/claim-0081.md))
- Technology adoption speed explains approximately 25% of cross-country GDP per capita growth differentials (Comin & Hobijn, 2011) — it is one of the most powerful single levers available. (See [claim-0082](../claims/claim-0082.md))
- NZ is below the OECD median on all four main diffusion channels: trade openness, FDI stock, clustering/agglomeration quality, and access to finance. (See [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md))
- Auckland's poor clustering conditions (congestion, dispersed urban form, poor rail/road quality) directly constrain the agglomeration channel of diffusion. (See [claim-0067](../claims/claim-0067.md), [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md))
- NZ's frontier firms — the domestic source of diffusion — operate at less than half the labour productivity of the small-advanced-economy frontier, and NZ non-frontier firms receive no measurable diffusion from the international frontier, making domestic frontier performance unusually consequential. (See [claim-0211](../claims/claim-0211.md), [claim-0212](../claims/claim-0212.md))

## How it works (mechanisms)

- **Direct diffusion support:** technology extension services (advisors who bring new practices to firms), innovation vouchers for SMEs, and shared R&D facilities lower the cost of adoption for the 95% of firms on the adoption pathway.
- **Capital investment as diffusion:** investing in new plant, equipment, and software simultaneously raises capital intensity and embeds newer technology vintages — the mechanism that makes capital and diffusion policy complements. (See [claim-0084](../claims/claim-0084.md))
- **Clustering and agglomeration:** dense urban environments support tacit knowledge exchange, informal technology spillovers, and the labour mobility that carries know-how between firms. Better-functioning cities are better diffusion environments. (See [claim-0196](../claims/claim-0196.md))
- **Trade and FDI:** exposure to imported goods with embedded technology disciplines domestic firms to adopt; FDI brings management practices and frontier technology. Both channels are currently below OECD median for NZ. (See [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md), [claim-0090](../claims/claim-0090.md))
- **Talent and skills:** vocational and technical skills are the human capital that makes technology adoption possible at the firm level. NZ's GTCI ranking of 58th on vocational/technical skills is a binding constraint on diffusion. (See [claim-0096](../claims/claim-0096.md))

## Preconditions

- Absorptive capacity within firms: technology extension only works if firms have the management capability and workforce skills to act on advice. Complementary investment in management quality and vocational training is required.
- Finance access: adopting new technology requires capital. NZ's below-median VC availability and high SME interest rate spreads constrain the investment that embeds technology. (See [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md))
- Dense, well-functioning urban environments: clustering depends on cities that enable face-to-face interaction and labour mobility. Planning and transport reform are preconditions for the agglomeration channel of diffusion.

## Risks and trade-offs

- Technology extension services have mixed international evidence; design matters enormously and poorly targeted schemes generate deadweight.
- R&D tax credits disproportionately benefit large firms with dedicated R&D units; they may not reach the 95% of NZ firms that adopt rather than create.
- Agglomeration-led diffusion is a long-term process; urban form change takes decades, making it an unreliable near-term instrument on its own.
- The Australian Productivity Commission (2023) estimated that reviving diffusion could lift Australian GDP by 1.6–4.9%; the NZ figure would likely be in a similar range but is not independently quantified.

## Key claims

- [claim-0080](../claims/claim-0080.md) 95% of NZ firms adopt/adapt technology; only ~5% create new-to-world — diffusion is the dominant innovation pathway (BOS 2023)
- [claim-0081](../claims/claim-0081.md) Share of NZ firms "fully up to date" on technology: 51% (2007) → 44% (2022) — diffusion worsening
- [claim-0082](../claims/claim-0082.md) Technology adoption speed explains ~25% of cross-country GDP per capita growth differentials (Comin & Hobijn 2011)
- [claim-0083](../claims/claim-0083.md) NZ below OECD median on all four diffusion channels: trade, FDI, clustering/infrastructure, capital/finance
- [claim-0084](../claims/claim-0084.md) Capital investment is simultaneously a diffusion vehicle — "why not both" complementarity
- [claim-0096](../claims/claim-0096.md) NZ GTCI rank fell 11th → 18th; vocational/technical skills 58th/135
- [claim-0211](../claims/claim-0211.md) NZ frontier firms less than half the labour productivity of the SAE frontier; gap widened 48% → 45% over 2003–16 (NZPC 2021)
- [claim-0212](../claims/claim-0212.md) NZ non-frontier firms receive diffusion only from the domestic frontier — no measurable diffusion from the international frontier (NZPC 2021)
- [claim-0213](../claims/claim-0213.md) NZ 90:10 firm productivity ratio ~5 — narrow, but likely reflecting a low slow-moving frontier rather than effective diffusion (NZPC 2021)
- [claim-0218](../claims/claim-0218.md) Small economies should focus innovation resources on a few areas with critical mass, not "sub-therapeutic doses" (NZPC 2021)
- [claim-0219](../claims/claim-0219.md) Successful SAEs build innovation ecosystems around anchor firms providing "canopy cover" (NZPC 2021)
- [claim-0241](../claims/claim-0241.md) NZ firms converge faster to the local frontier than the national frontier — diffusion is geographically localised even within NZ (Zheng 2016)
- [claim-0243](../claims/claim-0243.md) Domestic tradability accelerates convergence to the national frontier — market reach as a diffusion channel (Zheng 2016)
