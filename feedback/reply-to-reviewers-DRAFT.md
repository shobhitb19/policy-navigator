Subject: Your comments on the spatial policy paper — what came of them

All,

Thank you for the comments on version 1. I've worked through them one at a
time over the last few days, and the paper is now at version 1.8. Several of
your points changed the argument rather than the wording, which is the useful
kind of feedback and the rarer kind. This is a single note to the three of you
rather than separate replies, partly because some of the points interact, and
partly because in a couple of places one of you supplied the answer to another.

Below is what changed, and — at the end — where I haven't taken the advice and
why.

## The objection that did the most damage, and the most good

Hamish asked why congestion and expensive housing should count as evidence of
dysfunction when almost every high-performing city has both, and asked for a
comparator that had fixed it. He was right, and the paper had no answer. If
those costs are universal among cities that do well, they cannot by themselves
explain why Auckland does badly.

Section 3.11 now takes it head on. The argument is that costs are the price of
access, so the question is not whether a city carries them but what they buy —
and Auckland carries the cost structure of a successful agglomeration while
returning the productivity structure of an unsuccessful one. It ends on a test
the paper has to meet: if the costs were simply the price of scale, the premium
should be commensurate with them. It isn't. Conceding the premise turned out to
strengthen the argument rather than weaken it, because it moves the anomaly from
"Auckland has problems" to "Auckland pays for something it isn't receiving".

## Where the paper sits in the literature

Andrew's point that the paper went straight to modern meta-analyses without
situating itself was fair. Section 2 now opens with Marshall, Porter, and
Krugman and the new economic geography, and makes the connecting claim that the
matching, sharing and learning mechanisms the paper relies on are Marshall's
three reasons restated with a century of data behind them.

Reading McCann properly was the single most valuable thing to come out of the
review. His argument is stronger than I had it: not merely that geography
matters, but that the New Zealand productivity debate has been conducted almost
entirely in institutional and market-versus-intervention terms, and that seen
through economic geography "there is nothing really paradoxical about New
Zealand's productivity performance". The paradox dissolves. That is a better
statement of this paper's thesis than anything I had written, so I've quoted him
— in Section 8 rather than Section 2, because what the paper needs from him is
not lineage but the observation that this was said, in a policy journal,
seventeen years ago.

Andrew's point that it did not take is kept, but narrowed. The claim is now about
uptake rather than absence: the literature is known here and accurately stated —
MBIE and MFAT's own Briefing is the proof of that — but the frame is not
routinely applied to domestic policy questions. That version survives somebody
arriving with a counterexample paper. The stronger version would not have, and
the paper had no business making a claim it couldn't defend about twenty years of
other people's analysis.

## The secondary city system

Andrew pushed hardest here and was right that "limited agglomeration effects"
overstated it. Zheng's firm-level work — which the paper already cited elsewhere
— shows the three major cities holding 48% of firms and 63% of employment but
52% of national-frontier firms and 73% of frontier employment. Christchurch and
Wellington carry more than their share of the country's most productive firms,
and Section 5.7 now says so.

Where it ends up is not quite Andrew's framing, though it starts from his
objection. Zheng also finds New Zealand firms converge faster toward their
*local* productivity frontier than the national one — all fifteen industries
catch up locally, only nine nationally. Productivity diffusion is geographically
bounded. So the section now argues the secondary city system is not a network of
complementary centres but closer to the opposite: a set of locally bounded
economies with real specialisations and real internal agglomeration, weakly
coupled to one another and to Auckland. Capability exists; it does not compound.

Section 5.8 also needed Andrew's necessary/not-sufficient distinction, and what
settled it was that the paper already agreed with him in the executive summary
and Section 6 — 5.8 was the part out of step. It now says a better-functioning
Auckland will not automatically lift Dunedin or Invercargill, and that the
national task is to improve the metropolitan system *and* strengthen the
connections along which productivity travels.

## The Long-term Insights Briefing

Andrew was right that MBIE's own work should be cited, and right that it states
the paper's mechanism in the Government's own words. It's now in Section 5, and
at more length in Section 8.

It turned out to be more useful than a supporting citation. The Briefing is
precise that spillovers diminish with distance and that urban density is what
makes clusters powerful, and it applies the core–periphery frame accurately to
New Zealand's position in the world. Its remit is international productivity and
trade, so it has no particular reason to turn that lens inward — but the
domestic question then goes unasked: do our regions carry the density the
mechanism requires? Section 8 asks it and answers with the elasticity evidence.
Auckland's agglomeration elasticity is about 0.04, the same as other major New
Zealand cities, and Auckland is only 5–8% more productive than Hamilton,
Tauranga, Wellington and Christchurch on size alone. If 1.7 million people buy a
premium that small, a cluster of a few thousand workers in a provincial centre
is not generating Marshallian returns.

The conclusion converges with the Briefing rather than against it: its own
Principle 3 emphasises clusters connected to global networks, and the domestic
corollary is that outside Auckland connection does nearly all the work and
density very little.

## Land use: complexity, and the worst of both worlds

Hamish's point about complexity was sharper than he knew. He speculated that
Amsterdam, Copenhagen, Utrecht and Basel probably have complex planning systems.
They are on Figure 5 — I went back to the underlying chart data, and it plots
New Zealand against Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, the Netherlands
and Switzerland. Every comparator country in the paper's own illustration has a
plan-led tradition, sixteen lines above a claim that complexity is the problem.

So the claim is now about **fragmentation**, which is what the Te Waihanga
evidence actually measures: 1,175 zones across 67 territorial authorities is a
statement about how many hands hold the pen, not about intricacy. Section 5.1
sets out two coherent models — simple and permissive, or complex but plan-led
with infrastructure committed in advance — and places New Zealand outside both.

Hamish's "worst of both worlds" formulation is now its own paragraph. It was the
sharpest thing said to me about this draft, and the State of the City 2026
report arrived in the meantime to evidence both halves: Auckland sits in the
global bottom decile for residential density, street connectivity and mixed-use
access, while population growth along its better-connected transit corridors
lags comparable cities badly. The paragraph ends: Auckland has taken on the
costs of both approaches and the benefits of neither.

## The history, and why the tension endures

Andrew was right that "oscillated" was wrong. The paper now describes an
enduring tension that successive governments have managed rather than settled.

His comment prompted a larger change than the one he asked for. Section 6 now
explains *why* the tension endures: New Zealand's centralisation. Councils hold
land use, consenting, local roads, water, waste and public transport, and
deliver a great deal of the country's infrastructure — but not health,
education, tertiary, welfare or justice, and their revenue base is almost
entirely rates. When the centre allocates, places compete for allocation,
because that is the only agency available to them. The zero-sum framing the
section objects to is therefore a rational response to the institutional
structure rather than a failure of imagination.

Checking that section also turned up an error of my own: it had listed transport
planning and social housing among functions held centrally, which is wrong on
both counts. Corrected.

## Smaller changes that came out of the rest

Andrew's caution about the Dublin, Helsinki and Tel Aviv comparisons is now in
the productivity premium box — those cities sit within far more densely
connected regional economic systems, so the gap shouldn't be read as
like-for-like. His observation that Auckland is nonetheless larger than Dublin
and Helsinki is in there too, and does more work for the argument than against
it.

Andrew's question about tax and financial settings prompted a qualification at
the end of Section 5.6. The spatial account is not the only explanation for New
Zealand's shallow business capital, and presenting it as though it were would be
over-claiming. The qualification stops there deliberately. Capital allocation is
a long-running argument with several serious explanations in play, and a paper
about spatial policy is not the place to adjudicate it — so it names no
candidates and takes no position on tax. The narrow claim is the one worth
making: the spatial dimension belongs in that conversation.

Andrew's point about governance — whether a highly centralised state can manage
a metropolitan economy holding a third of the country — is why Section 6 now
carries the centralisation argument. It also now carries the observation that
the distribution of decision rights is itself a spatial variable: the most recent
benchmarking found Auckland performing best where it holds the levers it
controls, and least well where national infrastructure and capital decisions
dominate. Where a decision is made is part of the spatial system rather than
separate from it.

I've stopped short of recommending devolution, and in the end stopped short of
recommending anything. The closing section now sets out what follows if the
argument holds, rather than what the department should do about it. That judgement
belongs to MBIE's leadership, not to a working paper written by one of its
officials — and the paper says so.

Aaron's point that Section 3 is dense and needed tying up at the end is done —
and it had become more necessary in the meantime, since answering Hamish added
four paragraphs to the section. There's now a closing passage restating the
causal chain in plain terms at the end of 3.10, with the comparator question
following it as 3.11.

## Where I haven't followed the advice

**A comparator city that fixed congestion and housing costs** (Hamish). I think
the request is slightly the wrong shape. No large city has eliminated these
pressures, and claiming one had would be easy to falsify. The defensible claim
is about keeping costs proportionate to benefits.

**The capability-node framing** (Andrew). I've taken the factual point — there
is real capability outside Auckland, and the paper now says so with firm-level
evidence — but not the framing. Three reasons. The evidence for it is in your
mapping work, which isn't published, and the paper is being prepared for
publication. Coleman, Maré and Zheng find New Zealand cities became *more*
diversified rather than more specialised over 1976–2013, with 23 of 30 urban
areas seeing their specialisation index fall. And "network" claims more than has
been shown — the examples are striking individually, but what connects them
isn't demonstrated, and on Zheng's evidence the connections are precisely what's
missing. That last point is why the section now argues something close to the
opposite of the reframing, while conceding the fact that prompted it.

**MBIE's own institutional history** (Hamish). The account of the cities and
regions branch, the Provincial Development Unit and the formation of MHUD is
substantive and I don't disagree with any of it. But the paper is heading for
publication, and a published position paper is not the right vehicle for an
account of its own department's institutional decisions — it would need more
length and more care than the paper can give it, and it would change how the
whole thing reads. The substantive claim is already there: Section 8 argues that
no institution is clearly responsible for the productivity performance of the
spatial system. Your history explains how that gap arose rather than
establishing that it exists, so the argument doesn't depend on it. I'd like to
use it in an internal discussion, where it would carry properly.

**The three-phase history** (Andrew). I've kept an enduring tension rather than
a single 1984 discontinuity. The abolition of the provinces was a centralising
act; post-war policy dispersed industry while simultaneously accommodating very
large metropolitan growth; and the Provincial Growth Fund is difficult to
describe as spatially neutral in intent — which is, I think, closer to Hamish's
reading of the same period.

**The New Zealand Initiative's housing work** (Andrew). Not added. The paper's
housing and land-use evidence is already carried by Nunns, Te Waihanga, MHUD and
the Council Chief Economist, and a further source would broaden the range of
perspectives without strengthening the evidence. I've kept the reference list as
tight as I can.

That's everything you raised.

Thanks again — this was a lot of work on your part and the paper is better for
it.

Richard
