## housing-supply-reform: Housing Supply Reform

**Objective** — Housing supply reform encompasses the regulatory, financial, and institutional changes that actively enable new dwelling construction, particularly in high-demand, high-productivity locations. It aims to reduce the price premium of productive locations, enabling better matching between workers and firms. The lever addresses a decades-long structural failure in which NZ's planning and consenting system has produced capitalised scarcity rather than productive investment — with the national housing stock now approximately $1.6 trillion, or around four times GDP, up from roughly twice GDP in the 1990s.

**Mechanism targeted** — Removing development restrictions increases the feasible supply of dwellings near jobs and services, reducing rents and enabling more efficient spatial sorting of labour. Streamlined consenting reduces the cost and time burden of building, lowering the threshold at which new supply is financially viable for developers. Infrastructure funding reform — through development levies, special-purpose vehicles, or tax increment financing — resolves a coordination failure where developers cannot build because infrastructure does not exist, and councils cannot fund infrastructure without certainty of development. National policy instruments such as the National Policy Statement on Urban Development (NPS-UD) and the Medium Density Residential Standards (MDRS) override local restrictive zoning, correcting the political economy failure in which incumbent homeowners capture local planning processes and block supply.

**Primary owner** — Ministry of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)

**MBIE role**
- Lead economic analysis and modelling to quantify the GDP and productivity consequences of housing undersupply, providing the evidence base that justifies regulatory reform.
- Support reform of development contribution rules and infrastructure funding mechanisms by advising on commercial structures (special-purpose vehicles, tax increment financing, development bonds) that can unlock investment.
- Coordinate with Treasury on fiscal settings — depreciation, land tax, and GST treatment — that affect the relative returns to residential construction versus speculative land-holding.
- Monitor and report on the supply pipeline (consents, completions, time-to-build) to identify where bottlenecks remain after regulatory changes.
- Engage with the construction sector to assess capacity constraints that may limit the supply response to regulatory reform, and to develop workforce and procurement strategies that increase build capacity.

**Constraints**
- Supply-side reform alone does not guarantee affordability in the short run: the time lag between policy change and additional completed dwellings can be three to seven years, depending on consenting, financing, and construction timelines.
- Infrastructure capacity — water, wastewater, and transport — must exist or be funded in advance; consented density cannot be built without it, and reform without complementary infrastructure investment produces consented-but-unbuilt sites.
- The construction sector must have adequate capacity to respond; if it is already operating near full capacity, regulatory reform shifts land values without producing additional homes.
- Without complementary inclusionary requirements or continued social housing provision, reform may increase overall supply without improving affordability for the lowest-income households.
- Planning reform frequently provokes reversal by successor governments; the Auckland Unitary Plan demonstrates that even successful reforms take decades to undo their predecessors, and political durability cannot be assumed.

**Related claims**
- claim-0066: Restrictive Auckland land use reduced NZ GDP by 0.9–1.8% by preventing workers from living where their productivity would be highest (Nunns 2019).
- claim-0075: Modelling of removing all NZ housing supply constraints finds GDP would be 5.0–8.4% higher and the labour force 3.3–7.2% larger (Nunns 2021).
- claim-0078: NZ housing stock is approximately $1.6 trillion, or around four times GDP — land wealth has roughly doubled relative to productive output over three decades, reflecting capitalised scarcity rather than productive investment.
- claim-0088: Auckland is now in the top third globally for new homes per capita and exceeds the peer-city average by around 60% — post-Unitary Plan reform demonstrates that supply reform can deliver measurable increases in housing production.
- claim-0070: High land prices do not directly reduce GDP; the mechanism is spatial misallocation — workers and firms are unable to sort into high-productivity locations when housing near those locations is unaffordable.
- claim-0107: A 1% rise in NZ population caused house prices to rise around 0.5% at mid-century but around 2.0% in recent decades — a fourfold increase that reflects the reduced supply responsiveness of the planning system over time.
- claim-0108: Auckland's 1970 District Scheme halved housing capacity from approximately 475,000 to approximately 250,000 people — a 'great downzoning' whose effects were not substantially reversed until the 2016 Unitary Plan.
- claim-0190: Preliminary modelling finds that artificially raising rents also lowers wages, so density controls and other development restrictions squeeze workers on two fronts simultaneously — higher housing costs and lower wages — implying the welfare cost of supply constraints is larger than a housing-cost-only analysis suggests (Donovan 2019, preliminary).
- claim-0191: Preliminary modelling finds NZ housing supply is affected by adjustment frictions ("sticky quantities"), consistent with the documented decline in NZ's housing supply elasticity over recent decades (Donovan 2019, preliminary).
- claim-0192: Preliminary modelling finds urban development generates positive regional spillovers, suggesting the case for enabling development extends beyond the developing city itself to its surrounding region (Donovan 2019, preliminary).

## infrastructure-provision: Infrastructure Provision

**Objective** — Infrastructure provision means the deliberate planning, funding, and sequencing of enabling infrastructure — water, wastewater, roading, public transport, energy, telecommunications, and social infrastructure — in advance of or alongside urban growth. The lever aims to ensure that new development generates agglomeration benefits rather than sprawl costs by getting the timing and location of infrastructure right relative to settlement patterns. It also addresses NZ's structural infrastructure deficit, where investment demand is forecast to require approximately 5.8% of GDP annually for 30 years just to keep pace with population growth and replace ageing assets.

**Mechanism targeted** — Infrastructure provided ahead of settlement concentrates growth in planned areas, avoiding the path-dependence costs of retrofitting — utilities installed under existing structures are consistently more expensive, and political resistance from settled communities blocks roads and densification. Structured lots with roads, water, and sewage attract formal development; unserviced land generates informal housing or low-density sprawl that destroys agglomeration potential by separating residents from employment without the network density to create knowledge spillovers. Enabling infrastructure in targeted growth corridors makes housing supply reform effective: consented density cannot be built without water and wastewater capacity. Regional infrastructure investment can also mitigate hyper-ageing in provincial areas by maintaining minimum service standards that retain working-age population. The sequencing logic — institutions, then infrastructure, then targeted interventions — means infrastructure provision amplifies the returns to other policy levers; reversing this order consistently produces worse outcomes.

**Primary owner** — Infrastructure New Zealand / Infrastructure Commission (Te Waihanga)

**MBIE role**
- Provide economic analysis of infrastructure investment priorities, including quantification of the costs of under-provision (stranded development, leapfrog sprawl, population loss from service-deficient areas).
- Advise on commercial structures for infrastructure financing, including development bonds, tax increment financing, and public-private partnerships.
- Support regulatory settings for the construction and infrastructure sectors — including procurement frameworks, workforce development, and productivity in the construction industry — that determine the cost of delivering infrastructure.
- Coordinate with HUD and local government on the relationship between infrastructure provision and housing supply reform, ensuring both levers are sequenced to reinforce rather than undermine each other.
- Contribute to the development of long-term infrastructure plans by providing demographic projections, regional economic analysis, and spatial demand forecasting.

**Constraints**
- Long-term funding commitments are required: infrastructure investment cycles are 10–30 years, whereas annual appropriations and three-year electoral cycles are structurally too short. Multi-year capital programmes or development bonds are needed but require fiscal and governance reform.
- Accurate demand forecasting is essential: infrastructure built for the wrong location, scale, or timing produces stranded assets. This requires integration with spatial plans and demographic projections that are not always available or accurate.
- Land acquisition powers and developer contribution frameworks must be in place to share costs and coordinate timing between the public and private sectors.
- Infrastructure provision in advance of demand requires public risk-taking that may not be repaid if growth does not materialise where planned; this risk is particularly acute in fast-growing developing cities and in NZ's smaller regional centres.
- Building enabling infrastructure for dispersed rural populations can be fiscally unsustainable as those populations shrink, requiring difficult decisions about which communities to service and which to allow to consolidate.

**Related claims**
- claim-0045: Urban structures last 150 years or more; early infrastructure decisions determine city form for generations — Dar es Salaam's 1970s road plans still match today's network, and those neighbourhoods have the city's highest land values, illustrating how early infrastructure choices lock in spatial outcomes.
- claim-0038: The sequencing principle — institutions, then infrastructure, then targeted interventions — is well-established; getting this wrong consistently produces worse outcomes, and targeted slum-upgrading interventions fail before connective infrastructure is in place.
- claim-0035: Leapfrog development accounted for more than 40% of built-up growth in major African cities between 2000 and 2010 — disconnected urban form destroys agglomeration potential by separating residents from employment and services.
- claim-0199: Doubling density reduces the unit cost of local public services by 17% — denser settlement enabled by well-sequenced infrastructure is more fiscally efficient than sprawl.
- claim-0112: The Resource Management Act 1991 removed specific requirements for infrastructure planning from NZ planning legislation, structurally decoupling urban development from infrastructure provision and creating the fragmentation problem this lever addresses.
- claim-0118: NZ spends 5.8% of GDP per year on infrastructure — near the top of the OECD by volume — yet ranks near the bottom on efficiency, indicating the binding constraint is poor investment quality and sequencing, not insufficient funding.
- claim-0119: Te Waihanga's "Forward Guidance" demand forecasting projects NZ infrastructure spending needs rising to over $40 billion/year by the 2050s, with around 60% required for renewals of existing assets rather than new capacity — underscoring the scale of the demand-forecasting and multi-decade funding commitment this lever requires.
- claim-0120: Consenting costs NZ infrastructure providers an estimated $1.3 billion/year, rising to 16% of total budget for small projects — a direct illustration of the regulatory friction that raises the cost of delivering planned infrastructure.
- claim-0122: NZ holds over $330 billion in infrastructure assets, most built after 1950 and now reaching the end of their service life simultaneously — a renewals "bow wave" that compounds the new-capacity demand this lever must fund.
- claim-0128: The NZ Government supported or supported in-principle all 16 recommendations of the 2026 National Infrastructure Plan, including demand forecasting, regional spatial planning, and multi-year budgeting reforms central to this lever's mechanism.
- claim-0130: NZ ranks in the top 10% of OECD countries for infrastructure spending but the bottom 10% for quality and efficiency — the clearest single data point for why this lever frames infrastructure provision as a sequencing and governance problem rather than a funding-volume problem.
- claim-0137: NZ's public-sector infrastructure gap is estimated at $104 billion already accrued, with a further $106 billion "future infrastructure gap" projected over the next 30 years between planned and required spending — quantifying the absorptive-capacity shortfall this lever exists to close.

## innovation-and-diffusion: Innovation and Technology Diffusion

**Objective** — This lever covers policies that accelerate the rate at which firms adopt and adapt existing technologies, as well as the conditions that enable knowledge spillovers and inter-firm learning. It recognises that approximately 95% of NZ firms innovate by adopting or adapting existing technologies rather than creating new-to-world innovations, and that the primary challenge for most of the economy is diffusion, not frontier R&D. The lever also addresses capital investment as a diffusion vehicle, since firms that invest in new plant and equipment simultaneously raise capital intensity and embed newer technology vintages.

**Mechanism targeted** — Direct diffusion support — through technology extension services, innovation vouchers for SMEs, and shared R&D facilities — lowers the cost of adoption for the vast majority of firms that are on an adoption rather than invention pathway. Capital investment functions simultaneously as a diffusion vehicle: investing in new plant, equipment, and software embeds newer technology vintages into the productive capital stock, making capital and diffusion policy natural complements rather than alternatives. Dense urban environments support tacit knowledge exchange, informal technology spillovers, and the labour mobility that carries know-how between firms; better-functioning cities are therefore better diffusion environments, and Auckland's poor clustering conditions — congestion, dispersed urban form, and poor infrastructure quality — directly constrain this agglomeration channel. Trade and foreign direct investment provide additional diffusion pathways: exposure to imported goods with embedded technology disciplines domestic firms to adopt, and FDI brings management practices and frontier technology; NZ is currently below the OECD median on both channels.

**Primary owner** — MBIE (lead agency for business and innovation policy)

**MBIE role**
- Design and fund technology extension services that bring new practices to firms, with particular focus on SMEs that lack dedicated R&D units and cannot easily access frontier knowledge independently.
- Develop and administer innovation voucher schemes that lower the transaction cost of accessing external expertise for small and medium-sized firms.
- Coordinate trade policy settings that increase import intensity and FDI flows as diffusion channels — working with MFAT on investment attraction and trade facilitation.
- Analyse and report on diffusion performance — including the share of firms "fully up to date" on their core technology — to track whether policies are closing the diffusion gap.
- Lead cross-agency work on the urban and agglomeration conditions (cities, clustering, infrastructure quality) that constitute the diffusion environment for the majority of the economy.

**Constraints**
- Absorptive capacity within firms is a prerequisite: technology extension services only work if firms have the management capability and workforce skills to act on external advice; without this, extension generates no uptake.
- Finance access is a binding constraint for many NZ SMEs: adopting new technology requires capital, and NZ's below-median venture capital availability and high SME interest rate spreads limit investment even when firms are motivated to upgrade.
- Technology extension services have mixed international evidence on effectiveness; design matters enormously and poorly targeted schemes generate deadweight without meaningfully accelerating diffusion.
- R&D tax credits disproportionately benefit large firms with dedicated R&D units; they may not reach the 95% of NZ firms that adopt rather than create, limiting their impact on the diffusion challenge.
- Agglomeration-led diffusion is a long-term process: urban form change takes decades, meaning that improving Auckland's clustering conditions will improve diffusion only gradually.

**Related claims**
- claim-0081: Approximately 95% of NZ firms adopt or adapt existing technology; only around 5% create new-to-world innovations — diffusion is the dominant innovation pathway for the NZ economy (Business Operations Survey 2023).
- claim-0080: The share of NZ firms "fully up to date" on their core technology has fallen from 51% in 2007 to 44% in 2022 — the diffusion problem is worsening, not improving, over time.
- claim-0083: Capital investment is simultaneously a diffusion vehicle, creating a "why not both" complementarity between investment and diffusion policy — raising capital intensity and embedding newer technology vintages are the same act (Winter et al. 2025 / Treasury AN 25/12).
- claim-0084: NZ is below the OECD median on import intensity, FDI stock, infrastructure quality, and access to capital and finance — all four of the main diffusion channels identified in the literature.
- claim-0079: NZ capital stock per worker is approximately 50% of OECD comparator levels — a structural deficit that suppresses labour productivity and reflects decades of under-investment in the productive capital stock.
- claim-0057: A 10 percentage point increase in the share of university graduates in a city is associated with a 3% productivity premium — an externality from knowledge spillovers that illustrates why clustering and agglomeration matter for diffusion.
- claim-0196: Doubling density is associated with a 21% increase in patent rates — dense cities generate more innovation through the informal knowledge spillovers that proximity enables.

## integrated-spatial-planning: Integrated Spatial Planning

**Objective** — Integrated spatial planning involves developing and implementing a long-term, cross-sector spatial strategy that aligns housing, transport, infrastructure, economic development, and environmental objectives across a city or region — and connects those decisions to national population and investment plans. It is more than a land use plan: it requires institutional machinery spanning ministerial portfolios, tiers of government, and planning horizons extending decades. The lever aims to prevent the incoherence that results when housing, transport, and infrastructure decisions are made in silos, producing roads built before housing, or housing consented without water capacity, or transit lines that stop short of employment centres.

**Mechanism targeted** — A long-term spatial plan commits infrastructure investment to growth corridors, enabling developers and local authorities to sequence delivery in a coordinated way. Land values in planned areas rise to reflect future access, making value-capture financing mechanisms feasible and creating a fiscal logic for plan-led development. Cross-sector alignment means transit investment is complemented by density permissions, and density permissions are only unlocked where infrastructure capacity exists — avoiding the common failure modes of transit investment without density, or density permission without infrastructure. A national spatial strategy also provides local authorities with a mandate to approve development that would otherwise face local opposition, shifting the political economy of planning decisions. Integrating demographic and spatial planning connects population projections — covering ageing, migration, and ethnic diversity — to infrastructure sizing, avoiding both over-provision and under-provision.

**Primary owner** — Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet (DPMC) / Cross-portfolio spatial unit

**MBIE role**
- Provide population and labour market projections that inform spatial planning at regional and national scales, drawing on demographic data, migration forecasts, and economic geography modelling.
- Lead economic analysis of the costs of spatial incoherence — including infrastructure mismatch, labour misallocation, and productivity losses from dispersed settlement — to build the evidence base for integrated planning investment.
- Develop and maintain spatial economic data infrastructure, including land value data, employment geography, and infrastructure condition data, that is required for credible integrated planning.
- Advise on the commercial and regulatory frameworks needed to implement integrated plans, including value-capture financing, development contributions, and public land development vehicles.
- Contribute cross-portfolio coordination on the settings — immigration, housing, infrastructure, transport, regional development — that a spatial plan must integrate to be coherent.

**Constraints**
- Cross-ministerial institutional authority is lacking: no single NZ agency currently has the mandate to coordinate land use, transport, housing, infrastructure, and population policy, meaning integrated planning requires sustained cross-portfolio political commitment rather than a single agency's initiative.
- Long-term commitment that survives electoral cycles is required — a 30-year spatial strategy is only credible if it is either statutorily entrenched or supported by sustained cross-party agreement, both of which are rare in NZ's adversarial parliamentary system.
- Integrated plans can become rigid and entrench assumptions that become obsolete — pre-COVID office demand projections, or pre-EV transport investment assumptions, illustrate how 30-year plans can be overtaken by structural change.
- Central coordination can reduce local responsiveness; subsidiarity requires careful assignment of which decisions should be centralised and which devolved to territorial authorities.
- Adequate data infrastructure — population projections, infrastructure condition data, land value data, and labour market geographics — must be integrated to produce a credible spatial plan, and NZ's data infrastructure for this purpose is currently incomplete.

**Related claims**
- claim-0045: Urban structures last 150 years or more, and early infrastructure determines city form for generations — the putty-clay quality of built structures means spatial planning decisions made now lock in outcomes for decades, making long-term integrated planning essential rather than optional.
- claim-0020: Central-local governance fragmentation is the primary structural barrier to Auckland's long-term planning capacity — central government controls most fiscal levers while Auckland Council holds statutory plan-making powers, producing chronic coordination failures on infrastructure sequencing and housing delivery.
- claim-0025: Metropolitan areas with governance bodies had declining sprawl between 2000 and 2006; those without governance bodies had increasing sprawl — the institutional precondition for integrated planning determines its effectiveness in practice.
- claim-0092: NZ population growth is slowing and diverging regionally, with metropolitan, provincial, and rural trajectories requiring differentiated spatial strategies rather than a single national approach.
- claim-0091: NZ's fertility rate was 1.55 in 2025, well below the 2.1 replacement rate; the population is ageing rapidly, with the dependency ratio projected to rise from 54% to 73% by 2073 — demographic shifts that integrated spatial planning must anticipate in infrastructure sizing and urban form decisions.
- claim-0194: The "Amenable City" framing proposes that cities should deliver a bundle of amenities valued by both households and firms, acknowledge pricing failures and heterogeneous preferences, and pursue dynamically efficient policy rather than a fixed end-state — a normative successor framing to the industrial-city model that this lever's institutional machinery would need to operationalise (Donovan 2019).

## investment-and-fdi: Investment and FDI Attraction

**Objective** — This lever covers policies to increase the rate of productive capital investment, both domestic business investment and inward foreign direct investment (FDI). It aims to close NZ's structural capital intensity deficit — with capital stock per worker at approximately 50% of OECD comparator levels — while also attracting FDI for its technology transfer and management practice spillover effects. The lever treats FDI as distinct from domestic investment because of these additional spillover benefits, which diffuse through the wider economy via labour mobility, demonstration effects, and buyer-supplier relationships.

**Mechanism targeted** — Higher domestic investment rates raise the capital-to-labour ratio directly, increasing output per worker. Investment in new plant, equipment, and software simultaneously embeds newer technology vintages, creating a complementarity between capital accumulation and technology diffusion that makes investment policy doubly valuable. FDI spillovers operate through multiple channels: foreign-owned firms bring management practices, supply chain relationships, and frontier technology that diffuse to domestic firms when workers move between employers, when domestic suppliers upgrade to meet foreign buyer standards, and when domestic competitors are exposed to new business practices. FDI from globally connected firms also increases Auckland's integration into global corporate networks, increasing the flow of knowledge, capital, and talent into the economy. Improving access to patient capital for growth-stage domestic firms accelerates the investment cycle that embeds new technology and enables scale.

**Primary owner** — MBIE (lead agency for investment attraction and business environment)

**MBIE role**
- Lead investment attraction through New Zealand Trade and Enterprise (NZTE), targeting sectors where FDI spillovers are likely to be largest and where NZ has comparative advantages that make it a credible investment destination.
- Advise on tax settings affecting investment — including depreciation rules, R&D co-investment, and the treatment of capital expenditure — to improve the after-tax return on productive investment relative to speculative alternatives.
- Review and calibrate the Overseas Investment Act screening framework to ensure FDI regulatory settings attract productive capital without creating undue national security or sovereignty risks.
- Analyse and report on capital intensity gaps between NZ and OECD peers, tracking progress on closing the deficit and identifying sectors where the gap is largest and most tractable.
- Develop and support access to patient capital for growth-stage firms, including working with New Zealand Growth Capital Partners and the venture capital ecosystem.

**Constraints**
- Investment incentives such as accelerated depreciation and tax credits can be costly and may subsidise investment that would have occurred anyway; additionality is difficult to demonstrate and poorly designed schemes generate fiscal cost without proportionate economic benefit.
- Urban quality and talent availability constrain FDI attraction: internationally mobile capital locates where skilled workers want to live and work, and Auckland's housing affordability, congestion, and productivity gaps make it less competitive for mobile investment than peer cities in Australia and elsewhere.
- FDI attraction can create dependence on footloose capital that exits when conditions change; sectors with low sunk costs are particularly susceptible to this risk.
- Liberalising FDI rules in sensitive sectors — land, infrastructure, strategic assets — raises legitimate sovereignty and national security concerns that limit how far liberalisation can go.
- The gap between NZ and OECD peers on capital intensity is approximately 50%, which is so large that closing it fully through policy alone in a single political cycle is not realistic; the lever requires sustained multi-cycle commitment.

**Related claims**
- claim-0079: NZ capital stock per worker is approximately 50% of OECD comparator levels — a structural deficit that directly suppresses labour productivity and represents the primary quantitative target for this lever (Winter et al. 2025).
- claim-0083: Capital investment is simultaneously a diffusion vehicle — the "why not both" complementarity means investment policy and diffusion policy are not substitutes but should be pursued together (Winter et al. 2025 / Treasury AN 25/12).
- claim-0089: Auckland's annual share of new business attributable to FDI is more than 80% below the OECD peer average, severely limiting the flow of management practices and frontier technology into the economy.
- claim-0084: NZ is below the OECD median on FDI stock and on all four of the main diffusion channels — import intensity, FDI, infrastructure quality, and capital and finance access.
- claim-0064: NZ capital stock per hour worked is roughly half that of OECD comparators; Denmark went from 81% more than NZ in 1970 to 164% more by 2019 — illustrating how compounding investment gaps produce increasingly large productivity divergences over time.
- claim-0077: Sydney and Melbourne are approximately three times Auckland's size; this size gap may explain around one-third of the 20% NZ-Australia productivity gap — growing Auckland's effective size through capital and FDI attraction is part of the mechanism for closing the productivity gap.

## land-use-planning: Land Use Planning

**Objective** — Land use planning is the regulatory and statutory framework that determines where different uses — residential, commercial, industrial, open space — are permitted and at what scale. It encompasses strategic plans, district and local plans, spatial strategies, and development assessment processes. The lever aims to align development with infrastructure capacity, capture value from public investment in accessibility, and avoid the long-run path dependencies that arise when low-density or misallocated development locks in suboptimal urban form for generations.

**Mechanism targeted** — A strategic spatial plan aligns infrastructure investment with growth areas, enabling sequenced and cost-effective provision; without this alignment, infrastructure is built reactively at higher cost or demand-side reforms produce consented but unbuilt development. Coordinating land use and transport planning allows transit investment to be complemented by density permissions, generating both productivity and emissions benefits that neither lever can achieve independently. Plan-led systems reduce uncertainty for developers and communities, potentially lowering the risk premium embedded in development costs. The long-run stakes of land use decisions are illustrated by Auckland's 1970 downzoning, which halved the city's housing capacity from approximately 475,000 to approximately 250,000 people and set in motion a four-decade affordability crisis that was not substantially reversed until the 2016 Auckland Unitary Plan — demonstrating how planning choices made in one decade create path dependencies lasting generations.

**Primary owner** — Ministry for the Environment (MfE)

**MBIE role**
- Provide economic analysis of the productivity and GDP consequences of restrictive land use — quantifying spatial misallocation costs and the returns to plan-led reform — to inform MfE and local authority plan-making.
- Advise on the economic and commercial dimensions of value-capture mechanisms that land use plans can enable, including development levies, targeted rates, and infrastructure financing districts.
- Coordinate with HUD and the Infrastructure Commission to ensure land use planning frameworks are integrated with housing supply reform and infrastructure provision, rather than operating as a standalone exercise.
- Contribute to the design of national policy statements that provide enabling frameworks for local plans, particularly where local political economy incentives would otherwise produce restrictive outcomes.
- Monitor regional plan performance — including consent rates, development pipeline, and land price signals — and report on whether plans are delivering the development outcomes they were designed to enable.

**Constraints**
- Plan-led systems can be slow and inflexible if plan reviews are infrequent; in fast-changing markets, plans that accurately reflect conditions when prepared can become binding constraints within a few years.
- Planning frameworks that reflect existing patterns rather than aspirational futures can entrench low density and car dependence by treating current form as the baseline rather than a starting point for change.
- Without coordination mechanisms, plans across adjacent councils can be inconsistent, allowing development pressure to be shifted rather than accommodated — a particular risk in multi-council metropolitan areas.
- The RMA 1991 removed specific requirements for infrastructure planning from NZ planning legislation, structurally decoupling urban development from infrastructure provision; this risk persists wherever land use planning is treated as a standalone exercise rather than integrated with investment and infrastructure decisions.
- Governance authority that spans the functional metropolitan area is a precondition: planning that stops at council boundaries cannot address metropolitan-scale market failures in housing supply or labour mobility.

**Related claims**
- claim-0066: Restrictive Auckland land use reduced NZ GDP by 0.9–1.8% by preventing workers from living where their productivity would be higher — the direct national cost of restrictive zoning that better land use planning aims to prevent.
- claim-0070: High land prices do not directly reduce GDP; the mechanism is spatial misallocation — workers and firms unable to sort efficiently into high-productivity locations; land use planning affects this sorting mechanism.
- claim-0108: Auckland's 1970 District Scheme halved housing capacity from approximately 475,000 to approximately 250,000 people — the archetypal downzoning that demonstrates how land use planning choices create multi-decade path dependencies.
- claim-0112: The RMA 1991 removed specific requirements for infrastructure planning from NZ planning legislation, decoupling urban development from infrastructure provision — the structural gap that integrated land use planning must now bridge.
- claim-0028: Per capita ground-transport CO₂ emissions fall consistently as urban density rises — land use planning that enables density produces emissions benefits as well as productivity benefits.
- claim-0199: Doubling density is associated with a 17% reduction in the unit cost of local public services — compact development enabled by permissive land use planning is more fiscally efficient than dispersed low-density development.
- claim-0190: Preliminary modelling finds that artificially raising rents also lowers wages, so restrictive land use controls squeeze workers on two fronts — higher housing costs and lower wages — implying a larger welfare cost than a housing-cost-only analysis suggests (Donovan 2019, preliminary).
- claim-0192: Preliminary modelling finds urban development generates positive regional spillovers, extending the case for enabling land use beyond the developing city to its surrounding region (Donovan 2019, preliminary).

## metropolitan-governance: Metropolitan Governance

**Objective** — Metropolitan governance refers to formal institutional arrangements for managing the functional urban area as a coherent unit — including coordinating land use, transport, infrastructure, economic development, and sometimes social services across the boundaries of individual local governments. The lever aims to close the governance gap between the administrative boundaries of territorial authorities and the functional economic area that determines how productivity, labour markets, and infrastructure operate in practice. The fragmentation penalty is well-quantified: the absence of any metropolitan governance body is associated with approximately a 6% labour productivity penalty in the affected city.

**Mechanism targeted** — A metropolitan authority with strategic land use powers can align development patterns with infrastructure investment cycles, enabling the long-run spatial planning that fragmented councils cannot credibly commit to. Own-source revenue — through land value capture, development levies, or dedicated taxes — gives the authority fiscal credibility and reduces dependence on central government transfers, which come with political conditions and timing uncertainty. Unified transport planning eliminates modal and spatial gaps that arise when multiple agencies plan independently and no single body is accountable for network-level outcomes. The governance-productivity relationship operates through multiple mechanisms: fragmentation increases coordination costs for firms operating across boundaries, reduces the scale of infrastructure investments that can be planned and financed, and creates collective action failures where individual councils block developments that would benefit the metropolitan area as a whole.

**Primary owner** — Department of Internal Affairs (DIA) / Local Government portfolio

**MBIE role**
- Provide economic analysis of the metropolitan governance-productivity relationship, including quantification of the fragmentation penalty and the returns to coordination, to inform the design of governance reform.
- Advise on own-source revenue mechanisms for metropolitan authorities — land value capture, targeted rates, development levies — and on the commercial structures that enable long-term infrastructure financing at the metropolitan scale.
- Support workforce and economic development functions within a metropolitan governance framework, ensuring that economic development mandates are clearly assigned and adequately resourced.
- Contribute to the cross-portfolio design of Auckland-specific governance arrangements, given MBIE's role in the productivity and investment settings that metropolitan governance affects.
- Monitor and report on metropolitan economic performance — productivity, housing supply, FDI, infrastructure delivery — to track whether governance arrangements are producing the outcomes they were designed to enable.

**Constraints**
- Political will from both central government and constituent local councils is a prerequisite: metropolitan governance reform typically requires central government legislation and local council cooperation, both of which can be difficult to sustain simultaneously.
- Amalgamation or centralisation can reduce local responsiveness and democratic accountability, creating a legitimacy deficit that undermines the authority's ability to make and implement difficult decisions about development and infrastructure.
- Large authorities can become bureaucratically slow; subsidiarity requires careful assignment of functions, retaining local decision-making for genuinely local issues while centralising decisions with metropolitan spillovers.
- Creating metropolitan authorities in political environments without strong fiscal decentralisation may produce underpowered bodies that have coordination mandates without the fiscal resources to act on them.
- Auckland's specific challenge — central government controlling most fiscal and strategic levers while Auckland Council holds statutory plan-making powers — means metropolitan governance reform must address the central-local relationship as well as the inter-council relationship.

**Related claims**
- claim-0023: Doubling the number of municipalities per 100,000 inhabitants within a metropolitan area is associated with a 5–6% reduction in labour productivity — the cross-city fragmentation penalty that metropolitan governance aims to address (OECD cross-city evidence).
- claim-0024: A metropolitan governance body reduces the fragmentation-productivity penalty by approximately 50%, from around 6% to around 3% — the quantified benefit of institutional reform.
- claim-0056: Doubling municipalities within a metro is associated with 3.2% lower productivity; the absence of any governance body creates approximately a 6% penalty; a governance body reduces this to approximately 3% (Ahrend et al. 2015, five OECD countries).
- claim-0025: Metropolitan areas with governance bodies had 0.28 percentage points higher annual population growth between 2000 and 2010 and declining sprawl; those without governance bodies had increasing sprawl — governance structure shapes both growth performance and urban form.
- claim-0026: Cities with a dedicated transport authority have public transport satisfaction rates 14 percentage points higher than those without — unified governance produces better network outcomes than fragmented agency arrangements.
- claim-0020: Auckland's central-local governance fragmentation produces disjointed planning and crowds out intergenerational decisions — key unresolved decisions including port location, second harbour crossing, and light rail corridor remain stalled due to the absence of a body with authority and resources to resolve them.
- claim-0047: Excessive urban primacy (approximately 15 percentage points above the mean) is associated with approximately 1.5 percentage points lower annual GDP growth — driven by political favouritism rather than city economics, and illustrating why metropolitan governance must be designed to serve economic function rather than political convenience.

## transport-investment: Transport Investment

**Objective** — Transport investment covers public investment in transport infrastructure and services — including urban rail, rapid bus transit, road networks, cycling and walking infrastructure, and the integration of these modes into a coherent network. The lever aims to expand the number of jobs accessible to workers within a given time budget, deepen effective labour markets, and reduce the transport poverty that concentrates disadvantage in poorly connected communities. It also addresses the emissions consequences of car-dependent urban form and the congestion costs — estimated at NZ$0.7–1.3 billion per year for Auckland — that erode productivity and quality of life.

**Mechanism targeted** — Transit investment reduces commute times and expands the number of jobs accessible within a given time budget, deepening effective labour markets and enabling the agglomeration economies that arise when workers and firms can interact efficiently. Complementary density zoning near transit nodes captures the value of improved accessibility, reduces car dependence, and generates the ridership that makes transit investment financially viable. Investment in underserved areas can break spatial poverty traps by connecting residents in peripheral or poorly connected neighbourhoods to labour markets they currently cannot access within a reasonable commute time; Auckland's Māori and Pasifika residents disproportionately experience transport poverty, spending more than is affordable on private transport in the absence of adequate public alternatives. Better connectivity, measured as market potential within 10 kilometres, more than doubles formal-sector employment with each doubling of market potential. An important caveat applies to road investment specifically: new road capacity tends to induce additional driving through induced demand, resulting in few sustained travel speed benefits; congestion pricing combined with new transport options is more effective than road investment alone at improving travel speeds and reducing congestion.

**Primary owner** — Waka Kotahi NZ Transport Agency / Ministry of Transport

**MBIE role**
- Provide economic analysis of the productivity benefits of transport investment, including labour market access modelling and quantification of agglomeration effects from improved connectivity.
- Advise on the commercial structures and funding mechanisms for major transport projects — including public-private partnerships, value capture, and land value uplift capture near new stations.
- Coordinate with housing and infrastructure policy on the complementarity between transit investment and density permissions, ensuring MBIE's contribution to housing policy is informed by transport network geography.
- Support workforce development in the transport construction sector, which faces capacity constraints that affect the cost and pace of infrastructure delivery.
- Contribute to the evidence base on induced demand and congestion pricing — including the fiscal and economic analysis needed to design effective congestion pricing schemes that improve network performance.

**Constraints**
- New road capacity induces additional vehicle kilometres travelled, offsetting congestion relief over time; Auckland's NZ$31 billion transport investment programme for 2021–2031 is forecast to increase congestion delays by 10%, illustrating that road investment without demand management is unlikely to reduce congestion.
- Transit investment can increase land values near stations, displacing low-income residents unless accompanied by affordable housing provisions; the distributional consequences of transport investment must be managed alongside the aggregate productivity benefits.
- Large fixed-cost infrastructure has long payback periods and locks in assumptions about spatial patterns — including land use, employment location, and travel behaviour — that may change substantially over a 30-year asset life.
- Governance authority to plan and fund at the metropolitan scale is a prerequisite: fragmented funding arrangements produce modal and spatial gaps where no single body is accountable for network-level outcomes.
- Land use changes — upzoning near stations — must accompany transit investment to generate demand; transit investment without complementary land use changes can undershoot ridership projections and undermine the business case.

**Related claims**
- claim-0034: City connectivity, measured as market potential within 10 kilometres, more than doubles formal-sector employment with each doubling of market potential — the primary driver of employment growth in developing cities and a mechanism directly addressed by transport investment.
- claim-0040: Bogotá's TransMilenio BRT reduced average travel times by 15 minutes, reached 930,000 people daily, and raised house values by up to 11% near stations — a positive case for integrated transit investment and neighbourhood upgrading.
- claim-0046: Mexico City metro expansion reduced labour market informality by 4 percentage points near new stations; for every $1 invested, real incomes rose 20% relative to a perfectly efficient labour market counterfactual — illustrating the labour market integration benefits of transit investment.
- claim-0017: Auckland road congestion costs approximately NZ$1.3 billion per year (Leung et al. 2017) — the direct economic cost of the problem this lever addresses through improved network performance and demand management.
- claim-0026: Cities with a dedicated transport authority have public transport satisfaction rates 14 percentage points higher than those without — governance structure affects not just planning but the quality of outcomes delivered.
- claim-0109: Auckland average urban travel speeds increased approximately 50% from the 1930s to the early 1990s with car adoption, then declined by approximately one-third by the 2010s due to congestion — eroding accessibility gains that had previously supported housing supply and labour market integration.
- claim-0111: New road capacity induces additional driving with few sustained speed benefits; congestion pricing combined with new transport options is the more effective approach to improving travel speeds and network performance.
- claim-0188: Congestion functions as a negative externality that disproportionately afflicts publicly-provided goods — including transport — where price signals are inefficient or non-existent, providing the conceptual rationale for congestion pricing and demand-management investment (Donovan 2019).

## zoning-and-far: Zoning and Floor Area Ratio (FAR)

**Objective** — Zoning rules specify what activities are permitted on each parcel of land and, through floor area ratio (FAR) or height limits, how much can be built. FAR is the ratio of total floor space to site area; a higher FAR permits denser development. The lever aims to remove the regulatory wedge between construction costs and floor-space prices — a wedge that in central London and Manhattan raises floor-space prices to 2–8 times above construction costs — and to enable the density that supports agglomeration, reduces emissions from car dependence, and improves housing affordability in high-demand locations.

**Mechanism targeted** — Higher FAR permits more floor space per site, increasing the supply of housing and commercial space and placing downward pressure on rents, all else equal. Mixed-use zoning enables walkable neighbourhoods with reduced car dependence, supporting lower per-capita transport emissions and the casual knowledge exchange that dense, mixed environments enable. Simplified permissive zoning near transit nodes concentrates density where accessibility is highest, maximising the return on both transit and housing investment. The consequences of restrictive FAR in supply-constrained cities are illustrated by the Mumbai case: the FSI restriction lowered from 4.5 to 1.33 in 1991 produced average residential space of just 4 square metres per person and left 54% of 16 million residents in slums — demonstrating that where housing demand is strong and FAR is restricted, the result is not restraint of growth but redistribution of growth into informal and substandard forms. Auckland's 1970 downzoning, which reduced average FAR from approximately 1.28 to approximately 0.80 and cut housing capacity by half, set in motion a four-decade affordability crisis not substantially addressed until the 2016 Unitary Plan.

**Primary owner** — Ministry for the Environment (MfE) / Local government (territorial authorities)

**MBIE role**
- Provide economic analysis of the regulatory wedge between construction costs and floor-space prices, quantifying the fiscal and productivity costs of restrictive zoning as part of the evidence base for reform.
- Advise on the relationship between zoning reform and housing supply response, including analysis of where consenting, construction costs, and financing — rather than zoning — are the binding constraints on new development.
- Coordinate with HUD on the complementary settings — development contributions, infrastructure capacity, affordable housing requirements — that determine whether zoning reform translates into additional dwellings or merely into higher land values.
- Monitor the gap between permitted and built density across NZ's major urban areas, to identify where FAR reform is sufficient and where other constraints must be addressed to achieve a supply response.
- Contribute economic and sectoral analysis to national policy statements that set permissive zoning frameworks overriding local restrictive rules, particularly where local political economy incentives would otherwise prevent reform.

**Constraints**
- Without affordable housing requirements or complementary social housing provision, upzoning can raise land values and displace existing low-income residents in the short run, before new supply materialises to reduce prices overall.
- Rapid density changes in established neighbourhoods generate opposition from incumbent residents and homeowners that can stall reform; the political economy of zoning change requires active management.
- FAR changes alone do not guarantee a supply response if construction costs, financing, or consenting processes are the binding constraints — as the gap between permitted and built density in Auckland historically demonstrated, with years elapsing between zoning reform and significant completions.
- Infrastructure capacity — water, transport, and utilities — must accompany FAR liberalisation; upzoning without infrastructure capacity produces consented but unbuilt sites and can create service stress in areas that do develop.
- Governance authority must span the relevant market area: piecemeal upzoning by individual suburbs can be offset by restrictive neighbouring suburbs, shifting development pressure without accommodating it and undermining metropolitan-scale housing market outcomes.

**Related claims**
- claim-0027: Building regulations in central London and Manhattan raise floor-space prices to 2–8 times above construction costs — the regulatory wedge that permissive FAR and zoning reform aims to reduce.
- claim-0037: Mumbai's FSI restriction of 1.33 (reduced from 4.5 in 1991) produced average residential space of 4 square metres per person and a 54% slum share — an extreme case demonstrating that restrictive FAR in supply-constrained cities does not restrain growth but redirects it into informal and substandard housing.
- claim-0041: Doubling per-capita income raises floor space per person by approximately 29%; doubling population with income held constant reduces it by approximately 40% — income, not population, drives the demand for vertical layering; FAR rules must work with income growth to produce the pyramidal development patterns seen in productive cities.
- claim-0066: Restrictive Auckland land use reduced NZ GDP by 0.9–1.8% via spatial misallocation of labour — the national cost of restrictive zoning that this lever directly addresses.
- claim-0108: Auckland's 1970 District Scheme halved housing capacity from approximately 475,000 to approximately 250,000 people — the archetypal downzoning that this lever would reverse, and whose consequences persisted for more than four decades.
- claim-0050: Exclusionary zoning — including minimum lot-size requirements — in developing cities raises formal housing costs until squatter settlements become the cheaper alternative for low-income households; removing unnecessary regulations eliminates the market incentive for squatter settlement formation.
- claim-0088: Auckland is now in the top third globally for new homes per capita after the 2016 Unitary Plan intensification reforms — evidence that zoning reform, when comprehensive and sustained, can deliver rapid and significant improvements in housing supply.
- claim-0190: Preliminary modelling finds that artificially raising rents also lowers wages, so density controls squeeze workers on two fronts simultaneously — higher housing costs and lower wages — implying the welfare cost of restrictive FAR rules is larger than a housing-cost-only analysis suggests (Donovan 2019, preliminary).
