# Place-Based Policy Knowledge Base — Overview

## What this knowledge base is

This knowledge base is a structured collection of evidence-backed claims about urban economics, spatial policy, and productivity — with a focus on New Zealand and Auckland, set in an international comparative context. It is designed for use by AI systems, researchers, and policy analysts who need to reason about urban policy without navigating source documents.

The knowledge base draws on 28 primary sources spanning international meta-analyses, OECD comparative studies, developing-country urban economics research, and New Zealand-specific studies published between 2009 and 2026.

## What it covers

The knowledge base is organised around **185 claims** (IDs claim-0001 through claim-0194, with some gaps) and **9 policy levers**. Claims are atomic, evidence-backed propositions; levers are actionable policy instruments.

### Topic areas

**1. Agglomeration and productivity** — The core finding of urban economics: doubling city population raises wages and output by roughly 2.7–6.4% (median 4.6%) across 295 studies globally. This elasticity varies by sector (services > manufacturing), spatial scope (national > metropolitan), and era (peaked around 2000, declining since). Density simultaneously raises wages, innovation, fiscal efficiency, and lowers emissions. Worker sorting (productive people selecting into cities) explains a large share of apparent productivity premiums. NZ's structural shift from manufacturing (25% of employment in 1976, now under 10%) to services has systematically favoured large cities and is unlikely to be reversed by regional policy; initial industry mix explains only ~10% of cross-city growth differences, with city-specific competitive advantage doing the rest. The Henderson/Duranton model formalises the underlying equilibrium: migration to cities continues while agglomeration benefits (matching, sharing, learning) exceed congestion costs, and the Roback spatial-equilibrium model shows households and firms can be drawn to different locations for different reasons — households toward high-amenity locations, firms toward agglomeration benefits.

**2. Urban form, land markets, and zoning** — How cities grow (horizontal vs. vertical, compact vs. sprawling) shapes long-run productivity and emissions. Building regulations in high-demand cities can raise floor-space prices 2–8x above construction costs. Auckland's 1970s downzoning halved housing capacity and, combined with post-1990 travel-speed decline, explains most of the fourfold increase in house-price sensitivity to population growth. Income growth — not population growth alone — drives vertical urban layering. Preliminary NZ modelling (Donovan 2019) suggests the welfare cost of supply restrictions runs wider than housing-cost analyses capture: artificially raised rents are also associated with lower wages, NZ housing supply faces adjustment frictions ("sticky quantities"), and urban development generates positive regional spillovers rather than simply displacing activity from neighbouring areas.

**3. Transport, connectivity, and accessibility** — Connectivity (measured by economic density accessible within 10km) is the single strongest predictor of formal-sector employment growth in developing cities. Transit investment reduces commute times, expands labour market catchments, and reduces informality near stations. Induced demand means road investment alone cannot sustain travel speed improvements; congestion pricing is more effective. Auckland congestion costs approximately NZ$0.7–1.3 billion per year.

**4. Governance and institutions** — Metropolitan fragmentation is associated with 3–6% lower labour productivity per doubling of municipal count. A metropolitan governance body roughly halves the fragmentation penalty. Auckland's central-local governance fragmentation is the primary structural barrier to long-term spatial coordination. Urban primacy driven by political favouritism reduces national growth; administrative deregulation is the most tractable remedy.

**5. New Zealand productivity context** — NZ's GDP per capita fell from ~110% of the OECD average (1970) to ~79% (2010), driven entirely by lagging labour productivity. Capital stock per worker is ~50% of OECD comparators. Technology currency is declining (firms "fully up to date" fell from 51% in 2007 to 44% in 2022). NZ's housing supply elasticity has declined by one-quarter to one-third since mid-century. Removing all housing constraints would raise GDP by 5–8.4%.

**6. Auckland's performance** — Auckland generates 38% of NZ's GDP but its GDP per hour worked is only ~9% above the national average — far below the 25–35% typical for comparable primate cities. It ranks last among its 10 peer cities on productivity. Its city centre (21% of Auckland GDP) outperforms the rest; FDI inflows are 80%+ below the OECD peer average. Housing supply has dramatically improved post-2016 Unitary Plan.

**7. Demographics and population** — NZ's total fertility rate reached 1.55 (2025), well below the 2.1 replacement rate. Annual population growth slowed from 1.7% to 0.7% in one year. A labour shortage of 250,000 is projected by 2045. Superannuation cost is forecast to rise from 5.1% to 8% of GDP by 2065. Auckland will become a majority non-Pākehā city before the early 2040s. Households and firms do not necessarily sort into the same places for the same reasons: NZ city-level evidence finds Quality of Life (household amenity) and Quality of Business (firm attractiveness) are negatively correlated (-0.57) across NZ cities, with population growth raising QB robustly while its effect on QL has weakened over time — meaning population concentration and economic-activity concentration can diverge spatially.

**8. Skills, mismatch, and human capital** — Across OECD countries, 22% of workers are overqualified and 40% are mismatched by field of study. Overqualification carries a ~14% wage penalty. Skills use at work (not just possession) correlates strongly with GDP per hour at country level. NZ ranks among the highest OECD countries on combined mismatch, partly due to its >25% foreign-born share and imperfect credential recognition.

**9. Developing-country urban economics** — The urban productivity premium from city size holds in Asian and Latin American cities (5–6% per doubling) but is insignificant in African cities once infrastructure quality is controlled for. Leapfrog development (>40% of new built-up area in major African cities 2000–2010) destroys agglomeration potential by fragmenting city form. Early infrastructure sequencing (institutions → infrastructure → targeted interventions) is the universal design principle.

**10. Infrastructure investment and demand forecasting** — NZ spends 5.8% of GDP per year on infrastructure but ranks near the bottom of the OECD on efficiency, with an estimated $104 billion infrastructure gap already accrued and a further $106 billion gap projected over the next 30 years. Te Waihanga's "Forward Guidance" demand model projects spending needs rising past $40 billion/year by the 2050s, around 60% of it renewals of ageing assets ($330 billion in stock, mostly post-1950). Consenting costs alone run to $1.3 billion/year. The 2026 National Infrastructure Plan's 16 recommendations were supported or supported in-principle by the Government, though the Green Party's dissenting response flags an inconsistency between the Plan's renewals-first intent and the GPS on Land Transport's new-capital-heavy funding split.

**11. Immigration and migration economics** — NZ's immigrant population share rose from 15% (1990) to 27% (2019), the second-highest in the OECD, and population growth depends almost entirely on international migration in Auckland, where net internal migration is negative. The Productivity Commission frames migration through "absorptive capacity" — physical and social infrastructure that limits how fast a country can accommodate growth in the short run — and concludes immigration is neither the cause of nor the likely solution to NZ's productivity underperformance, which instead stems from labour-input growth rather than output-per-hour gains. RBNZ sign-restricted SVAR evidence (Javed, Power & Richardson 2026) corroborates this: an expansionary immigration shock lifts GDP, employment, and house prices, but labour productivity shows only a muted, statistically insignificant response — except for older-cohort (30–44) migrants, where productivity gains are significant. Structural concerns include employer-tying under the Accredited Employer Work Visa enabling monopsony power, a low-skill/low-wage labour-market trap from easy access to migrant labour, and an estimated 8% of temporary migrant workers experiencing employment-rights breaches against an under-resourced Labour Inspectorate.

## Key policy themes

**Auckland is underperforming for its size.** At 1.72 million people, Auckland should generate a 25–35% GDP per capita premium over the national average (the international norm for primate cities of its scale). It generates only 13%. The main binding constraints are: dispersed urban form (land use regulation suppressing density), insufficient capital intensity (~50% of OECD comparators), inadequate transport connectivity (congestion eroding agglomeration benefits), governance fragmentation between central and local government, and low FDI (80%+ below peer average).

**Housing supply is the single highest-leverage lever.** Removing all NZ housing constraints is estimated to raise GDP by 5–8.4% and expand the labour force by 3–7%. Auckland's 1970s downzoning and post-1990 travel-speed decline together explain most of the city's housing affordability crisis. The 2016 Auckland Unitary Plan shows that reform works: Auckland now ranks in the top third globally for new homes per capita.

**Capital investment and technology diffusion are co-determinants of productivity.** Capital investment is not just a supply-side input — it embeds newer technology vintages (the "why not both" insight). NZ sits below the OECD median on all four technology diffusion channels: trade openness, FDI stock, clustering/agglomeration quality, and capital/finance access. Treating investment and innovation policy as separate silos misses this complementarity.

**Governance capacity is a precondition for all other levers.** Spatial plans, housing supply reform, and transport investment all require institutional machinery that spans ministries, tiers of government, and multi-decade horizons. NZ's three-year electoral cycle structurally discourages long-cycle investment. Infrastructure investment demand is forecast at 5.8% of GDP per year for 30 years.

**Demographics are a slow-moving constraint that needs to be embedded in spatial planning.** NZ's working-age population will shrink relative to retirees, rural areas are already declining, and Auckland will see rapid ethnic demographic change. Spatial investment allocation — concentrating on growth metros vs. maintaining provincial services — is becoming a strategic rather than technical question.

**Immigration policy should be evaluated against its own objectives, not treated as a productivity lever.** The Productivity Commission and RBNZ SVAR evidence converge on the same conclusion: migration is not the cause of NZ's productivity underperformance and is unlikely to be its solution, since GDP growth has come from more workers and hours rather than higher output per hour. The more useful framing is "absorptive capacity" — whether infrastructure and services can accommodate population growth fast enough — which links immigration policy directly back to the infrastructure-provision lever above.
