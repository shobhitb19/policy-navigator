# evidence-0003

Density-elasticity meta-analysis: citation-weighted recommended elasticities for the economic, environmental, and fiscal effects of urban density, drawn directly from the primary synthesis paper rather than a secondary citation.

Ahlfeldt and Pietrostefani (2019) collected 473 analyses from 268 studies, restricted to 347 quantifiable density-elasticity estimates from 180 studies, and produced citation-weighted "recommended" elasticities for 15 outcome categories (their Table 6). Five categories — wage, rent, vehicle miles travelled, pollution, and average speed — rest on identification strategies the authors judge causally interpretable; the rest, including patent intensity, energy use, and local public spending, are best read as associations. This evidence card supersedes [evidence-0002](evidence-0002.md), which relied on a second-hand, bundled citation in Lall et al. (2021) that — among other compression losses — inverted the sign of the pollution effect.

**Key results (Table 6 recommended elasticities):**

- Wage: +0.04 (doubling density → +4% wages; +8% in non-high-income countries) — causal interpretation justified
- Patent intensity: +0.21 (associative; reflects both learning spillovers and sorting of innovative workers, not decomposed)
- Energy use: −0.07 (associative; doubling density → 7% lower energy consumption)
- Pollution concentration: **+0.13 — an increase, not a decrease** (causal interpretation justified; Carozzi and Roth 2018, confirmed by Borck and Schrauth 2018)
- Local public service unit costs: −0.17 (associative; doubling density → 17% lower unit cost)

**Limitations:**

- Evidence base is predominantly North American and European; non-high-income evidence is thinner and, where reported, sometimes diverges substantially (e.g. wage elasticity roughly doubles).
- Only 5 of 15 categories rest on a strategy the authors consider causally interpretable; the others are citation-weighted associations across heterogeneous studies and methods.
- The patent/innovation elasticity does not separate genuine learning-spillover effects from the sorting of already-innovative workers and firms into dense locations.
- "Density" pools horizontal compactness and vertical building height; the paper does not decompose which margin drives which effect.
