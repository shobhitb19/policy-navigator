# evidence-0001

Agglomeration elasticity: the percentage increase in productivity or wages associated with a doubling of city size (or density). Synthesised from 6,684 estimates drawn from 295 studies covering 54 countries and spanning six decades (1960–2020).

Donovan et al. (2021) conduct the most comprehensive meta-analysis of agglomeration economies to date, using Bayesian mixed effects models that address unobserved heterogeneity, errors-in-outcomes, and extreme values. For their preferred combination of study attributes — wages measured against population density, published panel micro-data, with controls for individual worker and firm effects, own skills, sectoral and occupational composition, and social capital — they find agglomeration elasticities lie in the range 2.7–6.4% with 90% probability, with a median of 4.6%. Estimates at metropolitan spatial scope (populations 0.2m–1m) have a lower median (3.3%) than national-scope estimates (6.4%). More rigorous controls consistently reduce estimates: individual worker effects reduce estimates by approximately 1.1%, housing controls by 3.8%, capital intensity by 2.4%. Manufacturing agglomeration elasticities are approximately 0.6% lower than economy-wide estimates and have declined over the full six-decade period. Overall estimates peaked around 1980–2000 and have since fallen — by approximately 1.5% over two decades.

**Key results:**

- Preferred-specification median elasticity: 4.6%; 90% credible interval 2.7–6.4% (Donovan et al., 2021, section 5)
- Metropolitan spatial scope (pop. 0.2m–1m): median 3.3%, 90% CI 1.5–5.0%
- National spatial scope: median 6.4%, 90% CI 4.6–8.2%
- Manufacturing sector: approximately 0.6% lower than economy-wide estimates
- Individual worker effects control: reduces estimates by approximately 1.1%
- Housing supply/price control: reduces estimates by approximately 3.8%
- Capital intensity control: reduces estimates by approximately 2.4%
- Published studies yield estimates approximately 2.1% lower than working papers
- IV estimation: reduces estimates by approximately 0.3%
- Overall estimates rose 1980–2000, then declined; by 2020 approximately 1.5% below peak
- Manufacturing elasticities declined throughout the entire six-decade period
- Evidence of publication bias in the literature (non-significant results only 19% as likely to be published); correcting for bias does not materially change results
- Models explain only approximately one-quarter to one-third of variation in estimates

**Limitations:**

- Working paper (not peer-reviewed at time of writing); though methods and data are extensively validated
- Only approximately 25–33% of variation in elasticity estimates is explained by the model
- Publication bias exists in the underlying literature; correction does not fully resolve it
- Does not model differences in study quality beyond observable methodological attributes
- Sample restricted to English-language studies; non-English literature excluded
- Focused on production-side agglomeration economies; does not cover agglomeration in consumption
