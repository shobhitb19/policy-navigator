Conference/seminar presentation by Stuart Donovan (VLC) previewing a dynamic extension of the
Rosen-Roback spatial equilibrium model (co-authored with Arthur Grimes and David Maré, funded by
the NZ National Science Challenge "Building Better Homes, Towns and Cities"), estimated on census
data for approximately 130 New Zealand cities and towns over roughly 40 years (eight census
waves). Substantially overlaps in authorship and underlying model with [source-0030](source-0030.md)
(Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan 2018); claims drawn from this source focus on content not already
captured from source-0030 — the conceptual agglomeration-vs-congestion migration framework, and the
preliminary/unpublished findings of the dynamic model extension.

<a id="slide-us-growth-heterogeneity"></a>
## US MSA growth heterogeneity, 2010-2019

> "While ~80% of MSAs in U.S. are growing, 20% are declining" over 2010-2019, with MSA mean population growth of 7.1% and MSA median growth of 4.8%. (Donovan, 2019, slide deck)

<a id="slide-henderson-duranton-model"></a>
## Henderson (1974) / Duranton (2008) agglomeration-vs-congestion migration equilibrium

> Drawing on Henderson (1974) and Duranton (2008): "People migrate to cities as long as (private) agglomeration benefits > congestion costs." Migration equilibrium is reached where the marginal agglomeration benefit of additional population equals the marginal congestion cost. (Donovan, 2019, slide deck)

<a id="slide-agglomeration-sources"></a>
## Three sources of agglomeration economies: Matching, Sharing, Learning

> Agglomeration economies arise from three sources: Matching (a thicker labour force improves worker-firm match quality), Sharing (co-located firms share infrastructure and reduce costs), and Learning (proximity facilitates knowledge spillovers). "Agglomeration economies are relatively heterogeneous, varying by sectors and skills." (Donovan, 2019, slide deck)

<a id="slide-congestion-externality"></a>
## Congestion as a negative externality in publicly-provided goods

> Congestion is described as a "negative externality; often afflicts publicly-provided goods, where price signals are inefficient or non-existent," with transport, education, and recreation given as examples. (Donovan, 2019, slide deck)

<a id="slide-dynamic-roback-extension"></a>
## Dynamic extension of the Rosen-Roback model — research design

> The presentation previews ongoing research (with Grimes and Maré) that adapts the Rosen-Roback model to a dynamic setting, incorporates agglomeration economies in both production and consumption, and allows households to differ by skill level (education), estimated on census data for roughly 130 New Zealand cities and towns over approximately 40 years (eight census waves). (Donovan, 2019, slide deck)

<a id="slide-preliminary-findings"></a>
## Preliminary findings from the dynamic model

> Preliminary findings reported: "Climate explains ~25% of the variation in amenities. Top of the South Island has the most valued climate in New Zealand. Of the major cities, Tauranga and Auckland have the best climates." "Artificially raising rents leads to lower wages. Policies that restrict urban development, e.g. density controls, squeeze workers on two fronts: Higher housing costs and lower wages." Housing supply is affected by adjustment frictions ("sticky quantities"). "Urban development has positive regional spillovers." There are "strong positive agglomeration economies: In production, especially for high-skilled workers; In consumption, for both high and low skilled." (Donovan, 2019, slide deck — preliminary/unpublished findings)

<a id="slide-amenable-city"></a>
## The 'Amenable City' policy framing

> "The old industrial city is dead" — the policy response should "enable agglomeration; manage congestion" and "understand what it means to be an 'amenable' city." The Amenable City "delivers a bundle of amenities that are valued by both households and firms," "acknowledges the challenges created by imperfect (or non-existent) pricing and variation in preferences," and "accepts 'mega-trends', e.g. technology and climate change, and instead focuses on policies that are dynamically efficient. That is, let's do better over time." (Donovan, 2019, slide deck)
