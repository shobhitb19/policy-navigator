Empirical study of how the changing industry composition of the New Zealand economy between
1976 and 2013 has reshaped employment across the country's cities and towns. Uses Census-based
employment data for 30 of the largest urban areas, combining location-quotient classification of
industries, Bartik (1991) shift-share shock analysis, Esteban-Marquillas (1972) decomposition, and
a Regional Specialisation Index to separate the effects of national sectoral change from local
competitiveness. Produced for the New Zealand Productivity Commission's local government funding
and financing inquiry.

<a id="overview-finance-shift"></a>
## Overview: sectoral shift favouring large cities — finance employment example

> "The changing nature of jobs has disproportionately favoured large 'super cities' ... two thirds of the national increase in employment in the finance sector between 1976 and 2013 took place in Auckland, even though Auckland started with just over a third of the financial sector workforce at the start of this period." (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Overview)

<a id="section-1-1-manufacturing-decline"></a>
## Section 1.1: National decline of manufacturing employment 1976-2013

> "In 1976, manufacturing accounted for 25% of jobs in New Zealand ... By 2013 manufacturing accounted for less than 10% of national employment." (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 1.1)

<a id="section-1-2-table-1-2-city-growth"></a>
## Section 1.2 / Table 1-2: Employment growth 1976-2013 by city

> Table 1-2 reports total employment growth between 1976 and 2013 across the 30 largest cities and towns: Auckland +79%, Queenstown +361%, Tauranga +176%, Tokoroa −44%, with total employment growth across all 30 areas of +48%. (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Table 1-2)

<a id="section-2-1-table-2-1-sector-shares"></a>
## Section 2.1 / Table 2-1: National employment shares by sector, 1976 and 2013

> Table 2-1 reports national employment shares by sector in 1976 and 2013: Widely-distributed manufacturing fell from 17.6% to 6.8% of employment (a 61.2% relative decline); regionally-specialised manufacturing fell from 7.7% to 3.0% (a 60.9% relative decline); professional services rose from 2.6% to 9.7% (a 279.2% relative increase); and health and education rose from 12.5% to 19.3% (a 54.1% relative increase). (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Table 2-1)

<a id="section-2-1-wholesale-example"></a>
## Section 2.1: Auckland wholesale sector — actual vs expected growth

> "Based on its share of national wholesale trade employment in 1976, Auckland could have been expected to get 56% of the national increase in wholesale sector employment between 1976 and 2013. In reality, Auckland gained 79% of the national increase ... wholesale sector employment increased 42% faster in Auckland than could have been expected." (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 2.1)

<a id="section-2-3-table-2-6-excess-churn"></a>
## Section 2.3 / Table 2-6: Excess churn regression results

> "Not only do slowly growing cities have higher excess churn than other cities, but small cities have higher excess churn than large cities." Regression of 65-industry excess churn on ln(city growth) and ln(city size) yields a coefficient of −0.27 on ln(city growth) and −0.017 on ln(city size), R² = 0.80, N = 30. (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Table 2-6)

<a id="section-3-1-bartik-manufacturing"></a>
## Section 3.1: Bartik shock results — regionally-specialised vs widely-distributed manufacturing

> "Total employment reduced by 36 jobs if the shock reduced a regionally specialised manufacturing industry, but total employment increased by 60 jobs if the shock hit a widely distributed manufacturing industry" — figures are per 100-job own-sector shock, with cross-sector offset coefficients of −1.48 for widely-distributed manufacturing shocks and −0.60 for regionally-specialised manufacturing shocks. (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 3.1)

<a id="section-3-2-primary-sector-shock"></a>
## Section 3.2: Bartik shock results — primary sector

> Own-shock response for the primary sector is β = 0.63 (95% CI 0.4–0.8): "if a city is forecast to lose 100 jobs due to a primary sector shock, our estimates suggest it loses 108 jobs of which 63 are in the primary sector and a further 45 in other sectors" — unlike widely-distributed manufacturing, there is no compensating expansion elsewhere in the local economy. (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 3.2)

<a id="section-3-3-esteban-marquillas"></a>
## Section 3.3: Esteban-Marquillas decomposition — initial industry mix vs growth

> Applying the Esteban-Marquillas (1972) shift-share decomposition, the authors find that "differences in the initial mix of industries do not explain much of the differences in cross-city growth rates, only about 10%." (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 3.3)

<a id="section-4-regional-specialisation"></a>
## Section 4: Regional Specialisation Index trends

> "Regional specialization declined over time, with 23 of the 30 urban areas becoming more diversified between 1976 and 2013." The fastest-growing areas (e.g. Nelson, Tauranga) became more diversified rather than more specialised, with their Regional Specialisation Index declining by 0.10 points on average compared with 0.04 points for slower-growing areas. (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 4)

<a id="section-5-conclusion"></a>
## Section 5: Conclusion and policy implications

> The decline of manufacturing and the growth of services has disproportionately favoured large cities through stronger agglomeration benefits, a shift the authors describe as "unlikely to be overcome by regional interventions." Recovery speed from negative shocks depends on industry type, with rural-processing manufacturing harder to recover from than other manufacturing. "Government programmes aimed at enhancing the way non-tradeable businesses improve their productivity are likely to produce the widest regional benefits." (Coleman, Maré & Zheng, 2019, Section 5)
