Applies the Roback (1982, 1988) spatial equilibrium model to estimate Quality of Life (QL) and
Quality of Business (QB) indices for 130 New Zealand settlements across eight census waves
(1976-2013), following the city/location fixed-effects estimation approach of Maré and Poot
(2018). QL and QB are derived from quality-adjusted wage and rent differentials across locations,
under the assumption that in equilibrium households and firms are indifferent between locations
once amenity and disamenity values are capitalised into wages and rents.

<a id="abstract"></a>
## Abstract

> "Places that are attractive to live in tend to be sunny, dry and near water ... Since the mid-1990s, attractive places have also had relatively high shares of the workforce engaged in education and (to a lesser extent) health ... The quality of business is highest in larger cities." (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Abstract)

<a id="section-2-roback-model"></a>
## Section 2: Roback spatial-equilibrium model — Quality of Life and Quality of Business derivation

> Quality of Life is derived as QL_ct = α·ln(r_ct) − ln(w_ct), with α = 0.2; Quality of Business is derived as QB_ct = [γ/(1−γ)]·ln(r_ct) + ln(w_ct), with γ = 0.11, following Maré and Poot (2018). Both indices are estimated via city/location fixed-effects regressions on quality-adjusted wages (w) and rents (r). (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Section 2)

<a id="section-3-ql-qb-correlation"></a>
## Section 3 / Figure 3: Negative correlation between QL and QB across NZ cities

> "QL and QB for New Zealand cities are substantially negatively correlated" — the correlation coefficient is −0.57 for the whole period. "Only Christchurch, Tauranga and Queenstown achieve [above-average QL and QB] in all periods, and only 18% of settlement-year observations do so overall." (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Section 3, Figure 3 note)

<a id="table-2-2013-rankings"></a>
## Table 2: City rankings on QL and QB, 2013

> Top QL in 2013: Whitianga (2.40), Motueka (2.24), Coromandel (1.94), Queenstown (1.85), Katikati Community (1.75). Bottom QL: Tokoroa (−2.20), Hawera (−2.11), Te Kauwhata (−2.06), Eltham (−2.04), Winton (−1.60). Top QB: Rolleston (2.46), Waiuku (2.31), Auckland (2.28), Te Kauwhata (2.14), Wellington (2.05). Bottom QB: Opotiki (−2.06), Taumarunui (−1.98), Moerewa (−1.85), Murupara (−1.80), Waipawa (−1.68). (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Table 2)

<a id="table-3-ql-climate-education"></a>
## Table 3: QL regressions — climate, water, education, health, population

> With city fixed effects, 1996-2013: "a city with one SD higher rainfall can be expected to have 0.27 SD lower QL ... a city with one SD more hours of sunshine per month can be expected to have 0.21 SD greater QL" (both significant at 5%). "Cities by the water can be expected to have a higher QL measure by about 0.36 SD in both time periods" (significant at 5%). There is "no relationship between education and QL in the first period [1976-1991] but a positive and highly significant relationship in the second period [1996-2013]: a one SD increase in education is associated with a 0.27 SD increase in QL"; health is weakly significant (0.162*) only in the second period. Without city fixed effects, the accommodation/food/recreation industry share is strongly positively associated with QL (0.255** first period, 0.449*** second period), but this relationship disappears with city fixed effects (0.062, 0.059, not significant) — "places that are nice to live in are generally rich in these amenities, but intensifying those industries in any given city does not necessarily improve QL." Population growth of 10% was associated with a 0.09 SD decrease in QL in 1976-1991 (with city fixed effects), a relationship that is weaker and less precisely estimated in 1996-2013. (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Table 3)

<a id="table-4-qb-population-education"></a>
## Table 4: QB regressions — population, education, health

> With city fixed effects: "experiencing a 10% increase in population is associated with an increase of QB by approximately 0.1 SD in both periods, with statistical significance at the 1% level" (coefficients 1.374*** and 1.067***). In 1996-2013, education and health workforce shares are negatively associated with QB (−0.427*** and −0.371*** respectively), possibly reflecting government siting of education and health facilities in otherwise less business-friendly locations. (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Table 4)

<a id="table-5-qb-population-correlation"></a>
## Table 5: Raw correlation of QB and population across census waves

> The raw correlation of QB with ln(population) remains strong (approximately 0.76-0.83, weighted, including large cities) across all census waves from 1976 to 2013, weakening over time only once large cities are excluded from the sample. (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Table 5)

<a id="section-6-discussion"></a>
## Section 6: Discussion — divergent household and firm location preferences

> "Households and firms prefer different amenities and hence prefer different locations ... households appear to prefer sunny, dry locations near water, while firms appear to prefer to locate in larger cities." The authors note QB may be less reliably measured than QL, since the model uses residential rents as a proxy for commercial rents. (Preston, Maré, Grimes & Donovan, 2018, Section 6)
