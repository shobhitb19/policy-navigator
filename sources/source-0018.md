# Research on Housing as an Enabler of Economic Growth and Productivity

Ministry of Housing and Urban Development. (2024). *Research on housing as an enabler of economic growth and productivity* (Aide-memoire to the Minister, HUD2024-003621, 8 March 2024). New Zealand Government.

<a id="executive-summary"></a>
## Executive Summary

The aide-memoire synthesises research on four mechanisms by which housing conditions affect economic performance: (A) labour allocation, (B) agglomeration economies, (C) human capital accumulation, and (D/E) capital allocation. The key framing is that housing supply constraints prevent efficient sorting of workers and firms across space, suppressing productivity through each mechanism.

The document cites Nunns (2021) as the primary quantitative estimate for the full-reform scenario:

> "Nunns (2021) estimated that removing all housing supply constraints in New Zealand would result in output per worker, which is a proxy for labour productivity, being about 1.1% to 1.6% higher; the NZ labour force would become 3.3% to 7.2% larger; and total economic output (GDP) would increase between 5.0% to 8.4%." (MHuD, 2024, para. 5)

<a id="section-a-labour-allocation"></a>
## Section A: Labour Allocation

Housing costs in high-productivity locations prevent workers from relocating to where they would be most productive. This is the primary mechanism in the Nunns (2021) modelling. The aid-memoire distinguishes this from the Nunns (2019) estimate cited in claim-0066 — the 2021 estimate covers all NZ constraints, not just Auckland zoning.

<a id="section-b-agglomeration"></a>
## Section B: Agglomeration Economies

> "Donovan et al. (2022) estimate the average NZ agglomeration elasticity as approximately 0.05, suggesting that doubling city population increases output per worker by about 3.5%." (MHuD, 2024, para. 28)

> "Donovan et al. (2024) find that agglomeration benefits have reduced since the 1980s and 1990s." (MHuD, 2024, para. 28)

Table 1 (para. 30):

> "Auckland's agglomeration elasticity is approximately 0.04." (MHuD, 2024, Table 1)

> "Auckland is estimated to be 5–8% more productive than Hamilton, Tauranga, Wellington or Christchurch due to its size alone." (MHuD, 2024, para. 30)

> "Given Australia's major metros of Sydney and Melbourne are about three times the size of Auckland, then the lack of urban agglomeration might explain about a third of the 20% productivity gap with Australia." (MHuD, 2024, para. 31)

<a id="section-c-human-capital"></a>
## Section C: Human Capital Accumulation

Agglomeration facilitates knowledge spillovers and skill formation. Cities increase workers' human capital over time through learning-by-doing in dense labour markets. Housing constraints limit workers' access to these thick labour markets, particularly for lower-income households who face the steepest affordability barriers.

<a id="section-e-capital-allocation"></a>
## Section E: Capital Allocation and Wealth Effects

> "New Zealand's housing stock is valued at approximately $1.6 trillion, approximately four times GDP. This compares with a ratio of approximately two times GDP in the 1990s." (MHuD, 2024, para. 39)

High and rising housing wealth crowds out investment in productive capital (plant, equipment, R&D) by:
- Directing household saving into land rather than productive assets
- Raising the required return on productive investment (opportunity cost)
- Concentrating wealth among existing homeowners, reducing consumption by asset-poor younger households

NZIER (2017) — referenced in section E — estimated Auckland congestion costs exceed NZ$1 billion per year and more than 1% of regional GDP.
