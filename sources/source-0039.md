Final report of the Productivity Commission's inquiry into maximising the economic contribution
of New Zealand's frontier firms — the most productive firms in the domestic economy within their
industry. The core empirical work benchmarks New Zealand's frontier firms against those of five
comparator European small advanced economies (SAEs: Belgium, Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands,
Sweden) using CompNet firm microdata for 2003–16, in joint research with the National University
of Singapore (Zheng et al., 2021). The report's central finding is that New Zealand's frontier
firms operate at less than half the labour productivity of the SAE frontier. Policy chapters
argue for a "focused innovation policy" — deliberately concentrating government innovation
investment on a small number of areas with critical mass, in contrast to New Zealand's history
of thinly spread, small-scale initiatives. The report is an official statutory inquiry with
substantial commissioned econometric research; the benchmarking findings are empirical, while
the policy chapters are the Commission's judgement informed by SAE case studies.

<a id="overview-key-findings"></a>
## Overview: headline findings

> "A striking finding of this inquiry is that the labour productivity of New Zealand's frontier firms is less than half (45%) that of the best frontier firms in other small advanced economies (SAEs)." (p. 1)

> "For the last 25 years or more New Zealand's income per person has stayed at about 70% of the average of that in countries in the top half of the OECD." (p. 1)

The Overview sets out the inquiry's argument: successful SAEs export specialised, distinctive
goods and services at scale; innovation requires scale to recover its fixed costs; and New
Zealand's small domestic market and distance make attaining scale harder. SAEs employ three
complementary strategies — attracting high-quality FDI, supporting individual companies with
the fixed costs of innovation and exporting, and investing in building innovation ecosystems
around frontier firms in selected focus areas. New Zealand does the first two, with varying
success, but "does not, to the same degree, intentionally focus investment on a few innovation
ecosystems" (p. 4).

<a id="chapter-2-capital-shallowness"></a>
## Chapter 2: Capital shallowness (Figure 2.2, F2.3)

> "New Zealand businesses are typically capital-shallow (ie, workers have limited equipment and other capital goods to work with) and this has held down labour productivity. Historically, this has been partly explained by the high off-the-shelf cost of capital goods, past periods of high long-term real interest rates, and fast population growth. Low returns to investment, low wages and ready access to low-cost immigrant labour are also contributing factors." (F2.3, p. 25)

The chapter also notes that ready availability of labour at modest or low wages, including
through immigration policies allowing high levels of low-skill migration, "has reduced firms'
incentives to invest in labour-saving and productivity-enhancing equipment" (p. 25).

<a id="chapter-2-box-2-1-international-performance"></a>
## Chapter 2 / Box 2.1: New Zealand's international performance

Current-state indicators of New Zealand's international connections (pp. 26–27):

- **Low trade intensity**: exports 28% of GDP vs an SAE-group average of 59% (excluding hub economies Hong Kong, Singapore, Ireland)
- **Flat export growth**: export share of GDP unchanged since the early 1980s, in contrast to rising shares in most advanced economies
- **Exports lack complexity**: Economic Complexity Index ranking fell in the 10 years to 2018
- **Low GVC participation**: low on both forward and backward participation measures
- **Weak ODI**: outward direct investment 8% of GDP vs SAE average of 84% (Skilling, 2020)
- **Falling, low-quality FDI inflows**: FDI stock growth below OECD average; historic FDI mostly domestic-market-seeking rather than export-oriented

<a id="chapter-2-economic-complexity"></a>
## Chapter 2 / Figure 2.5: Economic complexity

> "New Zealand's economic complexity ranking has fallen over recent years. It has fallen more than other SAEs (while larger developing countries such as China, India and even Vietnam have overtaken New Zealand)." (pp. 30–31, citing Observatory of Economic Complexity, 2020)

<a id="chapter-3-benchmarking-frontiers"></a>
## Chapter 3: Benchmarking the New Zealand frontier (Figures 3.5–3.6, F3.1–F3.2)

> "Not only did the top firms in New Zealand have the largest productivity gap relative to the small-advanced-economy frontier, but that this gap increased over time. The New Zealand frontier declined from 48% to 45% of the small-advanced-economy frontier over 2003–16." (p. 40)

The lag exists across all nine broad industries studied. Over 2003–16 the New Zealand frontier's
labour productivity grew at 0.6% a year — similar to the Netherlands (0.7%), faster than Denmark
(-0.5%) and Finland (-2.4%), slower than Sweden (2.7%) and Belgium (5.2%) — meaning New Zealand's
frontier was not catching up despite its much lower starting level (F3.2, p. 42).

<a id="chapter-3-productivity-distribution"></a>
## Chapter 3: Width of the productivity distribution (Figure 3.7, F3.3)

> "New Zealand generally had the lowest gaps between the performance of frontier and non-frontier firms. The productivity distribution in New Zealand thus appears relatively narrow: the ratio of 90th-percentile to 10th-percentile productivity sits at around 5." (p. 43)

The distribution was broadly stable over 2003–16 (10th percentile growth 0.6%/yr; 90th
percentile 0.4%/yr), while in the European SAEs frontier firms pulled away (90th percentile
+1.7%/yr; 10th percentile −1.1%/yr). The Commission cautions that the narrow distribution may
reflect a low, slow-moving frontier that laggard firms find easy to keep up with, rather than
effective diffusion; other studies (Papa et al., 2018, on 2011 data) estimate New Zealand's
90:10 ratio at 6.3 (manufacturing) and 8.1 (services), within the SAE range (F3.3, p. 45).

<a id="chapter-3-diffusion-domestic-frontier"></a>
## Chapter 3: Diffusion comes only from the domestic frontier (F3.4)

> "Technology diffusion to non-frontier firms mainly comes from firms at the national frontier (in the same country). Yet non-frontier firms in the European countries in the Commission's small-advanced-economy study benefited from technology diffusion from firms at the small-advanced-economies frontier (albeit to a lesser extent than from their national frontier firms). This did not happen in New Zealand over 2003–16. This result likely reflects New Zealand's distant location, and that diffusion of tacit and non-codified technologies is difficult over distance." (F3.4, p. 45)

<a id="chapter-3-resource-allocation"></a>
## Chapter 3: Allocation of resources across productivity deciles (Figures 3.8–3.9)

> "While the allocation of labour is very similar in New Zealand and the European SAEs, a higher proportion of capital is allocated to the top two deciles in the European SAEs, and a smaller proportion to the lower deciles, than in New Zealand." (p. 46, MFP deciles)

> "In the European SAEs, more labour is employed in the top labour-productivity decile than in any other. In New Zealand, this happens in the 5th decile. This contrast in the allocation of labour is a concern, as is the much lower average capital intensity compared with the European firms." (p. 55)

<a id="chapter-6-innovation-ecosystems"></a>
## Chapter 6: Innovation ecosystems

Successful SAEs invest in building "innovation ecosystems" around their frontier firms in
selected focus areas (pp. 3–4, 92–97):

> "These ecosystems are made up of entities, their capabilities, and the networks between them. Firms are at the centre of the ecosystem, including larger 'anchor' firms providing 'canopy cover' for small and medium enterprise (SMEs) and entrepreneurs. The ecosystem also includes workers with the right skills, international links, research bodies, education and training providers, mentors and investors with deep knowledge and understanding of the industry, and enabling infrastructure and regulations." (p. 4)

The Commission finds New Zealand's innovation ecosystems "are not currently working well for
actual and potential frontier firms": links between firms and public research institutions are
mostly poor, and research is too focused on science excellence rather than impact (p. 4).

<a id="chapter-7-focused-innovation-policy"></a>
## Chapter 7: Focused innovation policy

> "A small economy has only a limited number of areas that can get to critical mass and support sustained world-class competitive performance. As a complement to broad-based innovation policies (which benefit all firms), finite government resources also need to be deliberately focused on a small number of high-potential areas rather than being thinly spread in what David Skilling terms 'sub-therapeutic doses'." (p. 5)

Most small advanced economies employ focused innovation policies (section 7.4). New Zealand,
by contrast, "has a history of small-scale, sector-focused initiatives that often fade away
without any clear idea of what they have achieved" (p. 5). Focus areas should reflect existing
and emerging strengths, may span standard industry classifications, and should be chosen
collaboratively with industry rather than top-down (p. 5).
