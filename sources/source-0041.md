Working paper (later published in *New Zealand Economic Papers*) exploring the causes and
economic consequences of rising and diverging regional house prices in New Zealand. Nunns
decomposes regional house prices into building value, hedonic land value, and a "distortion"
component — the wedge between prices and underlying supply costs attributable to binding
supply constraints — then calibrates a spatial equilibrium model on regional data for 2000–16
to run counterfactual scenarios in which distortions had not grown. The distinctive feature of
the model is its inclusion of trans-Tasman migration: workers priced out of high-productivity
New Zealand regions can move to Australia, not just to other New Zealand regions.

<a id="abstract"></a>
## Abstract: headline findings

> "Recent house price increases in New Zealand are due in large part to rising house price distortions, which reflect 'wedges' between house prices and underlying costs of supply. These distortions are largest in Auckland, Queenstown, Tauranga, Hamilton, and Wellington." (p. iii)

> "My 'upper bound' estimate is that comprehensively removing constraints to housing supply would have increased New Zealand's total economic output by up to 7.7%, increased per-worker output by 0.9%, and eliminated recent net migration outflows to Australia. More plausible counterfactual scenarios would result in smaller, but still economically meaningful, gains on the order of one to five percent of gross domestic product." (p. iii)

<a id="section-4-migration-shocks"></a>
## Section 4: Migration shocks and price responses

> "A migration shock equal to one percent of regional population leads to a 1.6% to 2.7% increase in local house prices … A similarly-sized migration shock leads to a 1.5% to 2.1% increase in local rents." (p. 30)

Regions with larger house price distortions (indicating binding supply constraints) appear to
have experienced larger increases in house prices and rents in response to migration shocks.

<a id="section-5-counterfactual-scenarios"></a>
## Section 5: Counterfactual scenarios (Table 4)

Three scenarios for 2000–16, each run under perfect and imperfect labour mobility:

- **Scenario 1 — reduce Auckland supply constraints** (halve the growth of Auckland land price distortions): output per worker +0.4–0.5%; **total GDP +0.9–1.8%**; Auckland house prices ~$816,700 rather than the observed ~$999,000.
- **Scenario 2 — reduce constraints nationally** (no further increase in distortions after 2000): output per worker +0.6–0.9%; total GDP +3.0–5.3%. Delivers roughly two-thirds of Scenario 3's benefits, implying most of the economic costs of supply constraints arose since 2000.
- **Scenario 3 — reduce distortions to zero** (aspirational upper bound): output per worker +0.9–1.4%; **total GDP +4.5–7.7%**; net trans-Tasman migration ceases (perfect mobility) or halves (imperfect mobility).

Ranges span the imperfect-mobility (lower) and perfect-mobility (upper) model specifications.

<a id="section-5-trans-tasman-channel"></a>
## Section 5: The trans-Tasman channel dominates

> "A comparison with the above results suggests that most of the estimated economic impacts arise from a reduction in net migration of workers from New Zealand to Australia, rather than reallocation of labour from low-productivity regions to high-productivity regions within New Zealand." (p. 38)

A model variant excluding trans-Tasman migration finds Scenario 3 would lift output by only
0.4–0.8% — the internal-reallocation effect alone is small relative to the retention effect.
