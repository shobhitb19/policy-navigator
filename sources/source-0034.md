## Section 2: Why knowledge spillovers are geographically bounded

The chapter argues that knowledge spillovers are inherently localised because the most economically valuable knowledge is tacit — embodied in people, practices, and relationships rather than codified in documents or patents. Tacit knowledge transfers most effectively through face-to-face interaction, shared workplaces, and repeated personal contact. These mechanisms are spatially constrained: unlike goods or codified information, tacit knowledge cannot be transmitted at zero marginal cost over distance. The implication is that proximity is a prerequisite for accessing the most productive knowledge externalities, and that knowledge spillovers attenuate sharply with distance. → [claim-0203](../claims/claim-0203.md)

<a id="section-3-clustering-evidence"></a>
## Section 3: Evidence on innovative activity clustering

Industries where knowledge spillovers are important — proxied by R&D intensity, university research concentration, and the share of highly educated workers — exhibit significantly higher geographic concentration of innovative output (patents, new product introductions) than industries producing standard goods. Audretsch and Feldman's own earlier work (Audretsch and Feldman 1996) found that industries with higher R&D intensity, a greater share of scientists and engineers, and proximity to university research agglomerate their innovative activity more than production activity, controlling for overall industry concentration. This relationship is robust across technology fields and time periods. → [claim-0204](../claims/claim-0204.md)

<a id="section-4-tacit-knowledge"></a>
## Section 4: Tacit knowledge, proximity, and policy implications

The chapter discusses the policy implications: because knowledge spillovers are geographically bounded, policies that concentrate R&D, universities, and skilled workers in fewer, denser locations may generate stronger national innovation outcomes than those that disperse investment geographically. The "geography of innovation" is therefore not simply a reflection of where inputs are located — it is actively shaped by how proximity facilitates knowledge exchange. The chapter reviews evidence from science parks, university-industry linkages, and cluster policies, concluding that proximity to knowledge sources is a consistent predictor of innovative performance at the firm and regional level. → [claim-0203](../claims/claim-0203.md), [claim-0204](../claims/claim-0204.md)
