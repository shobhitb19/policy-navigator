<a id="section-3-two-step-estimation"></a>
## Section 3: Two-step estimation strategy

The paper uses a two-step procedure to separate agglomeration effects from worker sorting. In step 1, individual wage equations are estimated using census microdata with person fixed effects, isolating the city wage premium from individual productivity differences (which would reflect sorting). In step 2, city-level average wage premiums — net of sorting — are regressed on city characteristics including population-weighted effective density and access to markets. This approach gives a cleaner estimate of genuine agglomeration economies than single-step OLS regressions, which confound sorting and agglomeration. The method follows the approach of Combes et al. (2008) and is the most rigorous technique available with the NZ data. → [claim-0207](../claims/claim-0207.md)

<a id="section-4-aggregate-results"></a>
## Section 4: Aggregate agglomeration elasticities

The paper finds positive agglomeration elasticities for New Zealand over the 1976–2018 period, but with wide variation over time and across cities. Aggregate agglomeration elasticities — the percentage wage increase from a doubling of effective density — are broadly consistent with the lower end of the international literature (approximately 2–4%), but are lower and more variable than the global meta-analytic range, consistent with the finding that small, open, trade-exposed economies face different agglomeration dynamics than large continental economies. → [claim-0207](../claims/claim-0207.md)

<a id="section-5-forking-paths"></a>
## Section 5: Diverging trajectories

The central finding of "forking paths" refers to the divergence in agglomeration dynamics between Auckland and other New Zealand urban areas since the 1984–1991 economic reforms. Auckland's agglomeration elasticities have strengthened over time, consistent with its growing role as a hub for knowledge-intensive services, professional services, and international trade. Meanwhile, other New Zealand cities — particularly those historically specialising in manufacturing and primary-sector processing — have seen weaker or declining agglomeration elasticities, reflecting structural change and the relative contraction of agglomeration-intensive industries outside Auckland. This divergence has spatial policy implications: the returns to urban investment are increasingly concentrated in Auckland, and regional cities face a compounding disadvantage as agglomeration effects weaken. → [claim-0208](../claims/claim-0208.md)
