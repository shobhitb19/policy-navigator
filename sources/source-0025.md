<a id="executive-summary"></a>
## Executive summary

New Zealand spends heavily on infrastructure but gets comparatively poor value for that spending, and faces a large and growing renewals burden as post-war infrastructure reaches the end of its usable life.

> "We spend a lot on infrastructure, but we don't always get good value. New Zealand invested around 5.8% of gross domestic product (GDP) annually on infrastructure over the past 20 years, making us one of the top spending countries in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). Yet we rank towards the bottom for efficiency, or 'bang for buck'... Consenting alone costs infrastructure projects $1.3 billion each year." (Te Waihanga, 2026, Executive summary)

<a id="ten-priorities"></a>
## Ten priorities for the decade ahead

The Commission identifies ten priority areas requiring attention over the next decade, derived from its "Forward Guidance" demand-forecasting method, spanning hospitals, water, road pricing, land transport sequencing, asset management on the downside, maintenance and renewals, flood risk, resource management, upzoning around transport corridors, and electrification.

> "Using our Forward Guidance – a method for forecasting infrastructure demand over time – the Commission has identified 10 priority areas requiring attention over the next decade... 6. Prioritise adequate maintenance and renewals: Central government agencies must prioritise adequate funding to prevent asset deterioration and costly reactive fixes... 9. Commit to upzoning around key transport corridors: This will lead to more efficient use of water and other networks and maximise the value of transport infrastructure investments." (Te Waihanga, 2026, Ten priorities for the decade ahead)

<a id="maintenance-and-renewals-funding-gap"></a>
## Maintenance and renewals as the priority for the decade ahead

The Plan's Forward Guidance projects infrastructure spending roughly doubling by the 2050s, with the majority needing to go toward renewing existing assets rather than new builds, against a backdrop of more than $330 billion of existing infrastructure assets — most built after 1950 — now reaching end of life.

> "Forward Guidance: We expect spending on infrastructure to go from just over $20 billion per year to more than $40 billion per year by the 2050s. Around 60% of this should go towards renewing and replacing what we already have. For sectors and areas with little demand growth, the figure could be even higher... Previous generations have left us with more than $330 billion worth of infrastructure, most of which was built after 1950. With the first big wave of post-war buildings and networks now reaching the end of their usable lives, we need to invest wisely to ensure we're handing over rebuilt and well-maintained assets, not burdens, to future generations of New Zealanders." (Te Waihanga, 2026, Section 4.2, "Prioritise adequate maintenance and renewals")

<a id="resource-management-reform"></a>
## Resource management framework reform

The Plan calls for a stable, durable resource management legislative framework with integrated spatial planning, noting the RMA's track record on cost, delay, and poor coordination between land use and infrastructure provision.

> "There is widespread concern that the RMA isn't adequately supporting community development aspirations or protecting the environment... In practice, the Act has led to high costs and long delays for consenting much-needed housing and infrastructure projects, as well as environmental failures and inconsistent engagement... This lack of coordination between land use and infrastructure provision has exacerbated housing affordability challenges and increased the overall cost of delivering infrastructure." (Te Waihanga, 2026, Section 6.1)

<a id="land-transport-funding"></a>
## Fixing land transport funding: time-of-use charging

The Plan recommends implementing time-of-use charging and fleetwide road user charges to address worsening urban congestion more cost-effectively than adding new road capacity.

> "Previous modelling for Auckland suggests time-of-use charging could cut excess delay by around 35% and deliver equivalent network performance with roughly 20% less new capital investment... Current road pricing tools – fuel excise duty (FED) and road user charges (RUC) – do not reflect the localised nature of congestion because they are set nationally." (Te Waihanga, 2026, Section 3.3, "Implement time-of-use charging and fleetwide road user charges")

<a id="upzoning-transport-corridors"></a>
## Upzoning around key transport corridors

The Plan argues that enabling more housing near existing or planned transport infrastructure capacity (such as Auckland's City Rail Link) increases the return on that infrastructure investment, drawing on Australian evidence that dispersed-city growth costs substantially more to service than compact-city growth.

> "In Australia, research from Infrastructure Victoria found more consolidated, compact cities had stronger economies and more affordable infrastructure. Their modelling suggested that the infrastructure required to service each home in a more dispersed city cost AD$59,000 (around NZD$68,000) more than in a compact one." (Te Waihanga, 2026, Section 6.1, "Commit to upzoning around key transport corridors")

<a id="consenting-costs"></a>
## Consenting costs and regulatory complexity

Infrastructure consenting in New Zealand is costly and has become more so over time, compounded by a fragmented zoning landscape relative to international peers.

> "Infrastructure providers spend around $1.3 billion each year on consenting. International comparisons indicate New Zealand may be near the upper end for regulatory approval costs. A typical infrastructure project requires a firm to spend, on average, 5.5% of their total budget seeking a resource consent. For smaller projects worth less than $200,000, the figure is more like 16%... We have 1,175 land-use zones across 67 territorial authorities. Japan – which has more than 20 times the population of New Zealand – has 13." (Te Waihanga, 2026, Section 6.1 / Executive summary)
