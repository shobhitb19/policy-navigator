Treasury's internal narrative on Auckland's economic role, developed 2016–17 through
engagement with Auckland agencies. The document anticipates several arguments of the
current debate: Auckland's productivity underperformance relative to its scale, the
absorption of growth benefits by housing and transport pressure, and — most distinctively —
the spatial concentration of New Zealand's national productivity frontier in Auckland,
based on Zheng's firm-level analysis for the Productivity Commission.

<a id="on-a-page"></a>
## On a page: the developing story

*Summarised below rather than quoted — the source is an in-confidence draft (see the banner
above). Page references are retained so the analysis can be traced if the document is ever
released or obtained through official channels.*

The document opens by placing Auckland at 37 percent of GDP and as the location of most of
New Zealand's national frontier firms, and projects that in thirty years it will hold 40
percent of the population and account for 90 percent of working-age population growth (p. 4).

It then states the core diagnosis: from a productivity perspective Auckland is not performing
as well as its size and international peer group would lead you to expect, and although
Auckland is more productive than the rest of New Zealand, that premium had been shrinking
over time (p. 4). The benefits associated with city scale were, on Treasury's assessment,
being outweighed by the negative impacts of growth, with environmental, housing and transport
indicators all showing a city under increasing pressure (p. 4).

The recommended "twin focus": (1) address the negative consequences of growth — housing
affordability and transport congestion; (2) deliver on Auckland's inherent opportunities —
global connections, diversity, knowledge-intensive service exports, and labour market
outcomes for young people, especially Māori and Pasifika.

The section closes by warning that without a better understanding of how Auckland's urban
form contributes to productivity, planning instruments and infrastructure investments risk
"hard wiring" the city in a form that does not deliver the productive Auckland New Zealand
needs (pp. 4–5).

<a id="national-frontier-firms"></a>
## National frontier firms

The document reports that most of New Zealand's national frontier firms are located in
Auckland, describing them as the most productive firms in each industry, noting that they
frequently engage with high-productivity firms in international markets, and identifying them
as the principal route by which new technology and knowledge spread through the wider economy
(p. 40, citing Zheng (2016); frontier defined as firms above the weighted 90th percentile of
firm-level MFP in each industry, LBD data).

**The underlying analysis is publicly available.** These findings relay Zheng (2016), held as
[source-0047](source-0047.md), whose precise statistics are that the three major cities hold
48% of firms and 63% of employment but 52% of national-frontier firms and 73% of
frontier-firm employment. Cite Zheng directly — see [claim-0240](../claims/claim-0240.md).

Related figures reported: Auckland's share of jobs in advanced industries (11.3%) exceeds the
average US city (8.7%); firms at the national frontier grow productivity at 0.77% per year on
average, almost 1.6 times faster than firms at the local frontier.

<a id="growth-pressures"></a>
## Growth pressures

Indicators cited in the document (p. 4): Auckland added 174,000 people — a city the size of
Hamilton — between the 2010 amalgamation and 2017, with infrastructure including housing not
keeping pace; New Zealand had the largest increase in house prices relative to incomes in the
OECD since 2010; congestion on the arterial network was increasing 2.5 percent a year; 45
percent of Auckland's population increase was due to migration; and over $80 billion of
transport investment had been identified for the following 30 years.
