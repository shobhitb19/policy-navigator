Early Treasury survey of economic geography, framing location outcomes as the balance of
exogenous forces (natural geography, history, institutions) and endogenous forces —
agglomeration (transport costs, scale and scope, thick labour markets, informational
externalities, consumption benefits) versus dispersion (living and factor costs,
infrastructure pressure, pollution, social problems). Historically significant for this
wiki as evidence that the core spatial-productivity framing — including the question of
whether New Zealand can hold economic critical mass against trans-Tasman pull — was on the
Treasury's agenda a quarter-century before the current debate.

<a id="executive-summary"></a>
## Executive summary: the location-forces framework

> "At the heart of the framework is the idea that people and firms choose their location to maximise their welfare and profits respectively and that various forces which can affect welfare and profits push and pull people and firms towards a particular place." (p. 3)

Agglomeration forces: lower transport costs; economies of scale and scope (specialisation,
thick labour markets offering job "insurance", better matching, bargaining power);
informational externalities (knowledge spillovers via movement of people between proximate
firms; telecommunications "predicted to remain a complement rather than a substitute for
face-to-face contact"); human capital accumulation ("people may learn faster in cities");
consumption benefits. Dispersion forces: higher living costs (particularly housing), higher
factor costs, infrastructure pressure, pollution, crime.

> "A common theme running through these agglomeration forces is the idea that higher density can lead to higher productivity. Empirical work from the United States suggests that doubling employment density can in some cases lead to a 6% increase in average labour productivity." (p. 4)

<a id="nz-in-the-world"></a>
## New Zealand in the world: the critical mass question

> "It highlights some of the advantages and disadvantages of New Zealand as a location and notes the weakness of agglomerating forces in New Zealand. The balance that will emerge between agglomeration and dispersion forces is unclear but the discussion raises a big question for New Zealand – can New Zealand maintain a critical mass of activity in the future or will activity agglomerate offshore?" (p. 4)

> "From the international perspective, this leads us to ask questions such as, is movement of people and firms to Sydney efficient? What does this mean for policy? In particular, whose welfare are we trying to maximise – those left behind in New Zealand or all New Zealanders regardless of their location?" (pp. 4–5)
