Sign-restricted structural vector autoregression (SVAR) study of the effects of expansionary
immigration shocks on the New Zealand macroeconomy, using quarterly data from 1992q1 to
2019q4. Follows the identification approach of Furlanetto and Robstad (2019), fully identifying
five structural shocks (immigration, domestic labour supply, business cycle, technology,
residual) so that the immigration shock can be cleanly separated from other labour market and
business cycle disturbances. Baseline model variables: immigration rate, real GDP, real wages,
labour force participation rate, and unemployment rate; the unemployment rate is swapped for
other variables of interest (price levels, house prices, household credit, labour productivity,
OCR, real exchange rate) one at a time to keep the model fully identified.

<a id="abstract"></a>
## Abstract

> "We investigate the impact of expansionary immigration shocks on the New Zealand economy using sign-restricted structural vector autoregression (SVAR) models. Our key findings indicate that an expansionary immigration shock results in a statistically significant increase (at the 68% credible interval) in the level of house prices and household credit relative to the baseline, while the unemployment rate shows a significant decrease. Moreover, labour productivity and headline consumer price levels show no significant deviation from baseline. However, non-tradable price levels (a sub-component of headline) show a statistically significant increase. Our variance decomposition analysis shows that an immigration shock is a major contributor towards the variation in real GDP levels (30%) and the unemployment rate (30%), at the three year horizon." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, abstract)

<a id="section-1-introduction-immigrant-share"></a>
## Section 1: Introduction — NZ immigrant share of population

> "This question is an important consideration for policymakers in New Zealand given the increasing share of immigrants as a percentage of the total population, rising from 15% in 1990 to 27% in 2019. This proportion exceeds that of most OECD countries, including Canada (21%), the United States (14%), Sweden (16%), Germany (14%), Norway (14%), the United Kingdom (13%), and France (12%). Among comparable economies, only Australia (29%) has a higher immigrant share." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 1, p. 1)

<a id="section-3-methodology-identification"></a>
## Section 3: Methodology — data, model, and sign-restriction identification scheme

> "We define an expansionary immigration shock as an exogenous increase in net migration that raises the immigration rate (the ratio of migrant stock to working-age population). By construction, this shock causes a positive co-movement between real GDP and the participation rate, as well as a negative co-movement between real GDP and real wages." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 3.3, p. 9)

<a id="section-4-1-unemployment-gdp-participation-wages"></a>
## Section 4.1: Baseline IRFs — unemployment, real GDP, participation rate, real wages

> "We find a significant fall in the unemployment rate that lasts around 18 months after the immigration shock. To be more precise, a 0.25% expansionary immigration shock is associated with a significant fall in the unemployment rate of over 0.20 percentage points in the medium-term (i.e., 2-3 years)." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.1, p. 11)

> "Quantitatively, we see an expansion in real GDP of around 0.4% six months after the expansionary immigration shock, while it remains significant for around 18 months." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.1, p. 13)

> "We observe a positive impact response of the participation rate of around 0.10%, and a negative impact response of real wage levels of around 0.18%. For the participation rate, we observe a significantly positive response for more than a year after an expansionary immigration shock, while real wages remain significantly negative for at least the first four months. However, in the long-term, the effect on real wages is likely to be positive, as the major mass of probability intervals lies in positive territory." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.1, p. 13)

<a id="section-4-1-price-levels"></a>
## Section 4.1: Baseline IRFs — consumer price levels (headline, tradable, non-tradable)

> "Headline consumer price levels show no significant deviation from baseline in response to an expansionary immigration shock. The sub-component analysis of consumer prices shows that non-tradable prices are significantly elevated (at the 68% level) approximately one year after the shock. Tradable prices, by contrast, are likely to be below the baseline, with most of the posterior probability mass lying in negative territory." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.1, p. 14-15)

<a id="section-4-1-house-prices-credit-productivity"></a>
## Section 4.1: Baseline IRFs — house prices, household credit, labour productivity

> "The most important results are significant increases in the levels of house prices and household credit, which unsurprisingly correspond with the hump-shaped responses of immigration and economic activity." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.1, p. 15)

> "Since New Zealand is characterised by high-skilled immigration, one would expect immigration to have a positive impact on labour productivity and consequently a positive impact on long-term economic growth. However, the IRF... reports otherwise: a muted response of labour productivity to an expansionary immigration shock. This result is in line with what the New Zealand Productivity Commission concluded in its 2022 report: 'immigration is likely to be neither the solution to, nor the cause of, the productivity challenges facing New Zealand' (New Zealand Productivity Commission, 2022)." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.1, p. 16)

<a id="section-4-2-fevd"></a>
## Section 4.2: Forecast error variance decomposition

> "The immigration shock contributes the most to the immigration rate, explaining more than 40% of the observed variation. The immigration shock explains between 10% and 30% of the variation in real GDP levels and the unemployment rate over a three year horizon. For the participation rate, the contribution of the immigration shock increases sharply, reaching 25% after three years... the immigration shock explains less than 10% of the variation in labour productivity over the full horizon period, which is less than the proportion of variation explained for the other variables in the model. Immigration shock explains 20% of the variation in house price levels observed at the three year horizon." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 4.2, pp. 16-18)

<a id="section-5-robustness-age-groups"></a>
## Section 5: Robustness check — younger (15-29) vs older (30-44) immigrant age groups

> "In response to a 'younger' immigrant shock, we observe a statistically significant fall (using the 68% credible intervals) in the unemployment rate and the level of tradable prices, while house prices are likely to increase given a major mass of probability intervals lie in the positive region. In response to an 'older' immigration shock, the IRFs of all variables are largely insignificant except for labour productivity. The median response of labour productivity remains positive throughout the horizon and shows a statistically significant increase (at the 68% credible interval) at around the 1 year horizon." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 5.1, pp. 19-21)

<a id="section-6-conclusion"></a>
## Section 6: Conclusion

> "Our robustness analysis highlights two key findings. First, the fall in the aggregate unemployment rate is more pronounced when relatively younger immigrants enter the labour market. Second, labour productivity is likely to increase with an increase in the inflow of relatively older immigrants." (Javed, Power & Richardson, 2026, Section 6, p. 21)
