<a id="section-4-primacy-growth"></a>
## Section 4: Urban primacy and GDP growth (Henderson 2003)

The only rigorous cross-country study that tests whether the urban structure affects aggregate economic growth (Henderson 2003, *Journal of Economic Growth*) reaches two main conclusions. First, **urbanisation per se does not affect economic growth** — urbanisation is a benign by-product of development, not an independent driver. Second, **urban primacy has large effects on growth**. Henderson finds that an increase in urban primacy of one standard deviation (approximately **15 percentage points**, starting from a mean where 31% of the urban population lives in the largest city) is associated with a reduction in annual GDP growth of approximately **1.5 percentage points per year**. The relationship is non-linear: at low levels of primacy, moderately larger main cities can foster growth through agglomeration learning effects; it is only beyond a threshold that primacy becomes harmful. Henderson uses GMM with lagged primacy levels as instruments; Bertinelli and Strobl (2003) replicate the finding nonparametrically. → [claim-0047](../claims/claim-0047.md)

<a id="section-3-primate-city"></a>
## Section 3: Political and institutional drivers of urban primacy

The empirical literature finds that **trade policy plays no systematic role** in determining urban primacy — the better and more recent studies (Ades and Glaeser 1995; Nitsch 2006) are clear on this. Instead, **political and institutional factors drive primacy**: there is strong evidence of a positive association between unstable and undemocratic regimes and primate city dominance (Ades and Glaeser 1995; Davis and Henderson 2003). The mechanism likely operates through multiple small advantages: easier access to government permits from proximity to bureaucrats, underpriced public goods and utilities in the capital, better business opportunities for government cronies, and superior road infrastructure to the primate city. Primate city favouritism is pervasive across developing countries. The Korean experience (Henderson, Lee, and Lee 2001) hints that **administrative deregulation is a powerful tool to reduce urban primacy** — reducing red tape is more beneficial for firms outside the capital, levelling the playing field. → [claim-0048](../claims/claim-0048.md)

<a id="section-3-chinese-cities"></a>
## Section 3: Chinese cities undersized due to migration barriers (Au and Henderson 2006)

Au and Henderson (2006a,b) exploit the fact that China's **hukou system** imposed strong barriers to rural-urban labour mobility, artificially constraining city growth. Using this institutional variation, they find that Chinese cities were **significantly undersized** relative to the productivity-maximising city size, resulting in **large income losses**. A second key finding: the net wage curve is quasi-flat after its maximum, meaning that cities slightly oversized incur only small welfare losses, while being below the optimum incurs large losses. This asymmetry has an important policy implication: the costs of being too small (due to mobility restrictions) are substantially larger than the costs of being too large. Restricting urban migration to prevent overcrowding is thus the wrong policy — the welfare cost of underpopulated cities is typically the bigger problem. → [claim-0049](../claims/claim-0049.md)

<a id="section-3-dual-housing"></a>
## Section 3: Exclusionary zoning, dual housing markets, and squatter settlements

Duranton synthesises the literature on dual housing markets in developing cities. The key mechanism: when **minimum lot-size requirements and other exclusionary zoning** raise formal housing costs above a threshold, squatter settlements emerge as a lower-cost substitute. Henderson (2007) shows this mechanism explains squatter settlement growth in Brazilian cities. Because squatter settlements are not "free" — their residents pay high prices for substitute public services (water from vendors, informal security), potentially more than formal sector housing in absence of exclusionary zoning — the policy implication is unambiguous: removing unnecessary formal sector housing constraints **lowers living costs, raises net wages, and is socially desirable**. Titling policies have additional benefits: Di Tella, Galliani, and Schargrodsky (2007) find that lack of formal titles affects residents' beliefs and economic behaviour; Field (2007) finds it depresses female labour supply (Peru). De Soto (2000) argues informally titled housing cannot be used as collateral, blocking enterprise development. → [claim-0050](../claims/claim-0050.md)

<a id="section-3-agglomeration-dev"></a>
## Section 3: Agglomeration economies in developing countries

The literature documents **localization economies** (within-sector) and **urbanisation economies** (across-sector) in developing countries: Brazil (Henderson 1988), Korea (Henderson, Lee and Lee 2001), India (Lall, Shalizi and Deichmann 2004), Indonesia (Deichmann et al. 2005). Results are "usually similar" to developed-country estimates of scale economies of 3–8% per 10% city size increase (i.e., 0.3–0.8% productivity increase). Two important caveats: (1) most studies do not control for **worker sorting** — more productive workers select into larger cities, biasing estimates upward; using French data, Combes, Duranton and Gobillon (2008) show sorting matters empirically, though agglomeration effects survive after controlling for it; (2) most evidence covers the **formal sector** only, though formal-informal sector linkages are often intense. These caveats mean raw agglomeration estimates for developing countries likely overstate the true causal productivity effect of density.
