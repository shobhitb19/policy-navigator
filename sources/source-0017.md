# Urban Productivity Estimation with Heterogeneous Prices and Labour

Maré, D. C. (2016). *Urban productivity estimation with heterogeneous prices and labour* (Motu Working Paper 16-21). Motu Economic and Public Policy Research. https://motu-www.motu.org.nz/wpapers/16_21.pdf

<a id="abstract"></a>
## Abstract

The paper develops a two-step estimation approach to measure urban productivity differentials for New Zealand firms. The first step estimates firm total factor productivity (TFP) controlling for spatial price variation, imperfect competition (Dixit-Stiglitz markup), firm heterogeneity, and labour quality. The second step relates estimated productivity to urban scale. The approach yields lower estimates of agglomeration elasticities than naive OLS, because much of the apparent urban premium reflects compositional differences in industry mix, firm quality, and worker quality rather than genuine agglomeration economies.

> "The paper develops a two-step approach to estimate the extent of agglomeration economies for New Zealand firms — the productivity gains that come from locating in larger cities." (Maré, 2016, abstract)

<a id="section-2-nz-evidence"></a>
## Section 2: NZ Urban Productivity Context

Auckland is New Zealand's only large metro. The analysis uses Statistics NZ's Longitudinal Business Database (LBD) and Integrated Data Infrastructure (IDI). Firms are allocated to Functional Urban Areas (FUAs) following the OECD definition — a dense core plus its commuting zone. Productivity is measured at the firm level; workers are treated as heterogeneous in quality.

The paper distinguishes three sources of apparent urban productivity differences:
- Industry composition (firms in more productive sectors may cluster in cities)
- Firm quality (better-managed firms may locate in larger cities)
- Worker quality (higher-skilled workers sort into cities)

Removing each effect progressively narrows the estimated premium.

<a id="section-4-1-productivity-premium"></a>
## Section 4.1: Labour Productivity and MFP Premiums

> "The raw difference in labour productivity between Auckland firms and those in other urban areas is 17.9%. Differences in industry composition explain about a quarter of this premium." (Maré, 2016, p. 15)

> "Within industries, labour productivity for firms employing in urban areas outside Auckland is, on average, 13.5% below that of Auckland firms." (Maré, 2016, p. 15)

> "Auckland firms are 7.9% more productive than comparable firms in other urban areas" (preferred MFP specification, without labour quality adjustment). (Maré, 2016, p. 15)

> "Auckland's MFP productivity is estimated to be 2.2% higher than that of other urban firms" (quality-adjusted labour specification). (Maré, 2016, p. 15)

Wellington labour productivity: 4.2% below Auckland. Wellington MFP (unadjusted): 3.8% above Auckland. Wellington MFP (quality-adjusted): 2.7% above Auckland. This counterintuitive result reflects Wellington's high share of government and professional services, which are measured as highly productive in value-added terms.

<a id="section-4-2-agglomeration-elasticities"></a>
## Section 4.2: Agglomeration Elasticities

> "The estimated agglomeration elasticity is 0.079 in the preferred specification without labour quality adjustment, and 0.022 in the quality-adjusted specification." (Maré, 2016, p. 16–17)

These figures imply that a 10% increase in effective city size raises firm MFP by 0.79% (unadjusted) or 0.22% (quality-adjusted). The quality-adjusted figure is the preferred estimate because it strips out the sorting of higher-quality workers into larger cities.

For comparison, the global meta-analytic central estimate is ~0.04–0.05 (Donovan et al., 2021). Maré's quality-adjusted 0.022 is below this range; the unadjusted 0.079 is above it.

<a id="section-4-3-industry-variation"></a>
## Section 4.3: Industry-Level Variation

> "Finance and insurance has the largest Auckland premium, at 41% above the productivity of equivalent firms in other urban areas." (Maré, 2016, p. 17)

Manufacturing, retail trade, and accommodation/food have smaller or near-zero Auckland premiums. This heterogeneity implies that aggregate city-level productivity estimates blend sectors with strong agglomeration gains against sectors where location matters less.

<a id="section-5-conclusions"></a>
## Section 5: Conclusions

Maré concludes that Auckland does exhibit a genuine MFP premium, but that a large fraction of the raw labour productivity differential reflects composition effects (industry mix, worker quality, firm quality) rather than pure agglomeration economies. Policy conclusions:

- Urban scale matters, but raising worker and firm quality and reducing urban frictions may have comparable or larger effects on measured productivity.
- The distinction between the raw premium and the composition-adjusted premium matters for evaluating densification policy: not all of Auckland's productivity advantage will be replicated by growing other cities.
