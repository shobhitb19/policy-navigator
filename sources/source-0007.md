<a id="abstract"></a>
## Abstract: Key findings

Output elasticity with respect to city population: **0.067–0.080** (LandScan data), implying urban productivity rises approximately **5–6%** with each doubling of city population in developing economies. African cities do not generate the same level of productivity, wage, or employment benefits as cities in Asia and Latin America. → [claim-0031](../claims/claim-0031.md), [claim-0032](../claims/claim-0032.md)

<a id="introduction"></a>
## Introduction: Urban wage premium variation across regions

Urban wage premiums in large emerging economies are far larger than in advanced economies or Africa: approximately **45% in China**, **122% in India**, and **176% in Brazil** (Chauvin et al., 2016; cited at p. 5). By contrast, Jones et al. (2017) estimate urban wage premiums of only **6% in Nigeria**, **11% in Tanzania**, and **9% in Uganda** after including full worker controls (p. 5). → [claim-0033](../claims/claim-0033.md)

<a id="section-3-agglomeration"></a>
## Section 3: Output and wage elasticities by region

OLS output elasticity (full sample, LandScan): **0.080** (≈6% per doubling). After adding firm and city controls it falls to **0.049** (≈3.4%), consistent with sorting bias. 2SLS (IV: historical 1930 population + latitude) raises it back to **0.107** (≈8%). For Asia and Latin America: establishment-level productivity rises ~**7.6%** per doubling in the 2SLS specification. Wage elasticities are similar in magnitude to output elasticities, consistent with competitive labour markets (p. 18–21). → [claim-0031](../claims/claim-0031.md)

<a id="section-3-africa"></a>
## Section 3: African cities — infrastructure and agglomeration

Once city infrastructure controls are added, the city-size effect on African firm productivity becomes **insignificant**. African firms lose approximately **four times** the percentage of annual sales due to power outages compared to firms in other developing regions. The OLS size effect for Africa is significant without controls but disappears entirely once city characteristics are included (pp. 20–21). → [claim-0032](../claims/claim-0032.md)

<a id="section-3-connectivity"></a>
## Section 3: Connectivity, firm scale, and formal employment

Road density and the De la Roca–Puga (DRP) measure of market potential both positively affect firm scale and formal employment. In fixed-effects panel regressions, formal sector employment **more than doubles** with each doubling of market potential within 10km of the average person in a city. Standard population density is not significant in the employment regressions; the DRP connectivity measure performs far better (pp. 22–23). → [claim-0034](../claims/claim-0034.md)

<a id="section-3-leapfrog"></a>
## Section 2/3: Leapfrog development and spatial fragmentation

In major African cities — including Accra, Kigali, Lagos, Nairobi, and Maputo — leapfrog patches (new development disconnected from existing built-up land) accounted for more than **40% of built-up area growth** over 2000–2010 (Lall et al., 2017, cited at p. 14). This spatial fragmentation increases infrastructure costs, reduces access to agglomeration economies near the CBD, and shrinks the effective market size for firms. Despite achieving population density, African cities have low connectivity: urban residents have ~**40% fewer potential interactions** than urban residents in Asia and Latin America (Henderson and Nigmatulina, 2016, cited at p. 13). → [claim-0035](../claims/claim-0035.md)

<a id="conclusion"></a>
## Section 4: Conclusion

The paper confirms that agglomeration economies of 5–6% per population doubling exist across developing countries generally, but that African cities are failing to convert density into productivity due to infrastructure deficits and spatial fragmentation. City connectivity — not population density per se — is the key driver of formal employment growth. → [claim-0031](../claims/claim-0031.md), [claim-0032](../claims/claim-0032.md), [claim-0034](../claims/claim-0034.md)
