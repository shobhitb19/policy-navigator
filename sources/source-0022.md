## Document structure

**Chapter 2** Adults' proficiency in key information-processing skills — literacy, numeracy, problem-solving in technology-rich environments. Mean scores and distribution by country.

**Chapter 3** Socio-demographic distribution of proficiency — age, gender, country of origin, language background, parental education. Includes analysis of native- vs. foreign-born adults.

**Chapter 4** How skills are used in the workplace — use intensity by domain (reading, writing, numeracy, ICT, problem-solving), variation across countries and industries, relationship with wages and job satisfaction, and country-level correlation with labour productivity.

**Chapter 5** Mismatches of qualifications and skills — qualification mismatch (overqualification/underqualification based on self-reported job requirements), skills mismatch (overskilling/underskilling based on PIAAC proficiency relative to co-workers in same occupation), and field-of-study mismatch. Covers prevalence rates, interactions between mismatch types, wage effects, and individual/job characteristics associated with mismatch.

## Section anchors

### section-2-proficiency
Country-level literacy, numeracy and problem-solving distributions. New Zealand performs around the OECD average on both literacy and numeracy, similar to Australia, Norway, and Sweden.

### section-3-demographics
Socio-demographic drivers of proficiency. More than 25% of adults in Australia, Canada, and New Zealand are foreign-born — among the highest shares in the 28-country survey — a structural factor that shapes the composition of the skills-qualified workforce and its labour market matching.

### section-4-skills-use
Skills use intensity at work and its relationship with wages, job satisfaction, and national labour productivity. The use of reading skills at work correlates strongly with GDP per hour worked at country level.

### section-5-mismatch
Three-dimensional mismatch framework combining qualification, skills, and field-of-study measures simultaneously. New Zealand ranks among the OECD countries with the highest combined mismatch rates (per chart data, Figures 5.2–5.8), consistent with its high foreign-born share and the pattern of credential non-recognition for migrants.
