<a id="periphery-framing"></a>
## New Zealand as a periphery economy (p. 15)

> "Around the world, as globalisation and technological change accelerated in the late 20th century, economic activity became increasingly concentrated in large, globally connected urban centres. These hubs benefit from dense infrastructure, skilled labour and fast information flows, all conditions that support knowledge-intensive and technologically-driven industries.
>
> In contrast, New Zealand's small scale and geographic isolation limited its integration into these global networks, reducing access to knowledge spillovers, collaboration and high value trade. … These characteristics are features of a periphery economy."

The briefing adopts the core–periphery frame from the new economic geography and applies it **externally**: New Zealand is the periphery relative to global cores. This is appropriate to the question it sets itself — how a small, distant economy connects to global markets, capital and capability. The domestic counterpart of that frame is simply not its subject: Auckland is not treated as New Zealand's core, and the internal distribution of production is not examined.

<a id="clusters-agglomeration"></a>
## Clusters and agglomeration (p. 25)

> "Industrial clusters and agglomeration effects are critical for knowledge-intensive industries, which benefit from proximity, talent pooling and rapid exchange of ideas. Place-based strategies that leverage regional strengths and address local constraints can unlock latent potential."

A direct endorsement of the agglomeration mechanism, in MBIE's own current words. Note that the mechanisms named — proximity, talent pooling, rapid exchange of ideas — are all distance-dependent, while the prescription that follows is framed at regional scale.

<a id="principle-3"></a>
## Principle 3: Prioritise internationally oriented, knowledge-intensive clusters (p. 54)

> "Knowledge-intensive industries thrive in clusters that enable talent pooling, rapid feedback and close collaboration, especially when these clusters are connected to global networks. To be effective, this approach should be tailored to reflect the unique differences and strengths of each region. Practical support from government, industry bodies and local institutions, in areas such as infrastructure, commercialisation assistance, investment attraction, training and regulatory clarity will be important to help these clusters and regions grow and succeed."

Preceded by: "Anchoring development in regional strengths and adjacent opportunities enables shared skills and infrastructure to be leveraged while fostering resilience."

The five principles are: (1) adopt a coherent, long-term economic approach; (2) strengthen strategic assessment, market intelligence and evaluation; (3) prioritise internationally oriented, knowledge-intensive clusters; (4) build a productivity ecosystem where innovation, talent and infrastructure work together; (5) use internationalisation and trade policy as integral productivity levers.

<a id="glossary-economic-geography"></a>
## Glossary: the spatial apparatus is present (pp. 57–58)

> "**New Economic Geography** explains how economic activity concentrates in certain regions due to increasing returns, transport costs and larger market size. It highlights the emergence of core-periphery patterns, where prosperous urban centres (cores) often host high productivity clusters of firms and workers, while outlying areas (peripheries) may lag behind."

> "**Urban economics** includes study of how geographic concentration of firms and workers, often referred to as agglomeration or clusters, can lead to productivity gains."

> "**Knowledge spillovers**: A process that occurs when ideas, skills or innovations unintentionally benefit others nearby, boosting innovation and productivity. These spillovers diminish with distance, making clusters and urban density especially powerful for sharing tacit knowledge and accelerating economic growth."

> "**Clusters and agglomeration**: Geographic concentrations of interconnected firms, skilled labour, research institutions and support services that foster innovation, productivity, and competitive advantage through proximity and collaboration."

## Observation on the document's spatial coverage

The briefing does not identify a domestic core. Auckland's position as New Zealand's
only metropolitan economy of international scale is not part of the analysis: it is
named twice in 68 pages, once as a publisher's address in a reference and once in a
table cell listing Christchurch and Auckland as hubs for aviation, engineering and
advanced materials (p. 44). The distribution of production within New Zealand, and
the question of which places carry the density the briefing's own mechanism requires,
are not examined. *(Checked by reading the full document; word frequencies — "cluster"
43, "region" 90, against "spatial" 0, "cities" 1, "urban" 4 — were how the pattern was
first noticed, but the finding rests on the reading, not the counts.)*

The theoretical apparatus is therefore present and correctly stated, but is applied to New Zealand's position in the world rather than to the geography of production within New Zealand. This should be read in light of the briefing's remit, which is international productivity and trade — the outward framing is a scoping choice rather than an oversight. It nonetheless means that the density-dependent mechanisms the briefing invokes are prescribed at a regional scale without the question being asked whether New Zealand's regions carry the density for those mechanisms to operate. The evidence bearing on that question is in [claim-0076](../claims/claim-0076.md), [claim-0207](../claims/claim-0207.md), [claim-0208](../claims/claim-0208.md) and [claim-0242](../claims/claim-0242.md).
