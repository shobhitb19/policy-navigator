<a id="introduction"></a>
## Introduction: Metropolitan concentration facts

OECD metropolitan areas (population ≥500k) cover approximately 4% of OECD land area, house roughly 50% of the OECD population, and generate approximately 55% of OECD GDP (p. 15). → [claim-0021](../claims/claim-0021.md), [claim-0030](../claims/claim-0030.md)

<a id="chapter-2-synopsis"></a>
## Chapter 2 synopsis: Agglomeration and governance

Reviews evidence that doubling city population is associated with a 2–5% increase in GDP per worker, drawing on Ahrend et al. (2014) (p. 36). Metropolitan fragmentation and governance structure mediate how much of this potential is realised. → [claim-0022](../claims/claim-0022.md)

<a id="chapter-2-fragmented-governance"></a>
## Chapter 2: Fragmented governance and productivity

Based on Ahrend et al. (2014) analysis of OECD metros: doubling the number of municipalities per 100,000 inhabitants within a metropolitan area is associated with a 5–6% reduction in labour productivity. A metropolitan governance body reduces this fragmentation penalty by approximately 50% (p. 56). → [claim-0023](../claims/claim-0023.md), [claim-0024](../claims/claim-0024.md)

<a id="chapter-2-governance-bodies"></a>
## Chapter 2: Governance bodies, sprawl, and population growth

Metro areas with a metropolitan governance body experienced 0.28 percentage points higher annual population growth over 2000–2010 and declining urban sprawl over 2000–2006 (as measured by land use intensity). Those without governance bodies experienced increasing sprawl over the same period (p. 59). → [claim-0025](../claims/claim-0025.md)

<a id="chapter-2-transport"></a>
## Chapter 2: Transport authority and public transport satisfaction

Based on the OECD Metro Governance Survey: metropolitan areas with a dedicated transport authority have public transport satisfaction rates approximately 14 percentage points higher than those without (p. 62). → [claim-0026](../claims/claim-0026.md)

<a id="chapter-2-land-use"></a>
## Chapter 2: Building regulations and floor-space costs

Citing Glaeser et al. (2005): building regulations in central London and Manhattan raise floor-space prices to 2–8 times above construction costs. The report presents this as evidence that restrictive planning rules drive a large wedge between supply costs and market prices in high-demand urban centres (p. 65). → [claim-0027](../claims/claim-0027.md)

<a id="chapter-3-countries"></a>
## Chapter 3: Metro GDP contribution and regional spillovers

OECD metropolitan areas (500k+) contributed approximately 50% of total OECD GDP growth over 2000–2010, despite covering only ~4% of land (p. 97). Large cities (>2 million population) generate positive productivity spillovers to surrounding regions up to 200–300km distant; halving travel time between a large city and its hinterland is associated with 0.2–0.4 percentage points higher annual GDP per capita growth in the hinterland region (p. 102). → [claim-0029](../claims/claim-0029.md), [claim-0030](../claims/claim-0030.md)

<a id="chapter-3-environment"></a>
## Chapter 3: Urban density and transport emissions

Drawing on Kennedy et al. (2009) and OECD city data: per capita ground-transport CO₂ emissions fall consistently as urban density rises. Compact cities emit substantially less transport CO₂ per capita than sprawling cities of equivalent population (p. 105). → [claim-0028](../claims/claim-0028.md)
