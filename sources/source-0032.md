## Section 1: Headline density elasticities across categories

The paper synthesises 347 density-elasticity estimates from 180 studies (out of 473 analyses from 268 studies in the full collected evidence base) and contributes 16 original elasticity estimates for categories where prior evidence was thin or inconsistent. Headline log-point elasticities reported in Section 1 include: wages +0.04 (rising to approximately +0.08 in non-high-income countries), rent +0.15, vehicle mileage travelled −0.06, pollution concentration **+0.13 (an increase, not a decrease)**, average speed −0.12, patent activity +0.21, consumption variety +0.12, green space preservation +0.28, car use −0.05, energy consumption −0.07, crime −0.085, and local public service costs −0.17. The authors conclude that density is, on net, a positive-welfare amenity, but caution that its distributional effects may be regressive. → [claim-0195](../claims/claim-0195.md), [claim-0196](../claims/claim-0196.md), [claim-0197](../claims/claim-0197.md), [claim-0198](../claims/claim-0198.md), [claim-0199](../claims/claim-0199.md)

## Table 6: Recommended elasticity estimates by category

Table 6 presents the paper's "recommended" elasticity for each of 15 outcome categories, each derived either from a citation-weighted mean of the collected literature or, where evidence was thin, from a dedicated original analysis using OECD data. Of direct relevance to this wiki: wage elasticity 0.04 (citation-weighted mean; 0.08 for non-high-income countries) — basis for causal interpretation; patent intensity elasticity 0.21 (citation-weighted mean, in line with the original OECD analysis) — associative, reflects both learning spillovers and sorting of innovative workers, not decomposed; energy-use-reduction elasticity 0.07 (citation-weighted mean) — associative; local-public-spending-reduction elasticity 0.17 (citation-weighted mean) — associative; and pollution-reduction elasticity **−0.13** (dedicated high-quality papers: Carozzi and Roth 2018, confirmed by Borck and Schrauth 2018 and the original OECD analysis) — **the negative sign under the "reduction" framing means pollution concentration increases with density**, and is one of only five categories the authors regard as causally interpretable. → [claim-0195](../claims/claim-0195.md), [claim-0196](../claims/claim-0196.md), [claim-0197](../claims/claim-0197.md), [claim-0199](../claims/claim-0199.md)

<a id="section-4-10-pollution-reduction"></a>
## Appendix 4.10: Pollution reduction — causal interpretation justified

The technical appendix's section on pollution states: "Carozzi and Roth (2018) and Borck and Schrauth (2018) are the most credible estimates... we are confident in recommending the -0.13 elasticity... Given that both... use instrumental variable strategies... a causal interpretation seems justifiable." This is one of the paper's most robust, causally-grounded findings: doubling density is associated with, and the authors believe plausibly causes, a roughly 13% **increase** in local pollution concentration, not a decrease. This directly contradicts the "8 percent less pollution" figure as it was relayed second-hand in Box 1.4 of Lall et al. (2021), which appears to have inverted the sign when compressing the result into a single headline bundle of density benefits. → [claim-0198](../claims/claim-0198.md)
