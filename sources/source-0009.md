<a id="chapter-2-floor-space-demand"></a>
## Chapter 2: Income and population elasticities of floor space demand

Chapter 2 estimates the drivers of urban floor space demand using the GHS-UCDB cross-city panel. The central finding is that income, not population, is the primary driver of floor space per person. Doubling a city's per-capita income (population held constant) raises floor space per person by approximately **+29%**; doubling population (income held constant) reduces floor space per person by approximately **−40%** because additional residents crowd into existing built-up area. Vertical layering (building height) responds even more strongly to income than horizontal built-up area does — confirming that pyramidal city form is an economic outcome, not a planning target. Cities in low- and lower-middle-income countries remain dense and flat because their residents cannot yet afford to demand more floor space. → [claim-0041](../claims/claim-0041.md)

<a id="chapter-1-2-urban-form"></a>
## Chapters 1–2: Horizontal vs infill/vertical expansion by income group

Cross-city analysis of 9,500 cities shows that between 1990 and 2015 every income group's cities expanded their built-up area faster than population grew — implying rising floor space per person everywhere. But the mode of expansion differed sharply by income. Low- and lower-middle-income cities grew almost entirely through **horizontal spread** (outward expansion of the urban footprint). High-income cities added substantial **infill development** (densification within existing footprints) and **vertical layering** alongside continued horizontal expansion, producing cities with denser, taller central districts. The contrast is captured in the "pancake vs pyramid" metaphor: poor cities spread flat; rich cities grow taller toward the centre. → [claim-0042](../claims/claim-0042.md)

<a id="density-meta-effects"></a>
## Box 1.4: Meta-synthesis of density effects (Ahlfeldt and Pietrostefani 2019)

Ahlfeldt and Pietrostefani (2019), "The Economic Effects of Density: A Synthesis" (CEPR Discussion Paper), synthesises the empirical literature on density effects. Their meta-analysis (cited in Box 1.4 of this report) finds that a **doubling of urban density** is associated with: **+4% wages**, **+19% patent rates**, **−7% energy consumption**, **−8% air pollution**, and **−14.4% unit cost of local public services**. These effects span productivity, innovation, sustainability, and fiscal efficiency, reinforcing the case for density as a cross-cutting policy objective.

<a id="spotlight-3-ghg-density"></a>
## Spotlight 3: GHG emissions and urban form — income-group heterogeneity

Spotlight 3 uses the Global Gridded Model of Carbon Footprints (Moran et al. 2018) matched to the GHS-UCDB city database. Figure S3.1 shows that per-capita GHG emissions **decline with urban density in high- and upper-middle-income countries**, consistent with the density-emissions literature. However, this **inverse relationship does not hold for low- and lower-middle-income cities**, which are already far denser than their high-income counterparts (e.g., Dhaka at 33 m² built-up area per person vs Dallas at 459 m²). In poor cities, high density reflects poverty and overcrowding, not low-carbon lifestyles. Cities in these groups are projected to see density fall as they develop — the opposite trajectory to high-income cities. → [claim-0044](../claims/claim-0044.md)

<a id="chapter-4-path-dependence"></a>
## Chapter 4: Infrastructure path dependence and optimal sequencing

Chapter 4 argues that urban structures have a "putty clay" quality: once built, they are difficult to modify and can remain in place for more than **150 years** (Hallegatte 2009). This makes the sequencing of investment critical. The cheapest developmental path is: (1) **infrastructure first** (sewerage, drainage, electricity, clean water, roads — most cost-effective to install at scale on clear land), then (2) **housing** (enabled by functioning land markets and infrastructure access), then (3) **industrial premises** (attracted by agglomeration economies and connective infrastructure). Reversing the sequence — settling land before installing infrastructure — results in informal settlements where retroactive servicing is far more expensive and often faces community resistance. → [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md)

<a id="chapter-4-dar-es-salaam"></a>
## Chapter 4: Dar es Salaam sites-and-services — early infrastructure durability

World Bank sites-and-services projects in Dar es Salaam (1970s) installed trunk infrastructure in new development areas ahead of settlement. Michaels et al. (forthcoming, *Journal of Political Economy*) finds that the 1970s infrastructure plans still closely match Dar es Salaam's road network today, confirming the durability of early investments. Neighbourhoods developed under the new-development projects **command the highest land values in the city — including above rich residential districts** — because they have a higher building footprint-to-plot ratio and more organised road networks. Upgraded slum areas (e.g., Manzese) have disorganised roads, small irregular plots, and land so undervalued it is not worth assessing for tax purposes. This contrast demonstrates that infrastructure investment determines a city's spatial form and land value distribution for generations. → [claim-0045](../claims/claim-0045.md)

<a id="box-3-6-mexico-city"></a>
## Box 3.6: Mexico City metro — informality and real income effects

Zarate (2020), "Spatial Misallocation, Informality and Transit Improvements: Evidence from Mexico City," estimates the welfare effects of metro expansion using a spatial general equilibrium model. Near new metro stations, informality fell by **4 percentage points**. For every **$1 invested in metro construction**, average real incomes rose by **20%** relative to a counterfactual of a perfectly efficient labour market — capturing the large welfare gains from reducing spatial misallocation in a city where informal labour markets and poor transit connectivity prevent workers from reaching their best-matched jobs. → [claim-0046](../claims/claim-0046.md)
