Canonical test of whether the urban productivity premium reflects agglomeration economies or
Darwinian firm selection (tougher competition in larger markets culling weak firms). The
authors nest a generalised Melitz–Ottaviano selection model within a standard agglomeration
model, yielding a sharp distributional prediction: stronger selection *left-truncates* the
firm productivity distribution, whereas stronger agglomeration *right-shifts and dilates* it.
Applying a new quantile approach to French establishment-level data, they find no sizeable
differences in truncation between denser and less-dense areas — selection does not explain
spatial productivity differences. The result is robust across sectors, city-size thresholds,
establishment samples, estimation techniques, and area definitions.

<a id="abstract"></a>
## Abstract

> "Stronger selection in larger cities left-truncates the productivity distribution whereas stronger agglomeration right-shifts and dilates the distribution. Using this prediction, French establishment-level data, and a new quantile approach, we show that firm selection cannot explain spatial productivity differences. This result holds across sectors, city size thresholds, establishment samples, and area definitions." (Combes et al., 2012, abstract)

<a id="section-1-main-findings"></a>
## Main findings

> "Our main finding is that there are no sizeable differences in left truncation between denser and less dense employment areas, indicating that selection does not play a major role in explaining the productive advantages of urban density. Instead, the entire log productivity distribution in denser areas is right-shifted relative to the distribution in less dense areas. Furthermore, more productive establishments are better able to reap the benefits of urban density, which dilates the log productivity distribution. As a result, while the average productivity gain is about 9.7 percent, establishments at the bottom quartile of the log productivity distribution are only 4.8 percent more productive in employment areas with above-median density than elsewhere whereas establishments at the top quartile are about 14.4 percent more productive in denser areas." (Combes et al., 2012, pp. 3–4)

The paper distinguishes selection (weak firms unable to survive tougher competition) from
sorting (productive firms choosing large markets); it tests the former. It does not
adjudicate among the standard agglomeration channels (sharing, matching, learning), and does
not address endogeneity of city scale, citing evidence that such issues are minor.
