# Treasury AN 25/12 — Why not both? The effects of innovation and capital on productivity in New Zealand

Winter, R., Devine, G., Janssen, J., & Thompson, S. (2025, October). *Why not both? The effects of innovation and capital on productivity in New Zealand* (Analytical Note AN 25/12). New Zealand Treasury.

**Full document:** [an25-12.pdf](an25-12.pdf)

---

<a id="executive-summary"></a>
## Executive summary

The paper asks why New Zealand's productivity lags behind OECD peers and concludes that two complementary deficits — low capital intensity and slow technology diffusion — are jointly responsible. Neither alone explains the gap; both must be addressed simultaneously (hence "why not both").

New Zealand's capital stock per worker sits at approximately 50% of OECD comparator levels (Figure 3, Penn World Tables). Gross domestic expenditure on R&D (GERD) was NZ$6.4 billion in 2024, approximately 1.5% of GDP, against an OECD average of approximately 2.7%; business R&D (BERD) is roughly 1% of GDP. New Zealand conducts only 0.02% of total OECD R&D.

The paper's central analytic distinction: approximately 95% of New Zealand firms innovate by *adopting or adapting* technologies developed elsewhere; only approximately 5% create new-to-world products or processes (Business Operations Survey 2023). Diffusion — not invention — is the dominant innovation pathway for the economy, so improving the *speed and breadth* of technology adoption matters far more than frontier R&D subsidies alone.

---

<a id="section-2-productivity-growth"></a>
## Section 2: Productivity growth

The paper situates New Zealand's productivity problem in international context. Since the 1990s reforms, New Zealand productivity has remained persistently below OECD peer levels and has not converged. Labour productivity growth has been slow relative to comparator small open economies.

Comin & Hobijn (2011) provide the cross-country benchmark: variation in the *speed of technology adoption* explains approximately 25% of cross-country GDP per capita growth differentials during the 20th century — more than most other single factors.

<a id="section-3-capital-innovation-nz"></a>
## Section 3: Capital intensity and innovation in New Zealand

**Capital intensity:** New Zealand's capital stock per worker is approximately 50% of OECD comparator levels (Penn World Tables, Figure 3). This is a structural deficit, not a cyclical one.

**R&D and innovation activity:**
- GERD: NZ$6.4 billion (2024) ≈ 1.5% of GDP (OECD average ≈ 2.7%)
- BERD: approximately 1% of GDP (approximately half the OECD median)
- NZ share of total OECD R&D: 0.02%
- Business Operations Survey (2023): approximately 95% of NZ firms adopt/adapt existing technologies; approximately 5% create new-to-world innovations

**Technology currency (diffusion breadth):** The share of New Zealand firms reporting their core technology as "fully up to date":
- 2007: 51%
- 2012: 47%
- 2017: 47%
- 2022: 44%

This declining trend — from a majority to a minority of firms operating with current technology — indicates the diffusion problem is worsening, not improving.

**The complementarity argument:** Capital investment is not only a supply-side input; it is also a *vehicle for technology diffusion*. When a firm invests in new plant, equipment, or software, it simultaneously raises its capital-to-labour ratio *and* embeds newer technology vintages. Treating capital investment and innovation policy as separate silos misses this complementarity. The paper's title captures the prescription: pursue both simultaneously.

<a id="section-4-diffusion-channels"></a>
## Section 4: Technology diffusion channels — NZ position

The paper reviews four "common channels" through which technology diffuses between firms and countries, and assesses New Zealand's position on each:

**1. Trade openness / import intensity**
- Imports of goods and services (excluding bulk commodities) carry embedded technology; competitive import exposure disciplines domestic firms to adopt.
- NZ is *below* OECD median on import intensity of manufactures and intermediate goods.
- NZ is *above* OECD median on imports of services (a partial offset).

**2. Foreign direct investment (FDI)**
- FDI is a primary channel for transferring management practices and frontier technology to host economies.
- NZ FDI stock is *below* OECD median.

**3. Clustering and infrastructure (agglomeration)**
- Dense urban clusters support knowledge spillovers, intangible investment, and informal technology exchange.
- NZ is *below* OECD median on rail and road quality metrics.
- NZ is *above* OECD median on urban population share and airports per million population (geographic dispersion offset).
- Citing Haskel & Westlake (2022): "dynamic cities requiring effective transport, high population density, and job mobility support cluster formation and intangible investment."
- The paper explicitly notes NZ may benefit from labour/construction regulation, infrastructure, and planning reforms as enablers of better clustering.

**4. Input costs and access to finance**
- High capital costs raise the hurdle rate for investment that would otherwise embed new technology.
- NZ is *below* OECD median on venture capital availability and SME interest rate spreads.
- NZ is *above* OECD median on low barriers to entry (a positive enabling factor).

Australia comparison: The Australian Productivity Commission (2023) estimated that reviving technology diffusion could lift Australian GDP by 1.6–4.9%.

<a id="section-5-policy-synthesis"></a>
## Section 5: Policy synthesis and future research

The paper does not produce a ranked policy list but frames the required response as addressing both capital intensity and diffusion simultaneously. Key enablers identified:
- Infrastructure (transport and urban) to support agglomeration and clustering
- Planning and labour market reforms to enable denser cities and labour mobility
- Trade and FDI openness to increase exposure to frontier technology
- Finance access for SMEs and growth firms
- Complementary R&D incentives that leverage the 95% adoption pathway (not only the 5% creation pathway)

Future research directions flagged: firm-level analysis of diffusion pathways; quantifying the capital-diffusion complementarity; regional variation in diffusion access.
