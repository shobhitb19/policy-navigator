<a id="section-3-methodology"></a>
## Section 3: Methodology

The paper estimates a TFP equation for 22 OECD countries and 13 industries (1995–2007) that includes own-country R&D capital stock, foreign-country R&D capital stocks (weighted by bilateral distance or trade intensity), and domestic inter-industry R&D spillovers. The spatial econometric approach accounts for cross-sectional dependence and uses instrumental variables to address endogeneity of R&D investment decisions. The distance-weighted foreign R&D variable is constructed using great-circle distances between capital cities, allowing the paper to test directly whether geographic proximity between countries amplifies knowledge spillovers. → [claim-0209](../claims/claim-0209.md)

<a id="section-4-results"></a>
## Section 4: Main results

Own-country R&D has a TFP elasticity of approximately 0.25 — a 10% increase in own R&D capital stock is associated with approximately 2.5% higher TFP. This estimate is broadly consistent with the prior literature (Coe and Helpman 1995 found 0.06–0.25 depending on country). Foreign R&D capital is also significantly associated with domestic TFP growth, with a smaller elasticity that reflects the attenuation of spillovers across national borders. The combined effect of own and foreign R&D implies that countries benefit not only from their own innovation investment but from proximity to and integration with other high-R&D economies. → [claim-0209](../claims/claim-0209.md), [claim-0210](../claims/claim-0210.md)

<a id="section-5-distance-spillovers"></a>
## Section 5: Distance and cross-country productivity spillovers

Distance-weighted foreign R&D specifications outperform trade-weighted specifications in explaining TFP variation, suggesting that geographic proximity — not just trade intensity — is a channel for international productivity spillovers. Countries closer to major R&D-intensive economies (Germany, USA, Japan) benefit more from foreign knowledge stocks than countries at the periphery of the innovation system, controlling for trade volumes. This finding is consistent with the Jaffe et al. (1993) and Li (2014) patent-citation evidence on geographic decay of knowledge spillovers, but extends it to the macro level: the geography of innovation has aggregate productivity consequences, not just effects on inventive output. New Zealand's geographic position — distant from all major OECD R&D centres — implies that it captures fewer foreign productivity spillovers than geographically central economies, compounding the limitations of its own relatively small R&D base. → [claim-0210](../claims/claim-0210.md)
