<a id="section-3-methodology"></a>
## Section 3: Methodology

The paper distinguishes two hypotheses: "dying over time" (the aggregate intensity of localisation decreases across all patent cohorts as global communications improve) and "dying with age" (a given stock of knowledge becomes less geographically bounded as it ages and diffuses into the public domain). These hypotheses make different predictions about the time-series behaviour of the distance/border coefficient in a citation equation. Using USPTO data on patent citations across 39 countries (1975–2002) and 319 US MSAs (within-US), the paper estimates gravity models and Jaffe-style matching regressions with interactions between distance/border effects and both citation age and calendar year. → [claim-0205](../claims/claim-0205.md)

<a id="section-4-international-results"></a>
## Section 4: International results

National borders are a strong barrier to knowledge spillovers. Patent citations are substantially more likely to stay within national borders than the distribution of patenting activity predicts. More importantly, this border effect has not declined over the 1975–2002 period — if anything, relative localisation within countries has increased. The paper rejects both the "dying over time" and "dying with age" hypotheses for the international sample: the home-country citation bias remains robust across all time periods and all citation ages tested. → [claim-0205](../claims/claim-0205.md), [claim-0206](../claims/claim-0206.md)

<a id="section-5-us-results"></a>
## Section 5: US MSA-level results

Within the United States, patent citations are geographically concentrated at the MSA level, replicating and extending Jaffe et al. (1993). Distance reduces citation probability, and the decay is steep at short distances. The paper finds that the within-US distance effect also shows no evidence of declining over the 1975–2002 sample period, contrary to what a simple technological-convergence story would predict. Citations between highly innovative MSAs (technology clusters) show somewhat less localisation, consistent with the idea that dense knowledge networks can partially substitute for proximity, but the core distance decay remains. → [claim-0205](../claims/claim-0205.md), [claim-0206](../claims/claim-0206.md)
