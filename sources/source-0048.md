<a id="about-this-report"></a>
## About this report

This is the fourth report in the State of the City series and is **not a full annual benchmarking edition**. It was prepared by The Business of Cities (TBoC) to inform *Whitiki Taua*, the Auckland Action Agenda — a programme led by the Committee for Auckland that emerged from three years of State of the City evidence. Where the 2023–2025 editions scored Auckland across ten pillars against a fixed ten-city peer group, the 2026 report is organised around five focus areas and uses indicative comparator cities selected per indicator for data availability.

Consequently, **the 2026 report does not restate the ten-pillar decile scores** that the 2025 edition reported (see [claim-0090](../claims/claim-0090.md) on the Prosperity decile). It recaps the 2025 wheel and describes the direction of travel qualitatively rather than re-scoring it. Care is needed when reading 2026 figures as a continuation of a 2025 series: the peer field and the units differ by indicator.

The retained core peer group is Auckland, Austin, Brisbane, Copenhagen, Dublin, Fukuoka, Helsinki, Portland, Tel Aviv and Vancouver.

The five focus areas are: global gateway and primate city; the innovation and talent engine; city centre and new connections; city of nature; and homes, neighbourhoods and opportunity.

<a id="executive-summary"></a>
## Executive summary

Auckland's strongest credentials are assessed as being in **global gateway** and **city of nature**. The clearest recent improvement is in the **innovation and talent engine**. The most challenges are seen in **homes, neighbourhoods and opportunity**, while **city centre connections** faces the biggest imminent change with the opening of the City Rail Link.

The framing throughout is that Auckland's underlying performance is improving across economic activity, investment, affordability and culture — "much of it the result of action at the city scale" — but that "visitors, investors and specialists have not yet recognised many of these gains, and concerns about talent continue to shape how the city is viewed."

The report positions Auckland's scale as conferring a distinct national role "which warrants settings suited to a major gateway city," and notes that progress in most focus areas "remains highly dependent on central-government alignment," with greater fiscal autonomy identified as a feature of some comparable international cities.

<a id="performance-perception"></a>
## Headlines and highlights: the performance–perception gap

Four summary trends are identified for 2026:

- **Fundamentals improving, perceptions flat.** "Auckland's fundamentals are improving, but perceptions among visitors, experts and investors have remained relatively flat. The result is a widening gap between the city's performance and how it is perceived."
- **Barbell performance.** Auckland records very high standout performances alongside areas of marked challenge.
- **Hidden specialisations.** Areas of excellence exist but require "active curation and purposeful storytelling."
- **The power to change.** "Auckland is tending to win where it has significant levers it controls, and losing where it does not. The strongest readings cluster around city-government and locally-led activity. Improvement priorities sit in territory where national infrastructure and capital dominate."

Largest measured rises since the previous edition include conventions and meetings (+61 places), space tech (+50), climate tech (+36), construction-cost affordability (+24) and university student mix (+20). Largest falls include urban planning effectiveness (−41), mobile internet speeds (−26), governance efficiency (−20), overall global city standing (−10) and talent concentration (−7).

The report states plainly that "labour market sentiment stays softer than most peers, the lived experience of place, amenity, aesthetics and buzz have all plateaued, and Auckland's productivity gap remains stark."

<a id="gateway-assets"></a>
## Focus area 1 — Global gateway and primate city: assets

Eight gateway roles are assessed:

- **Air gateway.** Auckland concentrates 73% of New Zealand's international passenger movements — "distributed more than in the capital cities of Ireland, Finland, Denmark or Chile, where geography and airport systems funnel 80%+ of international traffic through the main city." Auckland Airport is New Zealand's third largest cargo port by value, handling $26.6bn of exports and imports combined in 2024.
- **Corporate gateway.** "Well over 60% of leading New Zealand companies are currently hosted in Auckland, with figures rising amid corporate restructuring, mergers and acquisitions (M&A) and portfolio shifts that tend to centralise functions. … Auckland is New Zealand's only city with an internationally competitive city centre job density, ahead of several recognised European and North American city centres."
- **Labour market and skilled talent gateway.** "Auckland has an 8% highly-skilled population premium compared to the rest of New Zealand." Population growth over the last five years sits in the top quarter of major cities; student intensity is among the highest of any major city; the old-age dependency ratio is lower than most in the OECD. "However, larger business-oriented cities retain, upskill and attract more effectively than Auckland currently does."
- **Visitor economy gateway.** Over $3bn of international visitor spend; risen 60 places for international meetings hosted since 2023 into the global top 10%. "Though, compared to other gateway cities, it under-performs for its size, with its share of international visitor spend only matching its population share."
- **Trade gateway.** Ports of Auckland has a 28% share of New Zealand container throughput (down 1% since 2019) and handles about 19% of New Zealand's combined export and import cargo value.
- **International education gateway.** About 55% of New Zealand's international students. The University of Auckland generates nearly NZ$500m in international tuition and research revenue with more than 240,000 alumni.
- **Technology and innovation gateway.** "Auckland holds 48% of the national stock [of internationally visible, homegrown investable companies], with the share rising to 52% among firms with 20 or more employees, 54% among those that have attracted at least US$1m in funding, and 55% among those launched since 2020."
- **Gateway to global influence.** Auckland accounts for roughly half of all New Zealand city appearances in leading indices (55% of earned appearances; 55% of Global Top 10 placements), against Wellington 21%, Christchurch 13% and Hamilton 11%.

**Peak city centre job density** (workers per km², CBD): Auckland records **37,000**, sitting just below the peer average and behind Boston (**96,000**), Chicago, Brisbane and Barcelona, but ahead of Washington, Melbourne, Perth, Vancouver, Calgary, Adelaide, Wellington and Christchurch. *(Source: TBoC research based on Infometrics, Stats NZ.)*

**Gateway shares relative to population** (1.0× = proportional to population share): students 1.61×; air 2.1×; visitor spend 1.00×; corporate HQ 2.12×; US$1m+ funded firms 1.58×; high-skill premium +8.0 percentage points.

<a id="gateway-gaps"></a>
## Focus area 1 — Gaps and headwinds

Context: "currently nearly half of greenfield foreign direct investment across the whole OECD go to barely 40 subnational city-regions."

- **Visitor economy underweight.** "Auckland captures just 29% of New Zealand's visitor spend, has shed almost two percentage points of share to the rest of New Zealand since 2019, and is one of only two benchmark cities (with Helsinki) yet to recover aggregate spend to pre-Covid levels."
- **Not as much of a high-skill gateway as most.** "Its high-skill concentration sits behind Dublin, Lisbon, Stockholm and Oslo, with less of a 'gateway premium' than most peer regions. The likely costs are slower productivity gains from job-switching and specialist depth, fewer pathways for New Zealanders into higher-value roles, and large knowledge-intensive firms forming less readily."
- **A gateway for companies to capitalise but often can't retain them.** "It leads New Zealand in the concentration of firms with growth potential, yet peer cities retain more businesses as they add staff and raise external capital. New Zealand firms appear to move offshore earlier, weakening Auckland's role as a home for high-quality growth companies."
- **Population strength may temper.** Post-Covid population growth is among the fastest of any Anglosphere mid-sized city and 25-year growth runs well ahead of peers, "but 2050 projections settle Auckland into the middle of the pack."
- **Investor sentiment trails the fundamentals.** Multiple readings place Auckland below peers for investment appeal over a sustained period.

**Implication stated:** "Auckland's gateway flows aggregate to within 5% of the average share of other comparable international cities, performing especially well as a corporate, aviation and student gateway. Its challenge appears to lie in converting these flows into more investment, visitors and tradeable industries."

<a id="innovation-talent"></a>
## Focus area 2 — Innovation and talent engine

**Assets:**

- Auckland is 17th "rising star" out of more than 300 cities — "the only OECD city to achieve this feat and one of only seven outside Asia" (Dealroom Global Tech Ecosystem Index 2026).
- "Auckland's enterprise value — that is the value of more innovative businesses that have received investment to grow — has multiplied 19 times since 2019."
- "Auckland is overall up 11 places worldwide to 75th strongest business ecosystem, ahead of peers, Portland and Fukuoka, and the likes of Lisbon (87th) and Bogotá (125th). It is decisively clear of Wellington (141st) and Christchurch (261st)." Recent total funding growth (+117%) is second only to Austin in its cohort.
- Niches: 72nd for AI, 74th for fintech; foodtech top 40 in absolute scale and 23rd most concentrated by city size; space tech top 25 (up 51 places in a year); agritech up 67 places.
- University research: within the global top 15% for publication volume, citation impact slightly above world norm, eight fields in the global top 50. "Auckland has also nearly doubled its share of publications produced jointly with industry, from 4% to 7%, one of the largest rises among similar cities."
- "Scale-ups command 50% of venture capital deployed in Auckland against 29% on average globally, a signal of investor confidence in Auckland's scale-ups and of more limited access to capital for earlier-stage firms."

**Gaps:**

- "While 19 alumni-founded startups have achieved rounds over US$100 million, the founder alumni base is less than half the size of Lisbon's, Helsinki's or Vancouver's, and Auckland's position weakens at each stage of business growth."
- "The share of corporates in Auckland's tech ecosystem is only half that of Vancouver or Dublin, which makes it harder for reference customers, later-stage capital and first commercial deals to land."
- Auckland rates 15th worldwide for student mix and top 20 for desirability, "yet the retention pathway from student to labour market participant remains under-developed. Some talent concentration figures see Auckland fall over 40 places year on year."
- Data-centre stock sits in the bottom third of Asia-Pacific markets — "a constraint on AI and cloud-intensive innovation activity."

<a id="city-centre-assets"></a>
## Focus area 3 — City centre and new connections: assets

- The centre "generates about 8% of New Zealand's GDP, an outsized national contribution for its size."
- "It hosts over 5% of all New Zealand jobs in fewer than five square kilometres, its role matching Helsinki's in Finland and Dublin's in Ireland. Such concentration means its performance has a significant effect on national outcomes." (Measured value: **5.3%**.)
- "It hosts roughly one in four of all New Zealand finance jobs, a higher national share than most, as well as nearly one in six of all professional and technical jobs." (Measured value: **25%** of national finance jobs.)
- "Hosting close to 160,000 jobs, among the most concentrated among peer centres, it provides by far the thickest labour market in New Zealand, with the deepest choice of skills and employers."
- "Home to well over 50,000 tertiary students" (**53,900**), "one of the largest and most resilient student populations of any comparable city centre."
- Central residential growth has been faster than in almost all comparable city centres, exceeded only by a small number including Melbourne and Vancouver.
- New Zealand's largest and fastest-growing hotel market; convention pipeline close to Brisbane and Vancouver for mid-sized international conventions.

**Spectrum of performance** (Auckland value, with peer field): national financial jobs hub 25%; tertiary students 53,900; national jobs role 5.3%; **driver of whole-city jobs 15.8%** (share of the wider city's jobs located in the centre); **job-to-resident ratio 4.2 : 1**; **productivity US$121k GDP per worker**; rapid 30-minute public transport catchment; **non-car commute share 39%**. Rows are ordered from Auckland's strongest position to its weakest, so the last four are where Auckland sits worst against peers such as Zurich, Dublin, Vancouver, Toronto and Melbourne. *(Source: TBoC research based on Stats NZ, Infometrics and TravelTime.)*

Catalysts noted: City Rail Link; new NZICC capacity; the Auckland City Deal naming the city centre a national priority growth area and recognising Wynyard Quarter as an established innovation precinct; a pipeline of over NZ$6bn in private investment.

<a id="city-centre-gaps"></a>
## Focus area 3 — Gaps and headwinds

- **Bridging the gap.** "The centre generates more than 20% of regional GDP despite a smaller share of Aucklanders than most cities living within a convenient commute of it. City Rail Link is set to bring the commutable catchment share much closer to peer benchmarks, and in doing so, will widen access to higher value jobs, deepen the labour pool and spread the centre's economic benefits more evenly."
- **Making the city centre preferable by public transport.** "Until now, Auckland city centre has been more attractive for car users than in other cities, with less speedy and affordable public transport."
- **Growth has not yet spread across the network.** "Compared to other cities, the population living along better connected public transport corridors has grown much slower. A key litmus test of the City Rail Link is how well it leads to growth around new and existing rail catchments."
- **Congestion.** "Auckland remains regularly rated the most congested in Oceania, in the bottom 5% worldwide on traffic congestion outcomes, with the burden spread more evenly across the whole city than most."
- **Scale not yet fully harnessed.** "City centre productivity is very strong in national terms but internationally sits in the bottom third of peers, suggesting it needs to achieve stronger clustering of high performing sectors to achieve wider uplift in the city and nationwide."
- **Footfall plateau.** "Central pedestrian footfall has plateaued well below 2019, making one of the weakest recovery curves of city centres in high income economies."
- **Thin innovation and amenity base.** The centre "does not yet record the R&D, venture capital or culture mix that the likes of Sydney, Melbourne and Singapore do."

**2030 signals to watch:** more than 1 million residents within 45 minutes door-to-door of the centre by public transport; city centre jobs reaching 175,000 (from 159,000 today); city centre productivity per worker exiting the bottom third of international peers; Auckland exiting the world's 100 most congested cities.

<a id="homes-assets"></a>
## Focus area 5 — Homes, neighbourhoods and opportunity: assets

- "In a weakening development cycle, Auckland has seen one of the top 10 most positive trendlines of any major city in housing delivery, a tangible dividend from 10 years of planning. Measured affordability is improving and is now ahead of many close peers."
- "The supply mix is reorienting. The medium density share of new consents has shifted faster than in Vancouver or Brisbane, and the build to rent pipeline now sits alongside the more established end of international markets."
- **Gentle density has paid off.** "Beyond Auckland's core, areas that added gentle density have gained businesses, young people and a mix of uses much faster than those that did not. This trend, which is also reflected in other international cities, shows Auckland can accommodate growth while creating more economically active neighbourhoods."
- Auckland rates among the top 10% of major cities for everyday access to amenities, ahead of Zurich, Amsterdam and London; third among 50 cities for family life; global top 15 for urban quality.
- Its Business Improvement District network "is one of the densest in the world, ahead of all Australasian and European peers per capita."
- 70 marae and more than 540 official Māori place names — "the largest documented single formalisation by a major metropolitan jurisdiction."

---

<a id="homes-gaps"></a>
## Focus area 5 — Gaps and headwinds

- **Jobs and people are drifting apart.** "The distance between where Aucklanders live and where jobs are being created is rising, creating a key drag on medium-term productivity and liveability. Businesses are being created fastest in the inner suburbs, yet most new housing is going elsewhere, creating a potential spatial mismatch that raises travel times and recruitment challenges."
- **Connecting new housing.** "New housing is concentrated close to the city centre and at the urban edge, with comparatively little in those suburbs that public transport already serves. This may reinforce car dependence in a city which is already bottom decile globally for residential density, street connectivity and access to mixed-use locations."
- **Gap in walkable options.** "Auckland sees one of the steepest walkability gaps between central and outer suburbs, in terms of the choice that can be accessed in a 15-minute walk. Benchmarked against other cities, it is also more effective for city centre trips than for suburb-to-suburb journeys."
- **Welcome and inclusion have slipped.** Auckland is not standing out globally for spreading opportunity, minority welcome or cultural satisfaction; the public and community housing sector is small relative to need and to many high-performing liveable cities.
- **Opportunity and hardship unevenly shared.** Pacific and Māori unemployment continues to sit well above the Auckland average, as does the share of young Māori and Pacific people not in employment, education or training.

**2030 signals to watch:** house-price-to-income multiple continuing to improve relative to peers; the Pacific and Māori unemployment gap narrowing significantly; and "suburb-to-suburb job and amenity access improves relative to suburb-to-city access by 2030, so the network works across the whole city."
