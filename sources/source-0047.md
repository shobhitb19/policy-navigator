The primary source behind the Treasury's Auckland Story frontier-firms material (claim-0233).
Zheng uses the Longitudinal Business Database (909,033 firm-year observations, 2000–2012)
to define national and local productivity frontiers — firms above the weighted 90th
percentile of firm-level MFP in each industry — and tests how fast lagging firms converge
toward each. The central finding is that convergence to the *local* frontier is faster and
more pervasive than convergence to the *national* frontier: knowledge diffusion within New
Zealand is geographically localised. Firms are defined over labour market regions built
from Census travel-to-work data.

<a id="abstract"></a>
## Abstract

> "Results show that New Zealand firms have faster productivity convergence towards the local frontier compared to the national frontier, suggesting that knowledge diffusion is geographically localised to some extent. … Results show a positive link between domestic tradability and productivity convergence to the national frontier, suggesting that firms that operate across larger markets have greater potential to learn from firms at the national productivity frontier within the New Zealand economy." (p. iii)

<a id="frontier-spatial-distribution"></a>
## Spatial distribution of the national frontier

> "The three major cities of Greater Auckland, Greater Christchurch and Wellington account for 48% of the total firm population and 63% of total employment years in the country (Figure 2). Consistent with agglomeration benefits, the spatial concentration of firms at the national frontier is even more marked with 52% of frontier firms and 73% of employment in firms at the national frontier clustered in these three cities (Figure 3)." (pp. 4–5)

By sector: the primary-sector frontier is predominantly rural (Central North Island,
Gisborne-Napier-Wairarapa); the goods-producing frontier clusters in urban and semi-urban
regions (Greater Auckland, Southland-Otago, Taranaki); and "for the services sector,
national frontier firms are disproportionally clustered in New Zealand's three major
cities" (Figure 4).

<a id="frontier-growth-dynamics"></a>
## Frontier growth dynamics

> "The productivity of firms at the national frontier expands at an average annual rate of 0.77%. This is almost 1.6 times faster than the productivity growth of firms at the local productivity frontier and 2.2 times faster than the productivity growth of laggards. Laggard firms have not kept pace with the productivity performance of firms at the national frontier. As a result, the productivity gap between lagging and national frontier firms has increased over time." (p. 7, Figures 5–6)

<a id="convergence-results"></a>
## Convergence results

All 15 industries show positive productivity catch-up to the local frontier; only nine show
positive convergence to the national frontier (Figures 7–8). Industries with relatively
weak convergence to either frontier — including information media and telecommunications,
rental/hiring/real estate, and other services — account for 54% of private-sector
employment and 69% of gross output. Domestic tradability (the extent to which firms trade
output over geographic distance) is positively linked to convergence toward the national
frontier.
