# Te Waihanga (2022) — The decline of housing supply in New Zealand: Why it happened and how to reverse it

New Zealand Infrastructure Commission / Te Waihanga. (2022, March). *The decline of housing supply in New Zealand: Why it happened and how to reverse it*. Te Waihanga Research Insights series. Wellington: New Zealand Infrastructure Commission / Te Waihanga.

**Full document:** [the-decline-of-housing-supply-in-new-zealand.pdf](the-decline-of-housing-supply-in-new-zealand.pdf)

<a id="cut-to-the-chase"></a>
## Cut to the chase

New Zealand experienced faster growth in real house prices over the preceding 20 years than any other OECD country. The paper argues this reflects slowing housing supply responsiveness, not accelerating demand. Population and incomes grew more rapidly in the mid-20th century than in recent decades, yet house prices have risen faster in recent decades and new construction has slowed.

Econometric analysis shows that a 1% rise in population now causes house prices to increase by ~2.0%, versus ~0.5% in the mid-century period. The paper estimates housing supply elasticity has declined by one-quarter to one-third.

Two structural causes are identified: (1) urban planning policies became more restrictive from the 1970s onward; and (2) urban travel speeds, which rose rapidly as cars were adopted (1930s–1990s), have since declined due to rising congestion.

<a id="introduction"></a>
## Introduction

All large and mid-sized New Zealand cities have median house prices well over five times median household income. The social costs include reduced economic mobility, a growing share of income spent on housing, and poor health from substandard dwellings.

Infrastructure and urban planning are identified as key levers: transport infrastructure expands the locations available for housing development; water and wastewater services enable urban housing; and planning policies can either facilitate or constrain development.

<a id="benchmarking-against-our-past"></a>
## Benchmarking against our past

Using annual data from 1926 to 2018, the paper estimates a reduced-form housing market model following Malpezzi and Maclennan (2001). Key findings:

- **Mid-century (1938–1977):** 1% rise in incomes → ~0.9% rise in house prices; 1% rise in population → ~0.5% rise in house prices.
- **Recent decades (1979–2018):** 1% rise in incomes → ~1.2% rise in house prices; 1% rise in population → ~2.0% rise in house prices.
- **Housing supply elasticity** has declined by roughly one-quarter to one-third between the two periods (Table 7, p.35).
- New Zealand's dwelling stock increased by one-third in the 1950s and by over one-quarter in the 1960s and 1970s — faster than population. In 2010–2018, dwellings increased only 9%, below population growth.

The increase in housing's price sensitivity to demand reflects reduced supply responsiveness, not stronger demand. Population grew faster in the 1940s–1960s than in recent decades.

<a id="explaining-these-patterns"></a>
## Explaining these patterns

### The Alonso-Muth-Mills (AMM) model

The paper uses the AMM urban spatial model (following Bertaud and Brueckner 2005) to analyse the combined effect of planning restrictions (modelled as floor area ratio limits) and travel speeds (modelled as commuting costs) on housing prices and urban form.

Floor area ratio (FAR) limits raise house prices throughout the city by reducing housing supply in accessible locations. Lower transport costs expand the city's footprint and reduce overall prices. The two effects interact: limiting both intensification (via downzoning) and expansion (via congestion) simultaneously squeezes housing supply from both directions.

### Evolution of urban planning in New Zealand

New Zealand's planning history is divided into four stages:
1. **Pre-1926:** Growth without planning.
2. **1926–1953:** Unsuccessful centrally coordinated planning.
3. **1953–1977:** Local government-led planning; relatively permissive for housing.
4. **1977–present:** Consultation-based planning; increasingly restrictive.

**Auckland case study:**
- The 1961 District Scheme provided capacity for ~475,000 people against a 1961 population of 141,900 — more than a 3× ratio.
- The 1970 District Scheme halved capacity to ~250,000; FAR limit fell from ~1.28 to ~0.80.
- The 1981 District Scheme did not meaningfully reverse this; estimated FAR ~0.96.
- Post-RMA plans retained restrictions while adding complexity; plan length in Auckland, Wellington, and Christchurch grew rapidly after 1991.
- The 2016 Auckland Unitary Plan restored significant capacity, allowing central Auckland to grow to ~2.5× current population.

The Resource Management Act 1991 removed specific requirements for infrastructure planning, treating infrastructure as one of many effects to manage rather than as a foundation for growth. Prior legislation (Town and Country Planning Acts 1953, 1977) required local governments to plan infrastructure provision alongside development.

### Evolution of urban travel speeds

Average urban travel speeds in Auckland:
- Rose significantly between 1950 and 1970 as car adoption spread and roads were sealed (urban sealed roads: 20% in 1930 → ~90% in 1970).
- Slowed but continued rising 1970–1990.
- Have declined since the early 1990s due to traffic congestion. Travel speeds on Auckland arterials and motorways fell ~30% on average between 1986 and 2012 despite significant road widening (Vallyon 2013).
- Rising congestion has eroded ~one-third of travel speed gains since 1950.

New road capacity tends to induce additional driving with few sustained benefits for speed. Congestion pricing (as in Singapore and Stockholm) and new transport options are identified as the most promising approaches to reversing speed decline.

### Model calibration and results

AMM model calibrated to Auckland conditions and run for two historical periods:

**1937–1977 (baseline):** Population growth 241%; income growth 47%; travel speeds +50%; FAR limit 1.28. Model predicts 96% house price growth (observed: 113%) and 189% increase in built-up area (observed: 230%). Close fit.

**1978–2018 (baseline):** Population growth 78%; income growth 24%; travel speeds −11%; FAR limit 0.88. Model predicts 262% house price growth (observed: 251%) and 22% increase in built-up area (observed: 42%). Close fit.

**Counterfactual scenarios:**
- Avoiding 1970s/80s downzoning (FAR = 1.28): predicted price growth 156% vs 262% (−41%).
- Avoiding declining travel speeds (speeds +7% vs −11%): predicted price growth 153% (−42%).
- Avoiding both: predicted price growth 80% (−69%). Auckland prices would have been roughly half as high in 2018.

<a id="conclusions"></a>
## Conclusions

The paper recommends four responses, consistent with Te Waihanga's Infrastructure Strategy:

1. **Write plans that allow cities to grow and change** — standardise and liberalise zoning; plans should provide capacity for ~3× current population, as they did pre-1970s.
2. **Restore the link between planning and infrastructure** — regional spatial plans should be backed by funding plans and council zoning rulebooks.
3. **Make different choices to lift urban accessibility** — congestion pricing to reverse travel speed decline; deploy new transport options.
4. **Provide different infrastructure to serve changing cities** — address constraints on three-waters capacity in inner suburbs as intensification proceeds; monitor work-from-home impacts on infrastructure demand.
