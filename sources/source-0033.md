<a id="section-3-localization-test"></a>
## Section III: The localization test

The paper uses USPTO patent citation data to test whether knowledge spillovers are geographically localised. The key methodological innovation is constructing a control set: for each citing patent, a matched control patent is selected from the same technology class and application year as the cited patent but without a citation link. Comparing the geographic location of citing patents to control patents isolates the effect of the knowledge transfer. Geographic co-location is assessed at two levels: US state and metropolitan statistical area (MSA). Citing patents are substantially more likely to be located in the same state or MSA as the cited patent than the matched control patents, providing strong evidence that knowledge spillovers are geographically bounded. → [claim-0200](../claims/claim-0200.md)

<a id="section-4-results"></a>
## Section IV: Main results

The paper finds that within the United States, a citing patent is approximately 5–10 times more likely to originate from the same MSA as the cited patent than a randomly selected patent in the same technology field. At the state level, the localization effect is also large and statistically significant. These effects are present across technology fields but vary in magnitude — they are strongest in pharmaceuticals and medical technology and somewhat weaker in mechanical technologies. The authors also find that national borders act as a strong barrier to knowledge spillovers: US patents are far more likely to cite other US patents than would be predicted by the share of world patenting done in the US. → [claim-0200](../claims/claim-0200.md), [claim-0201](../claims/claim-0201.md)

<a id="section-5-time-decay"></a>
## Section V: Time decay

The paper tests whether localisation decays over time — the hypothesis being that once knowledge has been incorporated into the public domain (via publication, imitation, or labour mobility), it becomes less location-specific. The results are mixed: there is some evidence of decay over longer horizons (10–15 years after the patent is granted), but the core finding of strong localisation persists even for older cited patents. The authors conclude that while there is modest fading, knowledge spillovers remain geographically constrained for much longer than a simple diffusion model would predict. → [claim-0202](../claims/claim-0202.md)
