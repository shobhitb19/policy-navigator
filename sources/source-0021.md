# Koi Tū (2026) — People, place and prosperity: The case for a population strategy

Lala, G., Spoonley, P., & Gluckman, P. (2026, April). *People, place and prosperity: The case for a population strategy*. Koi Tū Centre for Informed Futures. ISBN 978-1-0671045-8-0.

**Full document:** [Koi-Tu_People-place-and-prosperity-report_April-2026.pdf](Koi-Tu_People-place-and-prosperity-report_April-2026.pdf)

---

<a id="executive-summary"></a>
## Executive summary

New Zealand is at a demographic inflection point. The paper identifies five converging trends: slowing population growth, declining fertility, growing reliance on immigration, rapid population ageing, and growing ethnic and cultural diversity. Each trend has distinct policy implications; taken together, they necessitate a long-term, bipartisan population strategy.

The paper argues that New Zealand's current policy landscape — dominated by three-year election cycles and fragmented sector-by-sector responses — is structurally ill-suited to addressing demographic challenges whose costs arrive decades after the required investments. A new population commission is proposed as the lead governance vehicle.

---

<a id="section-1-population-growth"></a>
## Section 1: Slowing population growth

New Zealand's population is forecast to grow from approximately 5.34 million in 2025 to 6.53 million by 2048, based on Stats NZ 50th-percentile projections (Stats NZ, 2025).

Annual population growth has already slowed sharply: from 1.7% (June 2023–June 2024) to 0.7% (June 2024–June 2025), driven by falling fertility and net emigration of New Zealand citizens.

**Regional divergence:** Metro areas (Auckland, Christchurch, Hamilton — the three fastest-growing city council areas as of 2025) are stabilising internal migration and growing primarily via international immigration. Provincial areas are seeing steady growth, primarily driven by international migration (changing their social identity). Rural areas have experienced population decline since 2019, with ongoing hollowing-out as younger adults migrate to cities.

**Infrastructure demand:** The New Zealand Infrastructure Commission estimates that infrastructure investment demand will increase from approximately $20 billion in 2025 to slightly more than $30 billion by the 2050s (in 2023 NZD terms) — equivalent to approximately 5.8% of GDP annually over 30 years (Sense Partners, 2022, as cited in NZ Infrastructure Commission, 2025).

**Dependency ratio:** New Zealand's dependency ratio (ratio of non-working-age dependents to working-age population) is forecast to rise from 54% in 2025 to 73% in 2073 (Stats NZ projections).

<a id="section-2-fertility"></a>
## Section 2: Declining fertility

New Zealand's total fertility rate (TFR) declined rapidly since roughly 2014, reaching 1.55 in 2025 — below the replacement rate of 2.1. In 1990, 60% of women in New Zealand worked; by 2024, that number had increased to 80%, contributing to later and fewer births.

**Drivers:** increased female education and workforce participation; housing costs and cost of living (one-third of low-income households spending over 40% of income on housing); inadequate paid parental leave (maximum $780/week, shared between parents); declining community-based child-rearing support.

**Labour force implications:** NZ labour force expected to grow from roughly 3 million to 4 million between 2024 and 2053; from 2045 onwards, age-structure changes contribute to average decline of approximately 4,800 people per year. BusinessNZ predicts a labour shortage of 250,000 people by 2045. Overall labour force participation rate is forecast to fall from 70.5% (2021) to 63.22% (2073).

**Primary school rolls** are forecast to decrease by 7% (approximately 36,700 children) from 2024 to 2034; secondary school rolls to decrease by 6% (approximately 20,000 children) from 2026 to 2034.

<a id="section-3-immigration"></a>
## Section 3: Growing reliance on immigration

With falling fertility, immigration is becoming the dominant source of both workforce and population growth. New Zealand's immigration operates almost entirely through a formal points-based system (shared with Australia and Canada). In 2021, approximately half of all residence approvals were in skilled migrant or business visa categories.

**Key sectoral dependence:** International medical graduates made up 43% of the NZ specialist workforce; migrants made up 40% of the 5,000 nurses employed in aged care.

**2024 net emigration spike:** 2024 saw the largest net migration loss of New Zealand citizens in 25 years, totalling approximately 44,000 people. This compounded the fertility-driven workforce shrinkage.

**Immigration and productivity:** The New Zealand Productivity Commission (2022) concluded that immigration is neither the cause nor the solution to New Zealand's productivity challenge. Workforce growth (including via immigration) must be supported by improvements in capital intensity and innovation. Immigration that outpaces investment in infrastructure and housing can decrease productivity.

**Global talent competition:** New Zealand fell from 11th to 18th in the Global Talent Competitiveness Index (GTCI) since 2015. In 2025, New Zealand scored below the high-income group average across all six GTCI pillars (enable, attract, grow, retain, vocational and technical skills, generalist adaptive skills). Vocational and technical skills were ranked 58th out of 135 countries; ability to attract talent ranked 23rd.

**Brain drain/gain:** Flow of permanent migrants into NZ has been more skilled on average than the local population for at least 30 years. However, skill levels of departing NZ citizens are poorly documented; the reality may be closer to "brain exchange" than net brain drain.

<a id="section-4-ageing"></a>
## Section 4: An ageing population

By 2048, 22% of New Zealand's population will be over 65, up from 17% in 2025. Growth will be particularly pronounced in the over-85 cohort ("old-old"). Several regions will exceed the hyper-ageing threshold of 30% aged 65+ by 2048: Tasman, Marlborough, Nelson, and West Coast.

**Fiscal implications:** The New Zealand government spends approximately five times as much on the average person aged 65+ as on the average person under 65 (Treasury, 2025). Cost of NZ Superannuation forecast to rise from 5.1% to 8% of GDP by 2065. In the 1960s, there were seven New Zealand workers aged 15–64 for every person over 65; by 2025, four; by 2065, the number is anticipated to reach two.

Overall labour force participation rate forecast to fall from 70.5% (2021) to 63.22% by 2073, even with increasing participation of 65-to-69-year-olds (which rose from 10% in 1993 to 49% in 2025).

<a id="section-5-diversity"></a>
## Section 5: Growing ethnic and cultural diversity

New Zealand's ethnic composition is projected to change substantially by 2043:
- NZ European Pākehā: 65% (2025) → 55% (2043)
- Asian: 21% (2025) → 31% (2043)
- Māori: 17% (2025) → 19% (2043)
- Pacific: 9% (2025) → 11% (2043)

These changes will be more pronounced among younger age groups.

**Auckland's unique demography:** Auckland's transformation is faster and more pronounced. By 2043: 42% of Auckland will identify as Asian (vs 31% NZ-wide); 45% NZ European Pākehā (minority in the city, vs 55% nationally); 18% Pacific; 13% Māori. Auckland will be a majority non-Pākehā city before the early 2040s.

Ethnic minority communities contributed approximately $50 billion to Auckland's GDP in 2023 (Eaqub & Sohanpal, 2026).

**Economic benefits of diversity:** A study of 12 European countries found that "innovation levels are positively associated with cultural diversity of the migrant community" (Ozgen, Nijkamp, & Poot, 2011). The Royal Commission of Inquiry into the Christchurch mosque attacks (2020) noted that diversity benefits include higher productivity and innovation for regions/cities with large immigrant populations, cross-fertilisation of ideas, and increased aggregate demand.

**Risk:** 16% of all New Zealanders reported having experienced racial discrimination in their lifetime (Ministry of Health, 2025 NZ Health Survey). Growing ethnic diversity can generate anxiety when economic opportunities are perceived to be unevenly distributed.

<a id="population-strategy-rationale"></a>
## Population strategy rationale

The paper defines population strategy as both actions to understand and modify demographic trends AND actions to manage their consequences. A strategy's value lies in three areas:

1. **Elevating key demographic topics above electoral-cycle contestation** — immigration, superannuation, and infrastructure are consistently under-planned because parties optimise for short-term political wins.
2. **Enabling long-term, bipartisan planning** — citing Singapore and Denmark as examples where successive governments commit to multi-decade strategies rather than undoing predecessor work.
3. **Enabling cross-sector coordination** — currently, immigration policy has no role for local authorities, despite the direct implications for regional infrastructure, housing, and services.

The paper calls for a new population commission (or equivalent agency at DPMC/Treasury level) to lead strategy development, with engagement from research institutions, industry, and civil society.

**Six elements proposed for a population strategy:** societal resilience, human capital investment, workforce planning (including immigration settings), infrastructure planning (housing, transport, aged-care), investment and economic planning (productivity alternatives to population growth), and spatial/regional planning.
