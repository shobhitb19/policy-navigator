# 09: Demographics and Population Strategy

## Summary

New Zealand is at a demographic inflection point. Fertility has fallen to 1.55 (2025) — well below the replacement rate of 2.1. Population growth has slowed sharply from 1.7% per year (2023–24) to 0.7% (2024–25), as net migration normalises after the post-pandemic surge. Population is simultaneously concentrating spatially — Auckland, Christchurch, and Hamilton are growing while rural areas have been shrinking since 2019 — and becoming more ethnically diverse, with Auckland projected to be majority non-Pākehā before the early 2040s. These are not independent trends: they interact in ways that create compounding challenges for the labour force, the fiscal base, infrastructure planning, and spatial strategy.

The labour supply dimension is the most acute in the near term. BusinessNZ projects a labour shortage of 250,000 workers by 2045. The labour force participation rate is projected to fall from 70.5% (2021) to 63.2% by 2073 as the workforce ages. Immigration can partially offset workforce decline but — as NZ Productivity Commission (2022) analysis found — does not automatically raise productivity per worker, and can reduce it if growth outpaces infrastructure and capital investment. The policy response must pair workforce maintenance strategies (immigration, participation rates, Māori and Pacific labour market inclusion) with the capital investment and technology diffusion needed to raise output per worker — not choose between them.

The fiscal and infrastructure dimension plays out over a longer horizon but with larger cumulative stakes. The dependency ratio (workers to dependants) is projected to rise from 54% (2025) to 73% (2073). NZS cost is projected to rise from 5.1% to 8% of GDP by 2065. At the same time, Te Waihanga's own Forward Guidance now projects infrastructure investment demand rising from just over $20bn/year to more than $40bn/year by the 2050s — a 2026 figure that supersedes an earlier ~$30bn estimate — against a backdrop of NZ already spending ~5.8% of GDP annually on infrastructure for two decades while ranking near the bottom of the OECD on investment efficiency. These pressures compound in a way that cannot be resolved through any single instrument: they require a coordinated, cross-cycle strategy that sequences fiscal settings, infrastructure investment, and spatial planning over a multi-decade horizon.

The ethnic diversification dimension is both a challenge and an underutilised asset. Ethnic minority communities contributed NZ$50bn to Auckland's GDP in 2023. Auckland's growing cosmopolitan character creates potential advantages in FDI attraction, international trade networks, and innovation — advantages that comparable polycentric city-regions (Singapore, Dublin, Amsterdam) have explicitly leveraged. But realising this potential requires inclusion infrastructure: credential recognition, language access, ethnic network development, and spatial access to high-productivity clusters — not just the absence of discrimination.

## The three strategic tensions

**1. Labour supply vs. productivity:** Falling fertility and ageing reduce the workforce. Immigration can partially offset workforce decline but does not automatically raise productivity per worker — and can reduce it if growth outpaces infrastructure. Quantitative SVAR evidence corroborates this: an expansionary immigration shock significantly raises GDP, participation, and employment, but leaves labour productivity largely unmoved, while also raising house prices and household credit — pointing to demand-side, not productivity-side, absorption pressure. The policy response must pair workforce maintenance (immigration, participation rates) with the capital investment and technology diffusion needed to raise output per worker. (See [claim-0154](../claims/claim-0154.md), [claim-0156](../claims/claim-0156.md), [claim-0161](../claims/claim-0161.md), [claim-0079](../claims/claim-0079.md))

**2. Infrastructure demand vs. fiscal capacity:** A growing-but-ageing population requires more infrastructure (housing, health, aged care, transport) while simultaneously eroding the tax base needed to fund it. The dependency ratio shift (54% → 73%) and NZS cost trajectory (5.1% → 8% of GDP) are fiscally severe. The infrastructure investment gap — Te Waihanga's own Forward Guidance projects spending roughly doubling, from ~$20bn to >$40bn/year, by the 2050s — compounds this, especially given NZ's existing bottom-of-OECD investment efficiency. (See [claim-0119](../claims/claim-0119.md), [claim-0118](../claims/claim-0118.md), [claim-0094](../claims/claim-0094.md))

**3. Urban concentration vs. regional equity:** Population is concentrating in metro areas while rural areas shrink. Concentrating investment in metros maximises agglomeration returns but strands regional communities and their existing infrastructure. A spatial population strategy must specify which investment follows people (to metros) and which is maintained for declining communities (in rural areas). This divergence is reinforced by a documented household/firm sorting tension: households are drawn to high-amenity, often smaller or non-metro locations (sun, water proximity, low congestion), while firms are drawn to large cities for agglomeration benefits — so population and economic activity do not necessarily concentrate in the same places for the same reasons. (See [claim-0091](../claims/claim-0091.md), [claim-0178](../claims/claim-0178.md), [claim-0182](../claims/claim-0182.md), [claim-0185](../claims/claim-0185.md))

## Open questions

- Can New Zealand sustain the infrastructure investment required (rising from ~$20bn to >$40bn/year by the 2050s, ~5.8% of GDP/year historically) within current fiscal settings, or does it require revenue reform?
- How should infrastructure investment be spatially allocated as rural populations shrink and metro populations concentrate?
- What is the right governance vehicle for a long-term population strategy — a new population commission, DPMC, or Treasury?
- How will AI and automation interact with the labour shortage projections — substituting for missing workers or compounding structural unemployment?
- Can ethnic diversity be translated into FDI and trade advantages (as Auckland's cosmopolitan positioning suggests), and what governance structures are needed?
