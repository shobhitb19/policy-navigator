# 01: Urbanisation and Spatial Systems

## Summary

Cities are the primary locus of economic activity in most countries. OECD metro areas cover roughly 4% of land, house 50% of population, and generate 55% of GDP — and contributed approximately half of all OECD GDP growth between 2000 and 2010. This concentration is not accidental: it reflects the fundamental economics of proximity — labour market pooling, specialised input sharing, and knowledge spillovers between workers and firms that compound at scale.

Urban hierarchies matter as much as city size alone. A primate city that is too large relative to the national system can suppress aggregate growth: Henderson (2003) finds that a 15-percentage-point increase in urban primacy is associated with roughly a 1.5pp annual penalty on GDP per capita growth. Polycentric systems — where two or three large cities specialise and compete — tend to outperform monocentric ones at comparable national income levels. The canonical benchmarks are Switzerland and the Netherlands: networked urban systems that generate agglomeration returns across multiple centres rather than concentrating them in one.

New Zealand is 84% urban (World Bank, 2024) but its system is distinctively monocentric. Auckland accounts for 37.9% of GDP with 35.5% of the workforce, but its GDP per capita premium over the national average is only 13% — far below the 25–35% typical for primate cities in comparable small-to-medium economies. The second tier (Wellington, Christchurch, Hamilton, Tauranga) is thin. This structure limits aggregate agglomeration returns and creates an unusual policy challenge: the country is urbanised enough that most future productivity growth must come from improving performance within existing cities, not from structural urbanisation.

Population dynamics are adding a new layer of complexity. NZ's growth rate has slowed sharply (1.7% → 0.7% per year between 2023–24 and 2024–25), rural areas are already shrinking, and population is concentrating into a small number of growth metros. These trends shift the central question from how to urbanise to how to spatially prioritise within an already-urbanised but under-productive system.

## Key themes

### Why cities generate productivity premiums
Geographic concentration enables three mechanisms: sharing (infrastructure, specialised suppliers, thick labour markets), matching (deeper labour markets improve firm-worker fit), and learning (face-to-face contact accelerates knowledge spillovers). These operate at different spatial scales — some benefits accrue within 10km (daily labour markets), others over 200–300km (city-to-hinterland knowledge diffusion). They also interact: a transit-connected city enables sharing and matching that a car-dependent city of the same nominal size cannot.

### Urban hierarchy design
Whether cities are arranged monocentrically or polycentrically affects aggregate growth, not just distributional questions. Leapfrog urban development — where new growth bypasses connected zones — fragments urban form and destroys the interaction density on which agglomeration depends. Major African cities 2000–2010 saw >40% of new built-up area as leapfrog, generating disconnected cities with roughly 40% fewer potential face-to-face interactions. A city that is nominally large but internally fragmented does not realise agglomeration returns. The lesson for NZ is that the thickness of the second urban tier is as important as the size of the first — a monocentric system that can only route agglomeration through Auckland is inherently more brittle than a polycentric one.

### New Zealand's spatial productivity puzzle
Auckland's measured productivity premium is surprisingly modest given its scale dominance. Labour productivity is 11.1% above the rest of NZ — consistent with theory — but multi-factor productivity after controlling for worker quality is only 2.2% above other urban areas (Maré 2016). The GDP per capita premium (13%) is far below international peers. Reasons include dispersed land use constraining density-driven spillovers, infrastructure deficits that raise effective commute times, capital stock per worker at ~50% of OECD comparators, and a thin second tier that forces all national agglomeration through a single node. This is not a minor underperformance: if Auckland achieved the primate-city premium typical of its peer group (25–35%), NZ's national GDP would be materially higher.

### Population divergence and spatial priorities
As rural populations shrink and urban populations concentrate, spatial investment allocation becomes a strategic rather than technical question. Continuing to fund rural infrastructure at historical levels maintains equity but consumes fiscal capacity that could generate higher returns in growth metros. Concentrating investment in cities maximises productivity but strands regional communities and their existing assets. NZ has not yet institutionalised a cross-agency mechanism for making these allocations deliberately and transparently — a gap that becomes more costly as demographic divergence accelerates.
