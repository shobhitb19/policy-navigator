# 05: Transport and Accessibility

## Summary

Transport investment shapes who has access to economic opportunity. The mechanism is not simply commuting convenience: transport determines the effective catchment area of the labour market. A city where residents can reach employment centres within 30 minutes by transit has a fundamentally larger and deeper labour market than one where the same trip takes 75 minutes or requires a car. Deeper labour markets enable better job matching — raising both worker wages and firm productivity through improved allocation of talent to tasks. This is the primary productivity mechanism of transit investment, not a co-benefit.

The evidence on transit investment returns supports this mechanism. Halving travel time between a large city and its hinterland is associated with 0.2–0.4pp higher annual GDP per capita growth in the hinterland. Mexico City metro expansion generated a 20% real income gain for workers near new stations relative to a perfectly efficient labour market — and informality fell by 4 percentage points near new stations, indicating that transit connectivity directly enables formal labour market participation. Formal employment more than doubles with each doubling of market potential within 10km in developing-city evidence, where connectivity — not raw density — drives employment growth.

The distributional case is strong and partially independent of the aggregate productivity argument. Workers without cars depend disproportionately on transit: low-income households, recent migrants, the elderly, and the young face constrained job access when transit is poor or unaffordable. Auckland's transport poverty is concentrated among Māori and Pasifika households in south and west Auckland, where PT coverage is weakest and the gap between fares and household income is highest; the city's public transport fares were the third highest globally in 2018. Bogotá's TransMilenio BRT reduced average commute times by 15 minutes, with proportionally larger gains for low-income users who had previously faced the longest trips.

Transport investment and land use must be planned together. Density without transit increases congestion rather than reducing emissions or generating agglomeration benefits. Transit investment without complementary upzoning around stations fails to generate the ridership needed to justify operating costs. The order of intervention matters: committing infrastructure ahead of zoning change enables private investment to follow; reversing the sequence leaves stations in low-density corridors without the residential catchment to sustain them.

## Key themes

### Labour market catchment as the core mechanism
The economic value of transit investment is primarily realised through labour market expansion, not through congestion reduction alone. A 30-minute transit catchment reaches far more workers and jobs than a 75-minute one; the accessible job count difference can be an order of magnitude in a radial city. Better transit is associated with higher wages for workers near new stations, higher firm productivity, and faster employment growth in transit-served corridors. The cost-benefit case for urban transit investment should lead with this mechanism, not with decongestion benefits, which are partially offset by induced demand.

### Transport poverty is geographically concentrated
In Auckland, the geography of transit under-service maps closely onto the geography of disadvantage. South and west Auckland — where Māori and Pasifika communities are concentrated — have the longest commutes to CBD and airport employment clusters, weakest PT coverage, and highest car dependence at the lowest rates of car ownership. This is a structural product of historical investment priorities (radial motorway over orbital transit) that creates compounding disadvantage: residents of underserved areas face higher transport costs as a share of income, fewer accessible jobs within a given commute time budget, and — when transit is genuinely unavailable — effective exclusion from labour markets they could otherwise access.

### The transit-density feedback loop
Transit investment creates land value uplift near stations, increasing development pressure. If zoning allows upzoning in response, higher density increases transit ridership, which improves route financial sustainability. If zoning prevents it, the land value gain is captured by existing owners without generating the ridership or density that would justify the investment. This feedback means transport investment and zoning reform should be designed jointly. NZ's Medium Density Residential Standards and NPS-UD, which apply upzoning within walking distance of transit stations, begin to institutionalise this link — though implementation has been uneven across councils.

### Auckland's uncompleted feedback loop
Auckland is the clearest available case of the transit-density loop failing to close. The city built rapid transit capacity — the Northern Busway, the electrified rail network, and now the City Rail Link — but, in the 2026 State of the City assessment, "the population living along better connected public transport corridors has grown much slower" than in comparable cities. New housing went to the city centre and the urban edge instead. The result is that transit investment has not been matched by the ridership catchment that justifies it, and intensification has not been matched by the access that would make it productive.

The consequences show up on both sides of the ledger. The city centre generates more than 20% of regional GDP but a smaller share of Aucklanders live within a convenient commute of it than in most comparable cities — so the effective thickness of the country's deepest labour market is narrower than its 160,000 jobs suggest. And Auckland remains bottom 5% worldwide on congestion outcomes, with the burden "spread more evenly across the whole city than most" — a signature of dispersed origins and destinations rather than a radial commuting problem, and a warning that centre-focused transit investment alone will not clear it.

The City Rail Link is the natural test. The report expects it to bring the commutable catchment share "much closer to peer benchmarks", and sets a 2030 marker of more than a million residents within 45 minutes door-to-door of the centre. Whether that materialises depends on decisions outside the rail project: feeder bus frequency and reliability, and above all whether growth is directed to the new and existing rail catchments. The report is explicit that this is "a key litmus test of the City Rail Link" — infrastructure delivery and land-use response are being assessed as one intervention, not two.

### NZ transport priorities
NZ's transport investment debate is dominated by the roading-vs-transit choice. The aggregate productivity evidence favours transit in dense urban corridors where road capacity faces sharply diminishing returns: Auckland's road congestion already costs ~$1.3bn/year, and additional road capacity induces demand rather than clearing it. The equity evidence favours transit across the board. Within transit investment, the orbital-vs-radial priority is less settled: radial routes serve the largest existing market (CBD commuting) but orbital routes serve suburb-to-suburb and cross-town movement that is increasingly important as employment decentralises. Pricing reform is the underused lever in this debate: Te Waihanga's 2026 modelling suggests time-of-use road charging in Auckland could cut excess congestion delay by ~35% while requiring ~20% less new capital investment than the equivalent road-building approach — though current national road pricing tools (fuel excise, road user charges) are not designed to reflect localised congestion. Implementation is also contested politically: the Green Party's response to the National Infrastructure Plan argues the Government Policy Statement on Land Transport 2024–2027 still directs disproportionate new capital expenditure to state highways relative to renewals and maintenance, in tension with the Plan's own prioritisation recommendations.
