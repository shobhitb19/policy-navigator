# 07: Resilience, Environment, and Sustainability

## Summary

Urban form has large consequences for environmental outcomes. Compact, transit-oriented cities produce fewer greenhouse gas emissions per capita than sprawling, car-dependent ones. The Atlanta-Barcelona contrast is an extreme but representative case: Atlanta at 6 people per hectare generates roughly ten times the transport CO₂ of Barcelona at 176 people per hectare. This relationship holds consistently across OECD city samples. Doubling density reduces energy consumption by 7% and pollution by 8%, while also reducing the unit cost of local public services by 14.4% (Ahlfeldt & Pietrostefani 2019 meta-synthesis). Compact urban form is simultaneously an environmental, fiscal, and productivity strategy.

The density-emissions relationship is not universal. In low and lower-middle income cities — which are already denser on average than high-income cities — the inverse relationship reverses: denser poor cities have higher, not lower, GHG emissions. This is because density in that context means overcrowding without transit infrastructure, clean energy, or insulated buildings. The emissions benefit of density is conditional on the quality of infrastructure and energy systems that accompany it. Transit-oriented densification in a city with clean electricity is a climate intervention; densification without transit or clean energy is not.

Dense cities also concentrate climate risk in ways that are qualitatively different from dispersed ones. Urban heat islands — where urban surfaces absorb and retain more heat — are more severe with higher surface coverage. Flood risk concentrates when impermeable surfaces increase runoff and when high asset value accumulates in low-lying areas. Critical infrastructure interdependencies (water, power, communications) create cascading failure risks when nodes are disrupted. Building climate resilience into dense urban form requires design standards, investment in green infrastructure (urban forests, permeable surfaces, distributed flood storage), and risk disclosure in property markets that allows households and firms to make informed location decisions.

Metropolitan governance is the common enabling factor for both emissions reduction and resilience investment. Sprawl control, transit investment, building standards, and flood risk management all require coordination across municipal boundaries and across current and future residents. A city that cannot govern its land use consistently cannot plan its climate adaptation either.

## Key themes

### Density and carbon emissions: the mechanism
The primary channel is transport: compact cities reduce origin-to-destination distances, making transit economical and reducing average trip length. Per-capita ground-transport CO₂ falls consistently as urban density rises across OECD cities. The policy implication is that zoning, land use, and transport investment decisions are climate decisions: when a city permits low-density greenfield development far from transit, it is locking in decades of car-dependent travel. The GHG consequence is not a side effect — it is a structural outcome of the built environment decision.

### Climate risk is spatially heterogeneous
Not all parts of a city face the same climate risk. Coastal flooding, riverine flooding, urban heat islands, and geotechnical instability each concentrate in specific geographies that are, in principle, knowable in advance. The policy question is how land use regulation and insurance markets respond to that information. If land use restrictions prevent development in high-risk areas, risk-exposed communities are protected at the cost of constrained supply. If insurance markets price risk accurately, property values in high-risk areas fall — potentially creating affordability windows that concentrate disadvantaged households in the highest-risk locations. This creates an equity problem nested within a climate problem.

### Resilience investment has the same governance problem as infrastructure investment
Climate resilience investment has costs borne now and benefits arriving over decades, under conditions of genuine uncertainty about the timing and severity of climate impacts. This makes it structurally under-supplied by short-cycle electoral institutions in the same way as long-horizon infrastructure. The institutional mechanisms needed — independent long-term planning bodies, cross-cycle revenue commitments, mandatory risk disclosure in property transactions — are largely the same. Climate resilience is not a separate policy domain requiring separate governance; it is best treated as a constraint on the same long-horizon spatial investment decisions that infrastructure planning already requires.
