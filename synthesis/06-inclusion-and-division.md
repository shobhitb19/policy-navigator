# 06: Inclusion and Division

## Summary

Cities concentrate both opportunity and disadvantage. Agglomeration raises average productivity and wages, but the distribution of these gains is not spatially even: workers and households sort into neighbourhoods by income, ethnicity, and access, and these sorting patterns can persist for generations. Disadvantaged households concentrated in areas with poor services, weak transit, and low job density face compounded barriers — not just low income but constrained access to the labour market, schools, and health services that would enable mobility.

Spatial sorting becomes a poverty trap when neighbourhood characteristics are themselves determined by prior deprivation. Poor areas receive less public infrastructure investment, which reduces service quality, which depresses property values, which limits local fiscal capacity, which further constrains investment. Breaking this cycle requires simultaneous interventions across multiple dimensions — not just housing upgrades, not just transport, not just employment programmes, but all three in sequence. South Africa's post-apartheid housing programme illustrates the failure mode: mass housing built on city peripheries without transport connections reproduced spatial division even while delivering formal shelter.

Infrastructure sequencing is one of the clearest findings in the inclusion evidence. Early infrastructure investment determines which neighbourhoods become desirable for generations: Dar es Salaam's 1970s sites-and-services areas have the highest city land values today, while retrospectively upgraded informal settlements have lower values despite equivalent physical upgrading. The implication is that retrofitting poor infrastructure is far more expensive than getting sequencing right — and that early decisions about where to run water, roads, and transit commit cities to neighbourhood quality trajectories that persist for 50+ years. Formal rights to land — tenure security, title, minimum lot requirements set at affordable levels — operate through a similar mechanism: they enable household investment in housing quality and use property as collateral, with measurable effects on female labour supply and children's human capital.

New Zealand faces two distinct inclusion challenges. The first is established: Māori and Pasifika communities in south and west Auckland have weaker labour market access (poor transit, fewer nearby jobs, lower vehicle ownership) and face barriers that reduce participation below potential. The second is emerging: Auckland is transforming rapidly in ethnic composition (majority non-Pākehā before the early 2040s, 42% Asian by 2043), and ethnic minority communities already contributed NZ$50bn to Auckland's GDP in 2023. Converting this diversity into sustained economic advantage — through FDI networks, trade relationships, and innovation — requires inclusion infrastructure beyond anti-discrimination policy: credential recognition, language access, and network-building that activates the economic potential of an increasingly cosmopolitan city. OECD survey data confirm that NZ already ranks among the highest in the OECD on combined qualification and field-of-study mismatch — a direct consequence of its high foreign-born share and weak credential recognition — with measurable wage penalties that suppress both household incomes and aggregate productivity.

## Key themes

### Spatial poverty traps: formation and persistence
Spatial poverty traps arise from residential sorting combined with neighbourhood feedback effects. Households with lower incomes concentrate in areas where housing is cheaper — typically areas with weaker services and longer commutes to employment. Lower-income neighbourhoods have less political voice, so public investment is lower. Reduced investment depresses school quality, health services, and transit access, reinforcing sorting. The result is that spatial inequality compounds over time even without active discrimination — the mechanism is market sorting plus a public investment feedback. Interventions that address only one dimension (housing or transport or employment) are consistently less effective than integrated approaches; the evidence from slum upgrading programmes is that targeted interventions applied without prior institutional foundations tend to fail.

### Infrastructure sequencing determines neighbourhood trajectories
The timing of infrastructure investment matters more than the quantum. Neighbourhoods that received planned infrastructure early — roads, water, sewerage, transit — before informal settlement became entrenched ended up with high land values and stable communities. Neighbourhoods upgraded after informal settlement was established ended up with lower values even after equivalent physical investment, because the street network, lot sizes, and building setbacks were already fixed in a configuration that limited future development capacity. The policy implication is forward investment in growth corridors — committing infrastructure before growth arrives — rather than reactive upgrading of areas already established in low-quality form.

### Land tenure and formal rights as inclusion levers
Insecurity of land tenure limits investment by households and firms alike. Without formal title, households cannot use property as collateral, are vulnerable to eviction, and have limited incentive to improve their dwellings. Women's land rights in particular affect labour supply, savings, and children's human capital investment. Conversely, minimum lot-size requirements that are unaffordable for low-income households push them into informal settlement, removing regulatory protection while generating squatter formation dynamics. The policy design challenge is formal systems that simultaneously grant rights and set standards that low-income households can afford to meet — a balance that most developing-city zoning frameworks fail.

### Skills mismatch as inclusion failure
Skills mismatch — overqualification, underqualification, and field-of-study misalignment — is an inclusion issue as well as a productivity issue. At the individual level, overqualified workers earn approximately 14% less than well-matched peers with the same qualifications and proficiency, and a 1 SD increase in literacy proficiency is worth roughly 6% in hourly wages across the OECD, independent of education level (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Ch. 5). At the aggregate level, high mismatch raises unemployment and reduces GDP growth through systematic waste of human capital.

NZ's high foreign-born share (>25% of adults, one of three highest in the OECD alongside Australia and Canada) creates structural mismatch pressure through credential non-recognition: skilled migrants are displaced into roles below their qualification level, imposing individual wage penalties and suppressing city-level productivity. NZ already ranks among the OECD leaders on combined qualification and field-of-study mismatch (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Ch. 5). Addressing this requires active credential recognition infrastructure and labour market matching mechanisms — not passive anti-discrimination settings — and connects the inclusion agenda directly to the productivity agenda: closing the mismatch gap is both a fairness objective and a growth lever.

### NZ's inclusion geography and the diversity dividend
Auckland's harbour and motorway geography creates structural inclusion risk: south and west Auckland (where Māori and Pasifika communities are concentrated) have longer commutes to major employment centres, weaker PT coverage, and higher car dependence at lower rates of car ownership. These are not immutable — they reflect historical investment priorities that can be partially corrected through transit investment and upzoning in southern corridors. The emerging challenge is broader: as Auckland becomes majority non-Pākehā, the question shifts from "how do we reduce the disadvantage of minority communities" to "how do we build the inclusion infrastructure — credential recognition, language access, FDI networks — that converts demographic diversity into economic advantage at city scale."
