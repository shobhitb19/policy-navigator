# 02: Agglomeration and Productivity

## Summary

Agglomeration economies — the productivity and wage benefits from geographic concentration of economic activity — are among the best-documented findings in urban economics. Global meta-analysis finds that doubling city size raises wages or productivity by 2.7–6.4% (median 4.6%). They operate through three mechanisms: sharing (specialised suppliers, thick labour markets, shared infrastructure), matching (better firm-worker allocation in deeper labour markets), and learning (knowledge spillovers accelerated by proximity and face-to-face contact). Density raises wages, patent rates, energy efficiency, and the fiscal efficiency of public services simultaneously.

But the range of measured elasticities — 2.7–6.4% from a single meta-analysis — signals that city size is not the whole story. Estimates fall substantially when worker sorting is controlled for: 91% of Auckland's apparent density-productivity elasticity is compositional (high-productivity workers selecting into dense areas), not a genuine agglomeration effect on firm productivity. Capital intensity, infrastructure quality, and land use constraints all moderate how much of any given agglomeration potential actually converts to output. NZ quality-adjusted agglomeration elasticities (0.022 MFP at firm FE, Maré 2016) sit below the global meta-analytic range — suggesting that complementary factors are limiting the conversion of proximity into productivity.

Capital intensity and technology diffusion are the most important of those complementary factors. NZ capital stock per worker sits at roughly 50% of OECD comparators — a structural deficit that limits what any given agglomeration level can generate. On diffusion, 95% of NZ firms adopt or adapt existing technology rather than creating new knowledge, and the share that are "fully up to date" has declined from 51% (2007) to 44% (2022). NZ sits below the OECD median on all four main diffusion channels: trade openness, FDI stock, clustering quality, and capital/finance access. These are not alternatives to agglomeration policy — they are prerequisites for agglomeration benefits to materialise at the firm level.

A productive city policy must therefore address urban form, capital investment, and diffusion simultaneously. Treating them as separate silos — urban economics here, innovation policy there, investment settings elsewhere — misses the complementarity: capital investment embeds new technology vintages (the "why not both" argument of Winter et al. 2025), and geographic clustering is itself one of the four main diffusion channels.

## Key themes

### Interpreting elasticity estimates
The 2.7–6.4% global range reflects genuine heterogeneity, not just noise. Elasticities are lower at metropolitan than national scope, lower for manufacturing than services, and have declined since ~1980 (by approximately 1.5 percentage points across the economy). Adding rigorous controls for worker sorting, capital intensity, and housing reduces estimates further. The right interpretation is: agglomeration economies are real and consistent, but their magnitude is conditional on firm type, sector, urban form, and the quality of complementary infrastructure and capital. A city policy that simply targets "more agglomeration" without addressing complementary factors will underperform.

### The NZ productivity puzzle
Auckland is the national test case. Raw labour productivity is 17.9% above other NZ urban areas, but industry-composition-adjusted it is 13.5%, and quality-adjusted MFP is only 2.2% above other urban areas. The State of the City 2025 benchmarking places Auckland at the 99th percentile globally on the Resonance productivity ranking — bottom of the distribution. Peer capitals (Dublin, Helsinki, Tel Aviv) achieve 30–40% GDP premiums over their national averages; Auckland's is ~15%. Its Prosperity decile fell from 6th (2023) to 4th (2025) — the steepest decline of any pillar across all State of the City editions. The gap between Auckland's scale dominance (37.9% of NZ GDP) and its relative productivity performance is the central puzzle in NZ urban economics.

### The 2026 update: the gap reaches into the core
The State of the City 2026 report is not a full benchmarking edition — it re-frames three years of evidence around five focus areas for the Auckland Action Agenda and does not restate the ten-pillar decile scores. What it adds to the productivity puzzle is a sharper spatial location for it. Auckland's city centre generates about 8% of New Zealand's GDP and holds 5.3% of all national employment in under five square kilometres, a national role comparable to Helsinki's in Finland and Dublin's in Ireland. Yet its output per worker (US$121k) sits in the bottom third of comparable international city centres, and its peak job density (37,000 workers/km²) sits just below the peer average. The under-performance is therefore not confined to Auckland's dispersed suburbs: it is present in precisely the square kilometres where agglomeration economies should be strongest and most measurable. On the input side the picture is the mirror image — Auckland concentrates corporate headquarters at 2.12×, air arrivals at 2.1× and international students at 1.61× its population share, and its gateway flows in aggregate sit within 5% of the comparable-city average. Concentration is broadly normal; conversion is not. The two thinnest conversion channels the report identifies are both about depth rather than scale: a highly-skilled population premium over the rest of New Zealand of only 8 percentage points (behind Dublin, Lisbon, Stockholm and Oslo), and an innovation ecosystem that has risen fast (up 11 places to 75th globally, enterprise value up nineteenfold since 2019) but retains too few firms as they scale, with an alumni founder base less than half Lisbon's, Helsinki's or Vancouver's.

### Capital and diffusion as co-factors
The standard agglomeration model holds city size constant and asks what productivity premium it generates. But if capital intensity is below equilibrium, agglomeration effects are suppressed regardless of city size — because capital investment is itself a vehicle for productivity gains and for technology diffusion. A firm in a dense cluster with low capital intensity and outdated technology does not perform like the textbook agglomeration model predicts. NZ's structural capital deficit (~50% of OECD comparators), combined with declining technology currency (44% fully up to date in 2022 vs 51% in 2007) and FDI stock 80%+ below OECD peer average, creates a multi-dimensional drag that spatial policy alone cannot solve.

Skills mismatch adds a third layer of drag. Across the OECD, approximately 22% of workers are overqualified for their jobs and 40% are mismatched by field of study; overqualification carries a wage penalty of roughly 14% relative to well-matched peers with equivalent qualifications and proficiency (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Ch. 5). At country level, the degree to which workers actually use literacy and numeracy skills at work correlates strongly with GDP per hour even after adjusting for raw proficiency scores — suggesting that the binding constraint in some economies is skill deployment, not skill possession (OECD, 2016, Skills Matter, Ch. 4). NZ sits among the OECD leaders on combined mismatch (qualification plus field of study), driven partly by one of the highest foreign-born shares in the OECD (>25%), where credential non-recognition systematically displaces skilled migrants into roles below their qualification level. A capital investment and diffusion agenda that does not address qualification recognition and skills deployment is likely to underperform, because the required human capital is present in the labour market but not connected to the roles that would realise its productivity potential.

### Optimal city size and the undersizing risk
The international evidence suggests that the costs of being undersized (too little agglomeration) exceed the costs of being oversized (too much concentration). China's hukou restrictions kept cities below productivity-maximising size, causing large income losses. In NZ's context, the risk is not that Auckland is too large — it may be too constrained, too dispersed in form, and too under-capitalised to realise the productivity premium its scale should generate. The policy implication is to reduce the constraints (land use, infrastructure quality, capital access, FDI) that prevent Auckland from operating at its potential, rather than to seek redistribution to secondary cities as a substitute for Auckland performance.

### Geography of innovation: why proximity to knowledge is durable
The learning channel of agglomeration — knowledge spillovers from proximity — is especially well-evidenced and has a specific geographic structure. Jaffe, Trajtenberg & Henderson (1993) showed using patent citations that within the United States, a patent is approximately 5–10 times more likely to cite another patent from the same metropolitan area than the general distribution of patenting would predict. National borders amplify this further: citations are substantially more likely to remain within the country of the cited patent. Crucially, Li (2014) extended this analysis across 39 countries and 319 US MSAs over 1975–2002 and found that the degree of localisation has not declined — the hypothesis that globalisation and digital communication have dissolved geographic knowledge boundaries ("dying over time") is rejected by the data.

The mechanism, as synthesised by Audretsch & Feldman (2004), is that the most economically valuable knowledge is tacit — embodied in people and practices rather than codifiable in documents. Tacit knowledge transfers through face-to-face interaction, which requires proximity. This is consistent with the Ahlfeldt & Pietrostefani (2019) finding that doubling urban density is associated with a 21% increase in patent intensity. The Thomas Allen (1977) finding — that communication frequency between engineers drops exponentially beyond 30 metres — provides a direct empirical observation of this proximity constraint at the workplace scale.

For New Zealand, this literature has a specific implication: geographic isolation is not a passive disadvantage but an active barrier to knowledge spillovers. Badinger & Egger (2016) find that geographic distance between OECD countries significantly reduces cross-country productivity spillovers, with distance-weighted foreign R&D outperforming trade-weighted specifications. A country at the periphery of the global innovation system captures fewer foreign R&D spillovers than geographically central economies — even controlling for trade intensity. Combined with a below-OECD-average domestic R&D base, NZ faces compounded disadvantage: small own-knowledge stock and limited foreign spillover capture from the global knowledge frontier. Urban agglomeration within NZ is therefore not only a domestic productivity tool but the primary mechanism through which NZ can partially compensate for geographic isolation — by ensuring that the knowledge-intensive activity that does exist within NZ is as concentrated, well-connected, and innovative as possible.

Donovan et al. (2022) show that agglomeration elasticities in New Zealand are real but sit at the lower end of the international range (~2–4%, sorting-adjusted), and that Auckland's agglomeration effects have been strengthening while regional cities' have weakened — a "forking paths" pattern consistent with structural shift toward knowledge-intensive services. This is not a case for abandoning regional cities, but for recognising that the policy question is not whether to agglomerate, but whether existing agglomeration potential is being realised, constrained by form, frictions, or capital intensity.
