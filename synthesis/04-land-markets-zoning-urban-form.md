# 04: Land Markets, Zoning, and Urban Form

## Summary

Land markets and zoning rules shape who can live and work where — and at what cost. These are not local amenity questions. Restrictive zoning in high-productivity cities prevents workers from relocating to where their labour is most valuable, generating economy-wide misallocation losses. NZ modelling (Nunns 2019) estimates that restrictive Auckland land use reduced national GDP 0.9–1.8% by preventing labour reallocation. Removing all NZ housing supply constraints would, in simulation, raise output per worker 1.1–1.6%, expand the labour force 3.3–7.2%, and lift GDP 5.0–8.4% (Nunns 2021). These are large numbers — comparable to major structural reforms across trade, competition, and tax policy.

Restrictive land regulation generates costs through several channels. Housing price inflation reduces real wages for workers in productive cities. High floor-space prices — 2–8 times construction costs in central London and Manhattan — signal that zoning is suppressing supply far below equilibrium. Workers who cannot afford housing in high-productivity cities either commute from further away (raising congestion costs and reducing effective labour market access) or remain in lower-productivity locations. Firms that cannot expand in dense clusters lose agglomeration benefits. Mumbai's FSI restriction of 1.33 produced an average of 4 sq m per person and pushed 54% of residents into informal settlements — an extreme case of how restrictive formal regulation generates informal outcomes.

The opposite failure — unplanned sprawl — is equally costly, though less prominent in NZ's current policy debate. Dispersed development raises per-capita infrastructure costs, increases transport emissions, and lengthens commutes. Leapfrog development — where new growth bypasses connected zones — fragments urban form and destroys interaction density. Urban containment without adequate infill supply simply converts the sprawl problem into a restriction problem; the solution requires simultaneously enabling density and sequencing infrastructure investment to guide growth into connected corridors.

NZ's reform trajectory is well advanced. The NPS-UD and subsequent legislative reforms have enabled significant upzoning in Auckland: new homes completed per capita now exceed the peer city average by 60% (Committee for Auckland, 2025). But housing stock remains valued at roughly 4× GDP (versus ~2× in the 1990s), and the infrastructure funding dimension of supply reform — who pays for the pipes, roads, and schools that growth requires — remains only partially resolved.

## Key themes

### The misallocation channel
The clearest mechanism linking land use to aggregate productivity is spatial misallocation: workers and firms failing to locate in the places where their productivity would be highest because those places are too expensive or too constrained to accommodate them. This is distinct from the conventional housing affordability argument, which focuses on consumption costs. The misallocation argument is about production: economy-wide output is reduced when workers cannot sort to high-productivity locations. High land prices do not themselves reduce GDP in national accounts (land values are a transfer), but the underlying constraint that generates high prices does suppress output — through wages foregone, commutes extended, and firms denied their optimal cluster.

### Sprawl as a coordination failure
Sprawl arises when individual development decisions — each rational from the developer's perspective — combine to produce collectively suboptimal urban form. No single developer internalises the cross-boundary effects of extending the urban fringe: higher infrastructure costs per household, longer average commutes, lower interaction density for all existing residents. Metropolitan governance with strategic land use powers is the standard correction, aligning development decisions with the full cost they impose. The key empirical finding: metros with governance bodies had declining sprawl 2000–2006; those without had increasing sprawl. Leapfrog development generates cities with ~40% fewer potential face-to-face interactions than connected equivalents of the same population. Australian modelling (Infrastructure Victoria) puts a concrete number on the servicing-cost differential: the infrastructure required to service a home in a more dispersed city costs roughly NZ$68,000 more than in a compact one — a finding Te Waihanga cites in support of upzoning around existing transport corridors rather than continued fringe expansion.

### Urban form is durable
Buildings last 100–150+ years; street networks and utility infrastructure persist even longer. The pattern established in a city's early growth phases is remarkably sticky — Dar es Salaam's 1970s planned road network defines the city's highest-value corridors 50 years later, while retrospectively upgraded informal areas have lower values despite equivalent physical investment. Decisions made now about how to accommodate growth — connected infill or dispersed greenfield — will shape city form and infrastructure costs for generations. This durability makes forward investment in growth corridors far cheaper than retrofitting poorly sequenced development later.

### The volume problem and the location problem are not the same problem
The 2026 State of the City evidence sharpens what Auckland's supply reform has and has not achieved. On volume the record is now strong and durable: through a weakening development cycle Auckland recorded one of the ten most positive housing-delivery trendlines of any major city, measured affordability is improving ahead of many close peers, and the medium-density share of new consents has shifted faster than in Vancouver or Brisbane. That is the harder test — holding delivery gains through a downturn — and Auckland passed it.

On location the record is poor, and the two findings are simultaneous rather than sequential. New housing has gone to the city centre and to the urban edge, with comparatively little in the suburbs that public transport already serves; meanwhile businesses are forming fastest in the inner suburbs. The report's summary is that "the distance between where Aucklanders live and where jobs are being created is rising." Auckland sits in the bottom decile globally for residential density, street connectivity and access to mixed-use locations, and shows one of the steepest walkability gaps between central and outer suburbs. The consequence is that supply reform and agglomeration policy have not compounded: the units were built, but not where the labour-market and accessibility benefits would have been largest.

The report also supplies the counter-argument to the standard political objection. Beyond Auckland's core, neighbourhoods that added gentle density gained businesses, young people and a mix of uses substantially faster than those that did not — a pattern the report says recurs internationally. Intensification, on this evidence, made those neighbourhoods more economically active rather than less. The finding is observational and vulnerable to selection (areas already gaining activity may be the ones that intensified), but it is the right evidence to put against the claim that density degrades neighbourhood life.

### NZ's reform experience and its limits
Auckland's supply reform is measurable and internationally significant: new homes per capita now exceed the peer city average by 60%. But two unresolved dimensions constrain its full effect. First, infrastructure funding: development contributions, infrastructure bonds, and value capture mechanisms each have partial coverage; no single mechanism provides the financial certainty needed for large-scale enabling infrastructure investment ahead of growth. Second, distributional adjustment: as zoning liberalisation raises construction activity and eventually moderates land prices, the short-run winners (developers, construction workers) and losers (incumbent landowners, some renters during transition) are not the same households. Managing that transition politically is part of sustaining reform over multiple electoral cycles.
