# 03: Governance and Institutions

## Summary

How cities are governed determines whether agglomeration potential is realised or wasted. Metropolitan fragmentation — many separate municipalities governing one functional economic area — is consistently associated with lower productivity, more sprawl, and worse transit. Each municipality optimises independently, leading to undersupply of cross-boundary infrastructure, inconsistent land use planning, and development that follows existing settlement patterns rather than directing growth efficiently. Doubling the number of municipalities per 100,000 inhabitants in a metro reduces labour productivity by 5–6% (OECD cross-city evidence). The absence of any metropolitan governance body is associated with approximately a 6% productivity penalty; a dedicated governance body roughly halves this.

A second, equally important governance failure is temporal. Most consequential urban investments — infrastructure, transport networks, spatial plans — have costs that arrive today and benefits that arrive over decades. Three-year election cycles and fragmented ministerial portfolios create systematic pressure to favour visible short-cycle spending over long-cycle investments. This is not a problem of political bad faith: it is a structural feature of democratic governance that requires institutional design responses — independent infrastructure bodies, long-term spatial strategies, or population commissions with statutory mandates that outlast any single government.

New Zealand's governance context illustrates both failures. Auckland's 2010 amalgamation created a unified metropolitan authority with strategic land use and transport powers — a significant improvement. But the central-local tension remains live: central government sets the national spatial framework (NPS-UD, National Land Transport Programme) while Auckland Council holds statutory plan-making powers, producing coordination failures on infrastructure sequencing and housing delivery. Looking ahead, NZ's own infrastructure agency now projects spending needing to rise from just over $20bn/year to more than $40bn/year by the 2050s, on top of an existing 5.8% of GDP/year average over the past 20 years — a scale that cannot be sustained within three-year political cycles without durable institutional mechanisms. The recurrence of near-identical infrastructure recommendations across NZ's 2010, 2011, 2015, and 2026 plans is itself evidence of the temporal governance failure this synthesis describes: diagnosis has not been the constraint, durable institutional follow-through has.

The approaching fiscal and demographic pressure compounds the governance challenge. The dependency ratio is projected to rise from 54% (2025) to 73% (2073), with NZS costs rising from 5.1% to 8% of GDP by 2065. A fiscal trajectory of this severity requires governance that can make credible commitments across multiple electoral cycles — whether through a population strategy commission, a long-term spatial investment fund, or cross-party compacts. The institutional vehicle matters less than the credibility of the commitment it makes.

## Key themes

### The fragmentation penalty — quantified
The OECD cross-city evidence on governance fragmentation is unusually clear for social science: doubling municipalities in a metro is associated with 3.2–6% lower labour productivity, and the effect is partially but not fully explained by reduced public transport satisfaction. The mechanism is coordination failure: without a body that can internalise cross-boundary externalities, no single municipality has an incentive to supply the right quantity and location of development. Transit systems fragment at administrative boundaries. Infrastructure investment follows existing settlement patterns rather than directing future growth. Land use plans across adjacent councils produce contradictory signals for developers. A metropolitan governance body cannot solve all these problems, but its absence reliably makes them worse.

### Temporal governance failures
Long-duration investments are systematically under-supplied by short-cycle democratic institutions. Infrastructure with 40-year payoffs cannot be reliably funded by governments accountable every three years — unless institutional design explicitly bridges the gap. The relevant mechanisms are: independent infrastructure bodies with multi-year mandates (NZ Infrastructure Commission provides some of this); long-term spatial strategies with statutory status that survive government changes; revenue streams (hypothecated levies, value capture) insulated from annual appropriations processes. NZ's current institutional landscape has components of each but no single vehicle that provides credibility for a genuinely cross-cycle investment commitment. The clearest evidence of this failure mode is its recurrence: New Zealand has produced national infrastructure plans in 2010, 2011, 2015, and 2026, with some 2026 recommendations identical to those from over a decade earlier (claim-0129) — the problems have been correctly diagnosed repeatedly without being durably acted on. The Government's 2026 response illustrates both the promise and the limits of partial fixes: Treasury has committed to using the Infrastructure Commission's Forward Guidance as a regular input to medium-term fiscal advice (claim-0131), but a recommendation for statutory multi-year budgeting was only "Supported in-principle," with the Government arguing the existing annual-appropriations system already has adequate multi-year features (claim-0133) — a response that keeps planning horizons inside the existing electoral-cycle-constrained framework rather than insulating them from it.

### What effective metropolitan governance looks like
Evidence suggests the key design features are: authority over the functional economic area (not just the core municipality), strategic land use powers that can override local plan provisions, coordination with transport and infrastructure agencies within a unified spatial strategy, and some degree of own-source revenue (development levies, land value capture, dedicated transport rates) to reduce dependence on central transfers. Auckland's supercity has most of these in design, though implementation has been constrained by the central-local tension around which spatial decisions belong to which tier of government.

### Governance as a precondition for other levers
Several other policy levers only work if governance capacity exists to implement them. Integrated spatial planning requires a body that can direct infrastructure across sector agencies. Housing supply reform requires plan-making authority and consenting capacity. Transport investment requires a unified transport authority that can operate across modes and municipal boundaries. The governance lever is therefore not one among equals — it is partly constitutive of whether other levers can be deployed effectively.
